## Overview

*The Trusted Advisor* argues that professional success depends on more than technical mastery. David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford, who between them have decades of consulting and advising experience, say that clients share their real problems and act on advice only when they trust the advisor, and that trust can be earned deliberately. The book is written mainly for lawyers, accountants, consultants and other advisers, though it applies to anyone who serves internal or external clients.

The numbering used here follows the book. The introduction and "How to Use This Book" are summarized first, unnumbered. Chapters 1 to 22 follow in order, grouped into the three parts the authors describe as an hourglass. Part One (Chapters 1 to 7) is broad and anecdotal and covers the benefits of trust, what a trusted advisor is, and the three core skills of earning trust, giving advice and building relationships, plus the mindsets and the question of sincerity. Part Two (Chapters 8 to 14) is the tight center: the trust equation and a five-stage process of engaging, listening, framing, envisioning and committing. Part Three (Chapters 15 to 22) applies the ideas to obstacles, client types, selling, current assignments, relationships between assignments and cross-selling, and ends with a quick-impact list. An appendix collects all the lists.

Three ideas hold the book together.

1. **Trust is a formula you can work on.** Trustworthiness grows with credibility, reliability and intimacy and shrinks with self-orientation, and the last two, not expertise, are where most advisors fall short.
2. **Earn the right before you advise.** Trust builds through five stages, engage, listen, frame, envision and commit, and the commonest error is rushing to answers before the client feels understood.
3. **Focus on the client, not yourself.** Selling and serving merge when you put the client's interests first, take small personal risks, tell the truth and keep your promises.

## Introduction and How to Use This Book

The authors open by admitting that they have made every mistake the book describes. Between them they have roughly fifty years as consultants and as advisors to other professionals, and what they learned, none of it taught in school, is that good advice is not enough. You only get to use your advisory skills once someone trusts you enough to tell you their real problems. David Maister had been writing about professionalism and client relationships, while Rob Galford and Charlie Green had been running "Trusted Advisor" workshops for leading professional firms. After meeting at a conference they realized they each held part of the same puzzle.

Their thesis is that professional success depends on technical mastery plus the ability to earn clients' trust and confidence. The book is aimed at advisors in consulting, accounting, law, engineering, public relations, executive search and similar fields, although it also speaks to people serving internal clients. It focuses on the most basic form of trust, the one between two individuals. The authors argue that trust has become scarce. Clients scrutinize bills and staffing, shut professionals out of early conversations, force long-term suppliers into beauty contests and demand detailed reporting. The old world of automatic trust based on an honorable calling is gone, and what replaces it is the need to earn trust throughout a career.

Many of the questions professionals ask, such as how to get more access, win introductions, cross-sell, avoid being typecast or make clients less price-focused, share one answer: each requires the client to do something for you, and clients do that for people they trust. The authors claim trust can be managed and improved without turning relationships into a mechanical process, and they set out to treat it as a process with a beginning, derailments and an evolution over time.

On how to use the book, they describe it as an hourglass. Parts One and Three are broad, with anecdotes and practical suggestions, while Part Two is tighter and more formal and builds the structure of trust. They like lists, which they invite readers to modify, and they repeat every list in an appendix that can be skimmed first, used to jump to a topic, or kept as a reference afterward.

> **Key takeaway.** Technical skill gets you into the room, but clients share their real problems only with advisors they trust, and trust can be earned deliberately.

## Part One: Perspectives on Trust

### Chapter 1: A Sneak Preview

The first chapter opens with a question: what would you gain if your clients trusted you more? The authors answer with a sixteen-item list. More trusting clients reach for your advice sooner, act on your recommendations, bring you into more complex and strategic issues, respect you, share information that improves your work and pay your bills without quibbling. They also refer you to others, forgive your mistakes, warn you of dangers, protect you inside their own organizations, involve you early when an issue is forming and trust your instincts, including about people. Stress drops on both sides.

Next they ask whether you have a trusted advisor of your own, and what such people have in common. Their list of twenty-two traits mixes the emotional with the intellectual. Trusted advisors seem to understand and like us without effort, are consistent, offer fresh perspectives, do not force things on us, help us think rather than substituting their judgment, stay calm and help us separate logic from emotion. They correct gently but do not pull punches, play the long game, share reasoning and not only conclusions, give options plus a recommendation and let us choose, challenge our assumptions, act like a real person and not a role, remain reliably on our side, remember what we said, stay honorable and discreet, use stories and metaphors to put issues in context, use humor to defuse tension, and are smart in ways we are not.

The authors use the Golden Rule to argue that your clients' list is probably close to yours, so if you want to be treated as a trusted advisor, you must pass as many of those tests as possible. The key discipline is to ask which of these traits your clients believe you have, not which you think you have. They add that the book is not only about the far end of the journey. The early benefits of earning trust arrive quickly, and the ability to earn trust is a learnable skill with a visible path.

> **Key takeaway.** Trust brings concrete rewards, from access to forgiveness, and the traits that earn it are the ones you would want in your own advisor, judged as clients see you, not as you see yourself.

### Chapter 2: What Is a Trusted Advisor?

This chapter describes how an advisor's standing evolves. Most professionals start as vendors performing a specific task with technical skill. At the next level, clients notice capabilities beyond the original specialty and call on you for broader problems earlier on. At the third level you are a valuable resource who puts issues in context and offers advice proactively. At the top, the trusted advisor, almost any issue, professional or personal, is open for discussion, and the client turns to you first, often in a crisis, change, triumph or defeat. These levels are a function of two dimensions, the breadth of issues you are asked about and the depth of the personal relationship the client lets you address. The authors stress that most daily work happens at the lower levels and that this is fine. The skill is being able to shift to any level when required.

![Four levels of a client-advisor relationship plotted by breadth of issues and depth of personal relationship](/figures/the-trusted-advisor/relationship-levels.svg "Redrawn: the relationship climbs from vendor to trusted advisor as both breadth of issues and depth of personal relationship grow.")

They also expand the usual marketing typology of product-based, needs-based and relationship-based selling by adding a fourth type, the trust-based relationship, marked by broad business issues and a deep personal relationship, and distinguished by its human dimension. Pinnacle relationships illustrate the extreme: agent David Falk with Michael Jordan, the sounding-board partnerships of Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, and a law-firm chairman whose client, learning he was terminally ill, asked her to guide his wife through everything that would follow. The advisor can act as mirror, sounding board, confessor, mentor and sometimes jester.

The authors list ten attributes of trusted advisors. They focus on the client as an individual and not as a role, with enough self-confidence to listen without prejudging and enough ego strength to subordinate their own. They value problem definition over technical mastery, compete to serve the client better, do the next right thing and not chase an outcome, follow an internal drive to do right, treat methods as means, seek out client contact and take personal risks, see selling and serving as the same dedication, and know that business and private life are both very human. Trusted advisors also care more about preserving the relationship than winning the current transaction, and they often invest before any income arrives.

The benefits include repeat business and referrals, fewer wasteful proposals (a producer who is trusted sells a show with a two-page idea, while his less trusted rival needs bound treatments), the chance to apply your best skills to matters that count, and the freedom to be yourself without posturing. The chapter closes with a study showing that students and faculty advisors each rated themselves more trustworthy than the other side and wrongly expected the other to agree, so start from the assumption that others trust you less than you think.

> **Key takeaway.** A trusted advisor sits at the top of a ladder defined by breadth of issues and depth of personal relationship, and gets there by putting the client and the relationship ahead of the current transaction.

### Chapter 3: Earning Trust

Hiring any professional means an act of faith. You can check credentials and past work, but in the end you hand your affairs to someone else and hope they will put your interests ahead of theirs. The authors argue that trust cannot be claimed by saying "trust me." It must be earned by giving evidence. They show this with David Maister's search for a probate lawyer. The first lawyers talked about their firm's size, history and fees, while the one he hired offered, before being retained, a free outline of the steps involved, what could wait and whom to notify, including things outside the legal work. A dentist, Andrew, earned trust through small repeated gestures such as phoning each evening after a procedure to ask about pain, until David and his wife stopped doubting his motives and accepted his recommendations.

Two stories show trust won or lost quickly. In "Charlie and the Sandpaper," a young consultant was asked about experience in marketing studies for industrial consumables, and was about to claim vaguely similar work when his senior partner said, "None that I can think of," and asked what else would be useful to discuss. The honest answer showed the partner would put the truth ahead of winning, and the firm got the work. In a second story, litigator Peter Biagetti noticed his client's hesitation on the courthouse steps before suing his own mother, said aloud how hard that must be, and the matter was settled, which led to the client moving all his legal work to the firm. Timing matters too: a good advisor knows when to bother a client and when to leave them alone.

The authors then list six characteristics of trust. It grows rather than appearing, since we say "I don't trust him" fast but "I trust her" slowly. It is both rational and emotional, so technical competence is necessary but not sufficient. It is a two-way relationship that you cannot create alone or force. It involves perceived risk, because trust only matters when the other party could, but probably will not, act differently. It differs for the one trusting and the one trusted, like leading and following in a dance. And it is personal: we do not trust institutions or processes, we trust people, so "It's business; it is personal." Trusting you means believing you will do what you say, that you are in it for the long haul and that you care about my interests as much as your own.

> **Key takeaway.** Trust is earned by generosity, honesty that keeps self-interest in check, small consistent gestures and a willingness to take personal risks, and it can never be demanded or delivered by an institution.

### Chapter 4: How to Give Advice

The authors argue that advice giving is not an objective, logical exercise but an emotional duet between advisor and client. Maister learned this early when he told a firm's management bluntly what they were doing wrong and was fired for being disruptive, even though he was right. The lesson was that an advisor's job is to be helpful, not merely correct, and that you must earn the right to criticize. The difficulty is that the person who hired you is often part of the problem, so diagnosis can never be separated from the client's sensitivities, emotions and politics.

They then take the client's view. Choosing an outside provider carries personal risk to reputation and career, and professionals tend to reveal complications the client had not seen, which can feel like adding worries rather than relieving them. Clients are accomplished people who now depend on someone using jargon and unexplained activity, so they feel anxious and out of control. Most want reassurance, calm and confidence. The authors suggest treating the client like your mother or father, not like a child: when you want to persuade a parent, you find respectful words, soften any implied critique and focus on a conversation that helps them see the issue afresh, not on delivering a verdict.

Language matters. "You've got to do X" invites resistance and leaves nowhere to go if the client says no, while exploring options, pros and cons and asking whether X seems best keeps the conversation alive. Turning assertions into questions is a rule of thumb. Maister's teaching story makes the same point: asking "Did everyone understand that?" invites silence, while "Have I made myself clear?" makes it easier to admit confusion. Rehearsing client conversations with a colleague, ideally on video, shows how you actually come across.

Good advice resembles good teaching. You must understand point A, where the client starts, through questioning and listening, and then lead step by step toward point B through Socratic questions, so the client concludes for themselves that B is better. This takes patience. In the same spirit, an advisor should give options, educate on them, give a recommendation and let the client choose, saying "If this were my business, I would do X" when pressed. Client politics are unavoidable because decisions involve several people, so call each attendee before a meeting and summarize what was decided afterward. Finally, the approach must be customized to each client, since some want you to skip the questions and simply say what you think.

> **Key takeaway.** Being right is not enough: advise through questions, options and respect for the client's emotions and politics, so the client reaches and owns the conclusion.

### Chapter 5: The Rules of Romance: Relationship Building

The third core skill is building relationships, and the authors argue that the best model for client relationships is the way we build close relationships in our personal lives. Where a professional identity tends to separate us from clients, relationship building requires common ground. The chapter offers eleven principles drawn from romance and business alike.

Go first: to earn a relationship you must make the first investment and accept the risk of rejection. Maister's wife, wanting to join his trip to Egypt but unable to ask yet, cooked an Egyptian meal with music and guidebooks on the table, an irresistible way of saying that she was willing to work to deserve his goodwill. Illustrate, don't tell: claims about yourself are met with skepticism, so demonstrate. A firm delivered a proposal in saddlebags to a bank, but such gestures only work as part of a consistent pattern. Before every meeting decide the two or three things the client must believe about you and plan how to show them, for example through questions that prove you did your homework. Small, unprompted gestures count too, like calling a client just to share an idea.

Listen for what is different, not what is familiar, because the client wants to be treated as an individual and "what clients want is..." is always wrong. Be sure your advice is being sought, because a person describing a problem may want only a sympathetic ear. Earn the right to offer advice by understanding the situation, understanding how the client feels about it, and convincing the client that you understand both. Keep asking, because people hint and a phrase like "I'm not sure that will work" can mean many things, so follow up gently. Say what you mean, since hints fail, and being a trusted advisor is not sycophancy: you tell the bad news with tact.

When you need help, ask for it. Pretending to be omnipotent backfires, and requests for help invite joint problem solving and warm feelings, as Cialdini's research on favors suggests. Show an interest in the person, remember what they told you, and use notes if it helps. Use compliments, not flattery: be specific and sincere. Show appreciation, because clients rarely see the behind-the-scenes effort, yet they, like everyone, expect to be appreciated for the business they bring.

> **Key takeaway.** Build the relationship the way you would a personal one: go first, show don't tell, listen for what is unique, and ask, appreciate and tell the truth with care.

### Chapter 6: The Importance of Mindsets

Skills are not enough, the authors say; a trusted advisor also needs the right mindsets. They name five: the ability to focus on the other person, self-confidence, ego strength, curiosity and inclusive professionalism. The first is summed up in the idea that you will do better by helping others reach their goals than by pursuing your own. They note this is simply how an exchange economy works, since to get what you want from someone you must first give them what they want, and they quote Dale Carnegie that finding out what a person wants and showing them how to get it is the only way to influence them.

They illustrate with newspaper publisher Tim White, who ran a management offsite while hardly ever stating an opinion. He scanned the table for reactions and asked individuals about their feelings, and the team left feeling consulted and that the process was fair. He achieved high-content results through low-content leadership. What blocks us is the belief that technical mastery is enough, and the constant internal chatter in client conversations about how to solve the problem, how to appear expert or what to say next. At the root is usually fear of embarrassment, failure or looking ignorant, and the professions attract high achievers who conquered such fears through technical excellence. The career transition from technician, who works away from the client, to advisor, who works in the client's presence, demands a change of skills and attitudes, and the habits that earned past success can become obstacles. An Episcopal priest adds that empathetic listening means going to where the other person is, often a painful place, and moving from "I" to "we." Even Green found himself worrying about how he would come across to a new CEO, only to learn the CEO was asking whether Charlie liked the project and him.

Self-confidence means being secure enough to listen and wait before jumping to the answer, trusting there will still be time and talent to find it. Ego strength is different: it is the ability to focus on the process and not on credit or blame. Taking too much responsibility is also self-orientation, as a psychologist found when testing successful consultants, who would feel responsible if it rained on the company picnic. A client praised his advisor for staying calm and steering the discussion while the client himself could barely contain his anger. Curiosity is the habit of focusing on what you don't know, and the right answer evolves through inquiry. Inclusive professionalism rejects the idea that professionals are set apart from their clients, and holds that the essence of professionalism is aligning with clients to improve their situation, with joint responsibility, not control or secrecy about work product.

> **Key takeaway.** Five mindsets, above all attention on the other person, matter more than extra technique: they let you listen, wait and collaborate instead of defending yourself.

### Chapter 7: Sincerity or Technique?

Participants in the authors' programs often ask whether building trust is about tactics or about really caring, and whether using techniques without caring is manipulative. The authors answer with Jim, a businessman who teaches a course and photographs every student on day one, promising to know their names, faces, schools and employers by the next class. He studies flash cards all week, and his students are dazzled and never drop out. His technique is premeditated and visible, yet obviously sincere. The advice follows: if you already care, practice behaviors that show it; if you only go through the motions, you will be found out. But that does not mean you should avoid the techniques when you lack the feeling, because behavior and attitude influence each other, and acting caring often produces caring faster than trying to change your mental state. Clients can only observe sincerity through behavior such as attention, preparation and empathetic listening, so the best approach is to work from both ends toward the middle. Occasional tactics inconsistent with your usual manner will be spotted as phony. The consistent version of faking it becomes indistinguishable from the real thing, which is why one writer says the trick to earning trust is to avoid all tricks.

What if you simply do not like a client? The authors list four options and reject most. Serving the client without trying to build the relationship loses the benefits of trust and makes everyone lose. Faking it indefinitely is worth a try for a while but not a lifetime. Passing the client to a colleague is attractive because chemistry is personal. Resigning the account is the last resort, justified because reputation outlasts any lost revenue. If you are junior and cannot resign, check your motives, keep perspective on how long the situation lasts, and raise it with the engagement manager. Their survey finding that professionals like their clients only 20 to 30 percent of the time explains why this topic is avoided. In relationships there are only win-wins and lose-loses.

You need not make every client a friend. Being sociable and interested in the person is professional, but golf and dinners are not the point. Rob found his relationship weakened after his favorite sponsor left the company and his heart was no longer in it, showing that without real feeling tactics lose their power. Finally, clients can tell whether you are using them as a means to an end, so you must bet on the long-term benefit of the relationship and not bail out during rough spots.

> **Key takeaway.** Trust requires real care, and consistent caring behavior builds the feeling it expresses; occasional tricks fail, so work from both ends and move on from clients with whom you cannot connect.

## Part Two: The Structure of Trust Building

### Chapter 8: The Trust Equation

The authors now offer a framework, not a scientific law, for how the elements of trust interact. Trustworthiness has four components: credibility and reliability sit in the numerator, along with intimacy, and self-orientation sits in the denominator. Credibility concerns the trustworthiness of words, reliability concerns actions, intimacy concerns emotions and self-orientation concerns motives. Most professionals focus on credibility and reliability and wonder why clients still do not trust them, but a client may trust your expertise and distrust your motives, or admire your brilliance and dislike how you treat them. You generally need to do well on all four in the client's eyes. The authors illustrate with a famous surgeon whose skill was unmatched but who announced that "he" would be fine after operating on a three-year-old girl; he got away with it only because he was world famous.

![The trust equation: credibility plus reliability plus intimacy, divided by self-orientation](/figures/the-trusted-advisor/trust-equation.svg "Redrawn: the four components of trustworthiness and the realm each one covers (words, actions, emotions and motives).")

**Credibility.** It is the most commonly achieved component, because professionals concentrate on technical expertise. Credibility is content expertise plus presence, how we look, act and talk, and it has a rational side (accuracy, which makes you believable) and an emotional side (completeness, which makes you honest). Rational credibility is judged quickly with logic and references, while honesty takes longer. Most providers sell on competence while most buyers buy on emotion, so the way to raise credibility is to anticipate needs and voice what clients leave unsaid, for instance by offering a gentle insight beginning "if I were in your position, I might wonder about...." The tips include telling as much truth as possible, never exaggerating, avoiding claims that could be construed as lies (such as "we'll put our best people on it"), speaking with expression, introducing clients to one another instead of just citing references, saying "I don't know" quickly, doing current homework, resisting the urge to show off and loving your topic.

**Reliability.** It is whether you behave consistently and link words to deeds, and judgments depend on how many times the client has interacted with you. It has a rational side (on time and on spec, calls returned, meetings kept) and an emotional side, the repeated experience of expectations fulfilled in ways the client prefers. FedEx's reliability comes from details such as first-ring answers, consistent packaging and packages left where you want them. Consistency must match the client's preferences, not the provider's. Practical suggestions are to make and keep small commitments, send materials ahead, set goals for meetings, use the client's terminology and hours, review agendas with the client and reconfirm schedules.

**Intimacy.** It is the most common source of failure and also the least time-dependent. It means emotional closeness on the issues at hand, not sharing private lives, and it builds through mutual increases in risk, where one person offers something and the other responds or declines. Because it is scary, advisors avoid it, but business is intensely personal. The suggestions: be unafraid, offer candor, find the fun, test whether you are too close to the line by asking if you would want to discuss this and whether the client has a face-saving way out, practice your phrasing, do not overrate the downside and make the first move yourself. Rob helped a sidelined executive simply by letting him vent.

**Self-orientation.** It is the greatest source of distrust, and it covers far more than greed: self-consciousness, the need to look smart or be right, jumping to solutions, a long to-do list and fears of various kinds. Clients see it when we relate their stories to ours, finish their sentences, fill silences, name-drop or recite credentials. Low self-orientation looks like letting the client fill silences, open-ended questions, defining the problem before guessing the solution, reflective listening, admitting ignorance, acknowledging feelings and listening without distractions. The authors advise treating clients as friends and honestly assessing your interest in the work.

The chapter ends with economics. Scoring a new client at credibility 5, reliability 3, intimacy 2 and self-orientation 8 gives (5+3+2)/8, or 1.25, while an established client at 7, 8, 5 and 4 gives 20/4, or 5. That fourfold gap resembles the finding that winning new business costs four to seven times as much as expanding existing relationships, so a trust-based strategy is a profitable one.

![Trust equation worked example: new client 1.25 versus existing client 5](/figures/the-trusted-advisor/trust-economics.svg "Redrawn: the book's worked example, scoring a new and an established client on the four components (the numbers are illustrative guesses).")

> **Key takeaway.** Trustworthiness equals credibility, reliability and intimacy divided by self-orientation, so lowering your focus on yourself and building emotional closeness raises trust faster than more expertise alone.

### Chapter 9: The Development of Trust

This chapter introduces the second half of the structure: a five-stage model of how trust develops, which the following chapters take in turn. The stages are engage, listen, frame, envision and commit, and at each one the client feels something different and the advisor gains something different when the stage succeeds.

![The five stages of trust building: engage, listen, frame, envision, commit](/figures/the-trusted-advisor/five-stages.svg "Redrawn: the five-stage trust process, with the language and skill each stage calls for.")

Engagement is where the client starts to feel two things: there is an issue worth talking about, and this person is worth talking to about it. Both are needed, and it matters for existing clients too, since a long-standing client may accept a new issue yet not see you as the person to discuss it with. Listening succeeds when the client believes the advisor understands. It must be active and involved, attend to the unspoken, and include confirming and validating what you heard, so that the client has the experience of being listened to. Framing is the process of helping the client crystallize and clarify the issues through problem statements, hypotheses and points of view built around what matters to the client. It is often the moment when the client first consciously sees value added, and it may be political or emotional as well as logical.

Envisioning comes before solving. A problem can have many solutions depending on the future state the client wants, so advisor and client jointly picture in rich detail what the end result looks like, asking what you are really aiming for, what it will look like and how you will know you are there. The advisor may stretch the client's ambition or temper it with knowledge of what is achievable, and sometimes the client chooses to live with the problem. Commitment makes sure the client understands, in rational, emotional and political terms, what it will take to achieve the vision and finds the determination to do it. Trust grows from the candor with which the advisor lays out challenges and risks.

The authors give each stage its own language: interest and concern when engaging ("I've been thinking about your competitors"), understanding and empathy when listening ("Tell me more"), perspective and candor when framing ("I see three themes"), possibility when envisioning ("Wouldn't it be great if...") and joint exploration when committing ("What would it take, for each of us, to..."). The skills differ too: being noticed credibly, understanding another person, creative insight with emotional courage, collaboration and creativity, and generating enthusiasm while sometimes damping overenthusiasm. People favor the stage that suits their strengths, such as a strategy firm that thought framing mattered most, but no one step is the key.

> **Key takeaway.** Trust builds through five stages, engage, listen, frame, envision and commit, each needing its own language and skill, and none of them can be skipped.

### Chapter 10: Engagement

Engagement is the moment a client first thinks that the person in front of them might help with a specific problem. Many advisors assume trust building starts with listening, but something must come first: an initial connection that makes the client willing to talk. For the client this is a real decision, because raising an issue costs emotional energy and personal risk. A chief financial officer told a group of accounting partners that he is besieged by firms who just ask about his problems and wish to listen but bring nothing of value. Clients do not open up simply because we listen. They must believe we are worth talking to on this particular issue, and shock or surprise can break habitual perceptions.

With new clients, visible customization shows that you are willing to enter their world, as with the saddlebag and pizza-box proposals, or by using the client's own web format, products, video or live performance. The effect is the thought "these people are really trying to earn my attention," which is the start of trust. One consulting firm picked target clients carefully, did a genuine industry analysis with a point of view and approached them offering to share the insights for free. Another source suggests showing you understand what the buyer wants, not just needs, even before delivering anything. This works because it is rare for an advisor to leave their own worldview, since we usually worry about our lines, our slides and our appearance, all self-oriented.

Reengaging existing clients is harder because clients grant an audience but not necessarily an open conversation. The relationship that began with novelty needs new and interesting contributions to stay open. The authors suggest approaches that raise competitive developments, acknowledge the career challenges facing the individual, offer a solution to a managerial issue mentioned earlier, or show continuity ("I've thought a lot about something you said four weeks ago"). These emotional and personal topics are appropriate only after the right has been earned. Timing matters, too, and Covey's distinction between urgency and importance helps. With little time, state the agenda, start with the urgent and end with the important; with more time, start with the important and save five minutes for the urgent. Don't hold back from raising a topic, because even a hint of care pays off later.

To engage a new client, research the entity and the individual, including second-stage research through people who know them. Prepare two or three topics (not questions), ideally an unpublished point of view on competitors, and consider what you might have or be that interests them. Mention past work only when relevant, so you don't sound like a name-dropper. Don't make early interactions purely transactional, or you will be pegged as a technician. Talk to them as a new friend, not an old one, and if you cannot add value, postpone the meeting.

> **Key takeaway.** Before clients will talk, they must see something of value in you; earn that with research, customization and a point of view offered first.

### Chapter 11: The Art of Listening

The authors begin with Jack Welch's praise for a lawyer who "listens better than anybody else," and claim that every effective trusted advisor they have met is an excellent listener. Listening is necessary though not sufficient, and its purpose is to earn the right to comment on the client's issues. Cutting to the chase without that right reads as arrogance. Jim Copeland, then at Deloitte, describes meeting an angry client CEO after a merger and simply saying "tell me more," showing he was there for him. That one skill earned the chance to deliver content and later cross-sell, and years on the CEO asked him directly whether a $5 million project was the right thing to do, trusting an honest answer.

Why does being heard matter so much? Because it signals respect and protects the client's self-esteem, and an advisor might say, "What I like about your idea is X; now help me see how we get to Y." Two mistakes are common. The first is listening only to the rational, although earning the right is emotional too, and clients who demand "results, not soft talk" still want someone who cares. The second is listening too passively. Conversation needs regular acknowledgment, and when a message carries feeling, a bare "mm-hmm" tells the client that only the facts matter. If a CEO complains that a former employee is selling trade secrets, saying something like "you must be outraged" is essential.

The authors add that attention in business conversation wanders within thirty to sixty seconds, so listening takes discipline. They urge respecting the sequence of the speaker's story, because interrupting, rearranging or imposing your own structure destroys the intended meaning. They borrow the Ariel Group's progression from reflective listening ("what I hear you saying is...") to supportive listening ("that must be tough") to listening for possibility ("what have you thought about doing?"). Avoid questions like "what are the top three issues?" because you will get three even if only two matter, and an old courtroom joke about a pathologist with a brain in a jar shows the danger of leading questions. Listening matters even more in selling than in delivery, since a rigid presentation agenda means you impose rather than listen.

Agenda setting is a simple, risk-free listening tool: ask what else should be discussed, what the meeting needs to accomplish, and invite a brief discussion of the agenda so it is shared and not owned by one side. The chapter ends with checklists. Good listeners probe, hear unvoiced emotions and the story, summarize, empathize, spot hidden assumptions, let the client get it out of their system, keep them talking, remove distractions and make the client feel they are all that matters. Great listeners do not interrupt, respond too soon, match the client's story with their own, editorialize, jump to conclusions, ask pointless closed questions, offer their ideas first, judge, solve too fast or take calls.

> **Key takeaway.** Listen actively, emotionally and in the speaker's own sequence, and make the agenda a shared conversation; this earns the right to advise.

### Chapter 12: Framing the Issue

Framing is the third stage: crystallizing the client's complex issues and emotions into a problem definition that gives insight and a fresh way of thinking. In many situations an accurate problem statement is more than halfway to the solution. It is also the hardest step because it combines the rational and emotional and often has to be formed mid-conversation. The authors distinguish rational framing from emotional framing.

Rational framing is the comfortable territory for professionals: distilling a complex mix of issues into a few critical variables through a list, diagram or model. Strategy consultants believe clients buy their brilliance, and lawyers frame issues by categories of law, like the famous question of what a president knew and when. Models help because people can only juggle a limited number of perspectives at once. But rational framing is only a middle step, and brilliant insight falls on deaf ears if you have not earned the right through engaging and listening. The authors also doubt the value of guarding proprietary models. Advisors add value less by inventing new paradigms than by showing a particular client the relevance of an existing one.

Emotional framing handles situations where the discussion stalls on an emotional or political issue that no rational insight can solve. Maister, working with a firm's executive committee on partner accountability, noticed squirming and whispered side talk. By asking a whisperer about it, he surfaced the real problem: no one wanted to confront the biggest rainmaker. The conversation moved to who would do it and what support they needed, and the group could then deal honestly with the courage the plan required. Emotional framing is chiefly about courage to raise hidden feelings, and it is easier to remember that you are naming the client's emotions, not your own. A painful counterexample comes from a consultant, Joe, whose client CEO asked sadly, "What are we gonna do about all those people?" before a large layoff. Joe joked it away, the deal evaporated, and the restructuring happened years later with far deeper cuts.

The authors offer a technique called naming and claiming: acknowledge how hard the issue is to raise, accept responsibility for raising it and state the issue directly. It deals with the "elephant in the parlor." Ellen, an accounting partner, saw her client's red face and white knuckles while delivering bad news. Instead of rushing, she said, "You look a little angry," and waited. The client explained he was angry at his own people, not at her, which freed her of her fears, let him vent and began a joint solution. Framing also requires dropping blame, since blame is a defense of the ego and another form of self-orientation. To implement emotional framing, string together caveats such as "it's probably just me, but..." or "you've probably thought of this already, but...," enough to cover the perceived risk, then say the thing that must be said.

> **Key takeaway.** Framing the problem, rationally and especially emotionally, adds visible value; name the unsayable issue with caveats that take responsibility, and keep blame out.

### Chapter 13: Envisioning an Alternate Reality

Envisioning is the fourth stage and the one most often skipped. Its job is to make a specific future concrete by asking what you are really aiming for, what it will look like when you get there and how you will know you have arrived. Sales models tend to jump from problem definition straight to action, and focusing on benefits is related to envisioning but not the same thing. The authors point to political slogans such as the New Deal and the "I Have a Dream" speech as compact examples of articulating something attainable that builds energy and consensus.

In business, envisioning frees people from the technical, risk-averse mode of problem solving and into a more creative one. It is the one step that is not strictly necessary but can add the greatest value. United Research, a consulting firm later absorbed by Gemini, built its sales process around a large-scale envisioning exercise. Staff asked many people whether things could be different, how they would be better, what they would look like, what would have to change and where the benefits would show up. As people described the better future in detail, change became real and possible, energizing and comforting at once. The same thing happens in a two-person conversation about a mutually attractive future. After listening and framing there is a strong temptation to leap to commitment and action, but the authors advise replacing "why don't we...?" with "how would things be if...?" and asking about benefits, end states and outcomes.

They illustrate with Mark, a consultant frustrated that a CEO client had not kept his promise of access. His first framing was about himself, speculated about the CEO's motives and judged him. After revising it to focus on the client and drop blame, he hypothesized that the CEO had become too busy to deliver what he promised, leaving both unhappy. Mark then opened a conversation with the CEO about what they were trying to accomplish and how they would know it was right, and together they pictured a future in which delays were not read as personal slights, issues were raised early, and each could rely on an agreed formula. The cooperation he needed followed.

The summary warns that when the client asks "what can we do about this?" the words act like a Pavlovian bell for advisors who want to prove technical mastery. It is better to say, "Hold on, we'll get there, but first let's talk about where we want to go." With a clear destination, both parties have signed up for the benefits and drafted the specification of the eventual solution.

> **Key takeaway.** Before moving to action, jointly describe the desired future in concrete terms, because a shared picture of the destination releases creativity and makes commitment easier.

### Chapter 14: Commitment

By commitment the authors do not mean closing a sale or drafting a contract. They mean the final stage of trust building, when the advisor ensures the client understands what will be needed to solve the problem and is willing to do it. The word has two senses, a pledge to act and an emotional state of being impelled, and the second is the one that matters here. Without it advice is just opinion: the advisor says do this, the client agrees and leaves.

By this stage the problem is framed and the vision clear, so the conversations turn to what will get in the way, what to do about it, who must be brought in, who does what, what information is needed, when to check in and what the deadlines are. These conversations are not only for the advisor's work plan. They expose the client to implementation difficulties and the new behaviors required, and show whether real commitment exists. The advisor is partly protecting himself, since a client who is not committed will not benefit from even correct advice. If risks and barriers were hidden, the client may later feel the advisor was less than open. The advisor may even play devil's advocate to test resolve, which the authors call "buckling in for the ride." Trust rises from openness and candor, and the temptation to say "no problem, leave it to us" can read as arrogance or secrecy.

Clients commit either because they feel pain or energy about an issue, or because something new has captured them. The authors guess pain drives about 80 percent of commitment, so pain relief is a prerequisite. Clients usually commit to slightly less than they could, as a defense and to retain room for surprises, and it is gratifying enough that they commit at all.

Managing expectations is central. Spell out what you will and won't do, what the client will and won't do, the boundaries of analysis, areas or people to avoid, working arrangements, communication methods and frequency, who gets which reports and how they will be used, milestones, and how success will be measured. The authors add four tips: always tell the exact truth about what you can deliver, because work accepted at great personal pain is not worth it; start the project before you're engaged; show enthusiasm; and ask the troubling questions early. Offer your work plan for comment, show disguised examples of similar work, present cheaper alternatives, and be open about challenges.

When clients resist commitment, the cause is often an earlier stage left incomplete. A client with 700 stores stalled at every strategy discussion until Charlie's team grouped them into three types and ran a profit-and-loss statement for each; the starkly different results made the choices concrete, and commitment followed. When commitment is in doubt, back up one or two steps. Fear and complacency are other reasons, and the advisor can name and claim the fear or spend some trust to raise tension against complacency. The best guide is "who, what, when." Finally, trust-based commitment is joint and personal: instead of splitting tasks neatly, the advisor links the client's work to other projects and to the client's own growth, taking a role no one else could play.

> **Key takeaway.** Commitment is gained by candidly laying out what will be required, managing expectations in detail, and making the commitment joint and personal, not by closing a sale.

## Part Three: Putting Trust to Work

### Chapter 15: What’s So Hard About All This?

If the ideas so far are straightforward, why are trusted advisors rare? The authors collect fourteen common objections and answer each. The approach feels personally risky and flaky, but careful balance, awareness and practice make it a chance to stand out, since risk is the essence of intimacy. It is hard to stop worrying about yourself, though focusing on others actually helps you find yourself, and these behaviors can be learned. Firms breed a cult of content mastery because content is measurable, but great firms now recognize how little content matters without trust. Fear of looking ignorant makes us assertive, but we look worse that way, and emotional intelligence is the skill of recognizing and acting beyond the emotion. It is hard to listen before solving, so find habit-breaking devices; Rob keeps a pen in his writing hand and makes himself switch it to the other before speaking. Speaking the unspeakable takes courage, yet advisors overrate the risk of acting and underrate the risk of not acting. If you respect the person, "invading the private" rarely offends, and the worst reply is "no, thank you." The approach does not devalue expertise; it lets expertise get through. It is not moralistic but pragmatic, so test whether trust works in your experience. It is not too slow or too expensive, since clients pay for counseling done in their presence and counseling generates future revenue better than writing proposals. If a client wants only the work at hand, say so once and ask to confirm, then you know where to invest elsewhere. Taking a preliminary position is better than none, and admitting you were wrong protects credibility more than defending an error. Humility is not servility.

The most common mistake, more than all others combined, is rushing to action before completing the earlier stages. In their example, a systems consultant diagnoses the problem correctly and pitches a fix, and the client backs away because the consultant has not earned the right to discuss the problem. Four forces drive this. The first is self-focus: we rate our own intentions above others, as 800 executives showed when almost all rated themselves more trustworthy than their colleagues. The second is the belief that we are only selling content, a belief clients reinforce. The third is the desire for tangibility, since action is concrete while the early stages are ambiguous, and professionals are trained to ask closed questions and control meetings. The fourth is the search for validation, because professionals work in a paradoxical world of intangible products and mixed signals about people, and client feedback to action reassures them.

Risk is the main objection, and it turns out to be personal, not business, risk. Consultant Rebecca did not stay in touch with a client she liked, thinking it presumptuous, and the client later asked why she never called. The authors argue that risk is part of trust, not its opposite. Perceived credibility risk comes from an exclusive notion of professionalism (you must have all answers, hide gaps), while perceived intimacy risk comes from assuming clients want purely rational dealings, although buying is an emotional act. There is the risk of doing a wrong thing and the risk of not doing a right thing, and the second is worse. Underneath are fears: not having the answer, looking confused, revealing ignorance, misdiagnosing. The good news is that personal risks are usually reciprocated. Finally, managing your own emotions is much of the job, as in the story of the unknown "XP-27" reference you are too proud to ask about. The urges to control include taking credit, filling air time, listing credentials, capping the problem, hedging and telling your own story too soon.

> **Key takeaway.** The obstacles to trusted-advisor behavior are mostly personal fears of risk and a habit of rushing to action; recognizing them and practicing new habits is how you overcome them.

### Chapter 16: Differing Client Types

The authors warn against overgeneralizing about clients and offer principles for adapting to differences. First, prepare in advance by considering what is different about this client and about you in this situation, and take a lesson from your best and worst client experiences. Two stories show the cost of skipping this. Rob delivered a session at a consulting firm's offsite without being told the founders disagreed about positioning, and two of them derailed it within minutes. Maister facilitated a client's retreat for the third year without learning the group had split, and instinctively blamed the client. The lesson is that the advisor must take responsibility for asking in advance which topics are too delicate, where views are divided, where resistance is likely and what other initiatives are running.

Second, ask three questions of every client: what is their prevailing personal motivation (to excel, to act and get results, to analyze before deciding, or to build consensus), what is their personality, and how does their organization shape their worldview? Then adapt your style to how they like to be dealt with. The authors compare this to good acting, which is not lying but drawing out the part of yourself the role needs. Third, find something you genuinely like about the client, and if nothing comes to mind, that is a hint this is not the right client for you. Fourth, use the trust equation, since clients weigh the components differently. Some start with credibility and reliability and need to be led toward intimacy, while others signal readiness early. Clients who value being understood allow you to move fast, rich communicators want you to get to real issues quickly, highly rational people emphasize framing, and structured envisioning suits both skeptical, deductive thinkers and creative blue-sky types. Discourage clients who jump to action.

They then describe nine difficult types, each with a suggested response. The "Just the facts" client is pleading to be understood, so reflect their values in their own language and confirm. The "I'll get back to you" client is nervous about judging on the spot, so provide a one-page summary and make it easy. The "You're the expert, dummy" client is trying to dominate, so offer hypotheses and ask to explore further without calling the bluff. The "Let me handle that" client distrusts you or everyone, so use the trust process or name and claim privately. The "Let's go through this again" client hides a need for control in details, so make the big picture feel controllable by offering proportions or a familiar model. The "You don't understand" client wants to feel special, so ask them to teach you what is different. The "My enemy's enemy" client loves politics, so treat politics as a content issue with an agenda and pros and cons. The "Just like, you know, come on" client struggles verbally, so write up options to rank. The "Oh, by the way" client leaves you out of key meetings, probably because he has labeled you as an expert in one area, so ask for a private meeting, avoid any phrase that centers on what you want, and ask what it would take to get in the loop.

> **Key takeaway.** Clients differ in motivation, personality, how they weigh the trust factors and their difficult habits, so prepare, ask in advance and adapt your approach to each one.

### Chapter 17: The Lieutenant Columbo Approach

The authors use the television detective Lieutenant Columbo as a metaphor. In the show, the audience knows from the start who the criminal is, so the interest lies in watching a master draw out the truth. The key to Columbo is his style and character, not any method. He dismisses official procedure in favor of situational instinct, and he presents himself as rumpled and befuddled, with an old car, a cheap cigar, a trench coat, an unnamed wife and requests to use the restroom. He seems overwhelmed and underqualified, often dazzled by the brilliance of the suspect. In reality his style is chosen to eradicate a preconception and put the other party at ease so that the suspect lets down his guard. The authors stress they do not suggest looking incompetent, only that one should look at what happens one level below the surface.

Their parallel is that clients are often intimidated by the advisor's expertise and carry a "you expert, me dummy" feeling that evokes resentment, awe, competition and the wish to be liked. The client also arrives with a problem that has stakes, adding anxiety. The trappings of professional life reinforce this: the framed degree, the lab coat, the latest gadgets. Columbo punctures all of it by praising the other person's intelligence, wearing the anti-uniform, making small talk and downplaying his skills. The inhibitions vanish, information is offered, people reveal themselves as they are, and progress becomes possible.

The lesson sounds obvious, to put the client at ease and not overwhelm them with expertise, but it is hard to follow. The biggest barrier is our love of the things that put clients on guard, because we worked hard for the degrees and status symbols. The second is the belief that success is purely technical mastery, which leads us to nod sagely, drop wise remarks and then descend with the Answer. The enemy, as in Pogo's dictum, is ourselves: our inability to focus on the client and keep our own personality, needs and image out of the way. Columbo's genius is neutralizing expectations about himself. He is the anti-trickster who strips away illusions, becomes the professional equivalent of background music and subordinates his ego to the service of the case. The Columbo caveats from Chapter 12 ("you've probably thought of this already, but...") belong to the same toolkit.

> **Key takeaway.** Put the client at ease by dropping status symbols and ego, like Columbo, so they stop guarding themselves and tell you what is really going on.

### Chapter 18: The Role of Trust in Getting Hired

The chapter opens with a disguised consulting firm, Dalton, that pioneered industrial-style sales in professional services. It built four separate units for generating leads, closing, diagnosing and delivering, each with its own measures and rewards. For some years this worked, but delivery staff saw selling as no part of their job, the lead generators never called on existing clients and no one was looking for follow-on opportunities. The result was a machine with low client retention and thin margins that depended on a steady flow of new clients. When demand for its main service slowed and prime prospects ran out, the firm declined quickly. The authors do not condemn specialization. Their point is that selling and serving need some mechanism that connects them, since the professional habit of looking for new opportunities and the habit of excellent delivery belong together.

Most professionals still draw a line between selling (getting the client to agree) and serving (doing the work), but it blurs the moment you try to define it. Selling means showing the client that you have something to offer and can be trusted, which is an act of serving. Serving means delighting the client so that they rehire you and refer you, which is selling. The two are flip sides of one coin: acting like a trusted advisor and a caring professional.

"Service as selling" begins with discomfort about overt selling and the hope that quality will speak for itself. The authors suggest reframing the goal as getting hired, meaning convincing someone to put their trust in you. Their example is a beauty contest with three hours to present against three rivals. Instead of a standard presentation, use the three hours as the first three hours of the project. Because professional services are intangible, expensive and risky, with financial, emotional and career risk, the winner is the one who lets the buyer feel what working together would be like, which is also why incumbents have such an edge. Many professionals talk about serving without doing it, holding back knowledge until fees are paid, which loses both the work and the relationship. Clients prefer to buy from a sample, like a painting they can see.

"Selling as service" argues the reverse: if we see something bad about to happen to a client we are obliged to say so, and by extension we should point out significant opportunities for improvement. Doing so is what others call selling, and the authors call it contributing ideas. Advisors too often leave opportunities unnoted, which is unprofessional. The link between selling and serving is the same ethos: keeping the client's best interests at heart.

> **Key takeaway.** Selling and serving are one activity when you act as a trusted advisor, so win work by starting to help and by pointing out opportunities, not by pitching.

### Chapter 19: Building Trust on the Current Assignment

Maister has previously listed nine factors that raise a client's perceived value of service: understanding, sense of control, sense of progress, access and availability, responsiveness, reliability, appreciation, sense of importance and respect. It is not enough to respect the client, the client must experience the respect, for instance through regularly asking for their opinion. The authors then list tactics grouped by theme. To involve the client, use brainstorming, give them tasks, offer options and keep them informed on what, when and why. To make reports and presentations more useful, let the client set the format, provide a summary usable internally without editing and have a non-project person test readability. To help the client use what you deliver, coach them on dealing with others in their organization and on how to share results. To make meetings valuable, set goals and agendas, send material ahead, research attendees, agree next steps, send a summary within a day and call to confirm the goals were met. To be accessible, warn clients ahead of absences, brief assistants and get clients comfortable with junior staff.

During the assignment itself, do not disappear and return with a finished product. Keep clients updated, ask questions that show real work is happening and build their input visibly into the result. Share the thorny parts of the job, not to complain but to check whether the client sees them the same way, and connect the assignment to their larger agenda. Check in personally, ask who might be a "tough sell" and build barrier busters into the product, learn the names of support staff, revisit unresolved items from past notes, occasionally bring something of interest from outside the assignment, share more information rather than less and make many small commitments that you keep. Expect the client to change their mind as circumstances shift. After the courtship comes a short honeymoon, followed by doubt about whether the approach will work, so deliver a small, fast result early, or at least a status report.

Further advice: keep clients in the loop so they are never surprised, even by good news. Be careful about promises, because clients forget the promises you keep and remember the ones you break. Tell the truth, not what the client wants to hear, and accept responsibility, since "it wasn't my fault" sounds like a lame defense. Love your work and show it. Make sure your answer is not purely technical by asking how the work affects the client personally and financially, and create the follow-on plan for free. Don't ask for follow-on work too quickly: eager is good, aggressive is not.

The chapter ends with five things that destroy trust. Compromising an individual's confidences to show you're in the know. Missing the client's unspoken doubts, as when Charlie and Rob dismissed a banking client's complaint about format, and the client concluded that if the format failed, the content did too. Going around the client, because nothing stays secret. The attitude of "sometimes wrong but never in doubt," which alienates the organization. And blowing a deadline that matters to the client, even an arbitrary one; if it will slip, say so in advance.

> **Key takeaway.** On the job, trust grows through many small, visible acts: involving the client, early results, honest updates, kept promises, and avoiding the five trust destroyers.

### Chapter 20: Re-earning Trust Away from the Current Assignment

Trust is built outside current assignments as well, and the authors draw on Maister's interviews with clients of professional firms around the world. Their common complaints are that providers only want to sell, never make the client feel important, offer generic issues instead of company-specific ones, and romance clients with dinners and events that the clients do not want. The conclusions are that clients expect providers to keep earning future business, that they dislike a sales pitch and that they want advisors to invest their own unbilled time. Many advisors produce a sales plan where a relationship plan is needed, and clients can tell the difference.

Clients also say what they want. They want advisors to make an impact on the business and not just be visible, do more preliminary work on spec, help them think and develop strategy, lead their thinking about the business five or ten years out, use the client's data to add analysis, join offsite brainstorming sessions, sit in on meetings to understand how the business works, benchmark them against others, explain why competitors do what they do and propose other ideas. In short, they want a business partner, not a false friend, and they want mutual discussion, not presentations.

The authors urge advisors to stay in touch, since the instinct that calling without a live project is an imposition is mistaken. Calling only when you want something destroys trust, and a project is never over as long as you check in. It is never too late to reestablish a relationship, though it gets harder with time.

For large clients, firms want institutional relationships, which cannot rest on one relationship manager talking to a few decision makers. Trust is personal, so many people serving the client must establish multiple contacts and maintain consistent standards. Junior staff who work with the client's field people are often best placed to see emerging issues, since headquarters is often the last to know. The relationship manager acts as the client's representative inside the firm and focuses on the long-term relationship, not on generating fees, because salespeople are less accepted. Their job is to manage the relationship, not to build it alone: create chances for colleagues to meet more client executives, such as free internal seminars or sending a colleague to a client meeting. They coach the team, give them challenge and meaning, make them look good, serve them first, and provide research and summaries of trade publications. All of this is an investment requiring unbilled budgets and a longer horizon than fee credits allow, and the payoff is a clear link between nurturing relationships and profitability.

> **Key takeaway.** Keep investing in clients between projects: stay in touch, bring ideas on spec, and manage the relationship across your whole team without turning it into a sales plan.

### Chapter 21: The Case of Cross-Selling

Cross-selling means selling within an organization that is already a client, so it always involves new people on one side or the other. It seems ideal for relationships, but the authors point out that it is really a meeting of strangers carrying heavy expectations, while the decisive sales events happen between people who know each other well. Trust is personal and not very transferable, so an advisor can help a colleague gain a client's trust but rarely enough, like introducing someone to your prospective in-laws. Institutional trust does not transfer.

They classify three types using the old matrix of existing versus new customers and products. Type 1, Expand, introduces a new service to a current client person, with a new content expert on the firm's side. Type 2, Broaden, offers the existing service to a new person in the client organization. Type 3, Diversify, involves two new players, selling a new service to a new client executive. Their baseline is the trust equation: 1.25 for a new client and 5 for a current one.

![Grid of the three cross-selling types: expand, broaden and diversify](/figures/the-trusted-advisor/cross-selling-types.svg "Redrawn: cross-selling moves along two axes, whether the service is new and whether the client person is new.")

In Type 1, advisor Amy tells her client Curt about colleague Barbara and hands over a business card. Curt feels pressed for a favor while receiving little, and trust is being drawn down, not built; the authors score it even below a new client. Amy also has an internal sale to make to Barbara, who may get less credit for pursuing someone else's lead, worry about wasting time and fear embarrassment if Amy has misdiagnosed or overpromised. Amy must show the issue is real, that she has Barbara's interests at heart and that Barbara is not being set up, which she can do by investing a bit of extra time educating Barbara before approaching the client.

In Type 2, Amy asks Curt for an introduction to Don, another executive. Curt faces risk and no apparent upside. The existing client must be part of the selling team, because he is the most objective and aligned party, so Amy needs to explain honestly what is in it for him: better knowledge of his group, a good reflection on him, informed handling of shared issues, economies of scale, political opportunities and insights for his own work. What matters is that she truly believes that helping is in the client organization's interest. In Type 3, with four relationships at work, the sale should be built iteratively, with the internal sales first: talk to the expert, get more information from the client, define the problem and repeat until the final meeting involving everyone will be easy because everyone has invested time and knows each other.

The hidden relationship is between Amy and Curt, because Amy asks Curt to believe that Barbara is an expert, that Amy understands the new issue and that her motives are good. The challenge is Amy's own credibility, intimacy and self-orientation, not Barbara's. Emotional trickiness is handled by naming and claiming unspoken issues, with lines like "if I were in your shoes, I'd worry about bringing in someone new." The trust equation makes a good checklist, and investing your own time is the clearest signal of good intentions.

> **Key takeaway.** Cross-selling works only when the advisor invests time in each new relationship and builds trust with colleague and client, because trust does not transfer from an institution or a business card.

### Chapter 22: The Quick-Impact List to Gain Trust

The last chapter answers a frequent question: what are the fastest, highest-impact ways to gain trust? The authors give eleven. First, listen to everything, and paraphrase until the speaker says, "Yes, that's exactly it." Second, empathize for real: listening shows you heard, empathy shows you understood. When you think "this guy's an idiot," ask why he believes what he does. Anyone who understands us earns the right to disagree and still have our respect. Third, note what they are feeling, which takes a moment, pays off at once and feels riskier than it is, using a plain observation such as "you seem distracted." Fourth, build a shared agenda at the start of any interaction, which gives data, models "we-not-me" and creates buy-in.

Fifth, take a point of view. Articulating one, even a wrong one, stimulates reactions and crystallizes the issues, and phrases such as "let me float a trial balloon" make it safe. Sixth, take a personal risk, since intimacy is the act of risking a personal loss. Seventh, ask about a related area, which shows you care. Eighth, ask great, open-ended questions, which respect the speaker's frame of reference. Ninth, give away ideas: David Nadler suggests responding with three to five ideas, introduced as possibly wild. Expertise is like love, not limited, and you destroy it only by not giving it away. Tenth, return calls unbelievably fast; a pastor they know aims for ten minutes and calls it her most trust-creating habit. Eleventh, relax your mind before stressful meetings by concentrating on one saying at a time, such as "it's about the client," "the answer is a better question," "I am not the center of the universe," "it's a we game, not a me game," or "what am I afraid of here?"

Further tips include noticing and commenting on a feeling in yourself, making a commitment and delivering it, not answering the first time a question is asked but seeking clarification, sharing something revealing without manipulating, making an empathetic facial expression, and acknowledging what has been held back. A set of reminders includes: you do not have to prove yourself every ten seconds, ask "really? and then what happened?", have you earned the right to give an answer yet, turn arguments back into conversation, emulate Columbo, take responsibility for the emotional outcome, blame nobody, value problem definition over problem answers, and listen to your gut. The book ends with two suggestions: call your client now, and tell your romantic partner today how much you appreciate them.

> **Key takeaway.** The fastest trust builders are small behaviors you can start today: listen and paraphrase, empathize, name feelings, share the agenda, take a position, give ideas away and return calls fast.

## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The first theme is that trust is both rational and emotional, and professionals consistently underweight the emotional half. Chapter 3 says trust is personal, grows over time and entails risk, and Chapter 8 turns it into a formula in which intimacy and low self-orientation separate trusted advisors from merely competent ones. Technical expertise is treated as the entry fee, not the differentiator.

The second theme is the discipline of focusing on the other person. The mindsets of Chapter 6, the listening skills of Chapter 11, the Columbo approach of Chapter 17 and the quick-impact list of Chapter 22 all train advisors to quiet their own agenda: to listen before answering, ask open questions, admit ignorance, share the agenda and take responsibility instead of blaming. The third theme is process. The five stages (Chapters 9 to 14) give advisors a sequence that keeps them from jumping to solutions, with framing, especially emotional framing through "naming and claiming" (Chapter 12), as the most demanding step.

The fourth theme is that selling and serving are the same activity. Chapters 18 to 21 argue that you win work by beginning to help, keep clients by staying in touch between projects, and cross-sell only by building trust anew with each person involved.

### Strengths and limits

The strengths are clarity and usable language. The trust equation, the five stages and the many checklists give readers something to diagnose and practice, and the authors supply actual phrasing for hard moments, from caveats that make a risky comment safer to responses for nine difficult client types. Stories from the authors' own mistakes make the book candid and humane.

The limits are evidence and scope. Most support is anecdote and the authors' experience, the trust scores are admitted guesses, and the book is aimed at individual advisor-client relationships in professional services, with less to say about teams, power imbalances or markets where trust is not the main purchase criterion. The lists can feel exhaustive and repetitive.

*Where I'd push back:* the claim that anyone who tries to build trust succeeds understates cases where clients simply want a cheap commodity or where a firm's incentives work against the advisor, and the line between technique and manipulation is handled more briefly than it deserves.

### Applying it

1. **Score yourself on the trust equation** (Chapter 8). Rate each client on credibility, reliability, intimacy and self-orientation as the client would, and pick the weakest to work on.
2. **Listen and paraphrase before you advise** (Chapters 11 and 22). Share the agenda, ask open questions and do not answer until the client says "yes, exactly."
3. **Name the unsayable** (Chapter 12). When there is an elephant in the room, acknowledge the difficulty, take responsibility and state the issue.
4. **Envision and commit before acting** (Chapters 13 and 14). Describe the desired future together, then be candid about what it will take and manage expectations.
5. **Invest between projects** (Chapters 19 and 20). Stay in touch, bring ideas on spec and keep many small promises.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 3, 8, 11 and 12. Chapter 3 explains how trust works, Chapter 8 gives the trust equation, Chapter 11 covers listening and Chapter 12 shows how to frame hard issues.

Focus on the other person, self-confidence, ego strength, curiosity and inclusive professionalism.
