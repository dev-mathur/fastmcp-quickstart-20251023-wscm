## Overview

*The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* is Barbara Minto's guide to making your thinking clear to a reader. Minto, an alumna of McKinsey & Company who taught the method to consultants and executives, argues that clear writing is clear thinking made visible. Every good document, in her view, is a pyramid: one main point at the top, supported by a small group of ideas, each of which is supported in turn by another group. If your ideas do not fit that shape, the problem is in the thinking, not the prose, and fixing the structure first is the fastest way to a document the reader can absorb.

The book is organized into four parts. Part One (Chapters 1 to 4) explains why the pyramid works and how to build one top-down or bottom-up, with a detailed treatment of the introduction. Part Two (Chapters 5 to 7) covers the logic underneath: deduction and induction, the four ways to order ideas, and how to summarize a group so that it says something. Part Three (Chapters 8 and 9) applies this to problem solving, with a way to define a problem and to structure an analysis before collecting data. Part Four (Chapters 10 to 12) is about presentation on the page, on a screen and in prose. Two appendices deal with structureless problems and with standard introductions. The edition summarized here is a page-image scan, so the reading order was reconstructed from the page numbers and headings; the part and chapter titles follow the book's own cross-references.

Three ideas hold the book together.

1. **Ideas must form a pyramid.** Each idea summarizes the group below it, each group contains one kind of idea, and each group is in a logical order. The reader then meets the conclusion first and the support after it.
2. **The introduction is a story the reader already knows.** A situation, a complication and the question it raises lead to your answer, which is the top of the pyramid. Aim the whole document at a question that is already in the reader's mind.
3. **Structure the thinking before you gather or write.** Use MECE groupings, diagnostic frameworks and a clear definition of the problem to decide what to look for, and word actions as end products so you can tell whether anything is missing.

## Part One: The Pyramid Principle

This part explains why a pyramid works for the reader, what its parts are, how to build one and how to write the introduction that sets it up.

### Chapter 1: Why Pyramid Structure

The opening chapter argues that the reader of any document faces a heavy task. Even a two-page memo contains roughly a hundred sentences, and the reader has to take them in one at a time, relate them and hold them together. The task is much easier if they arrive as a pyramid, starting at the top and working down. Minto bases this on how the mind works: it sorts incoming information into pyramidal groupings in order to understand it, so any set of ideas is easier to comprehend if it arrives already sorted that way. The conclusion is that every document should be built deliberately as a pyramid of ideas.

The chapter first describes the mind's habit of imposing order. We see stars as constellations, and a row of dots as two groups when some are closer together. People group things that look alike or sit near each other, and the value of seeing things in logical units is immense. Minto illustrates it with the way a person remembers a shopping list. Asked to recall nine items, most people remember them if they sort them into categories such as dairy and produce, because the categories become the units the mind stores. Two limits follow. The mind can hold only about seven items at once, so each group in a pyramid should stay at or below that number, and it is not enough to group ideas, because you must also state the logic that links them, which is a statement one level above.

Since the reader can take in sentences only one by one, the best order of presentation is to give the summarizing idea before the ideas it summarizes. Ordering from the top down is the single most important act in clear writing. The reader assumes that ideas appearing together belong together, so a list that mixes unlike items sends the mind scrambling for a relationship that is not there. Minto shows how unlike a grouped pyramid and a loose list feel by presenting a list whose items cannot be sorted into any pattern, and the reader's frustration is the point.

The chapter then states the three rules that decide whether ideas form a proper pyramid:

1. Ideas at any level must summarize the ideas grouped below them.
2. Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea.
3. Ideas in each grouping must be logically ordered.

The rules can be checked in advance, before a word is written. Rule one reflects the fact that thinking is abstracting: a paragraph's point summarizes its sentences, a section's point summarizes its paragraphs. Rules two and three make that possible, since you can only draw a clean summary from a group whose members are alike and in a sequence that shows nothing is missing.

A pyramid also gives a conversation between writer and reader. Each idea tells the reader something he does not already know, which raises a natural question (Why? How? Why do you say that?), and the ideas below answer it. A whimsical sample, an argument that pigs make good pets because they are beautiful, shows the dialogue: the claim is clear enough that the reader can see exactly what the writer is saying and can disagree with it precisely. The same device makes a twenty-page business memo readable in minutes.

![A pyramid with a main point at the top, three key line points beneath it and two supporting points under each](/figures/pyramid-principle/pyramid.svg "Redrawn: the structure of a pyramid of ideas, with a main point, a key line and supporting groups.")

The chapter ends by noting that the structure can be used in two directions. You can start with the question and the main point and work down (the top-down approach), or begin with a pile of ideas and work out what they imply (the bottom-up approach), both covered in Chapter 3. In either case the finished document should place the answer first and the support after it.

> **Key takeaway.** Readers understand best when ideas arrive as a pyramid: a single point on top, supported by groups of like ideas in a logical order, with each idea summarizing the group below it.


### Chapter 2: The Substructures Within the Pyramid

Chapter 2 takes the pyramid apart. Every idea below the main point has a vertical relationship to the ideas above and below it and a horizontal relationship to its neighbors, and the chapter adds a third structure, the introduction, which tells the story that starts the whole thing. Minto starts with a plain observation: prose is written one sentence after another down the page, which hides the fact that the ideas sit at different levels of abstraction. Most writers have only a hazy notion of their ideas when they begin, and cannot know precisely what they think until they say it out loud or write it down. The substructures give them a way to find out.

**The vertical relationship.** An idea is defined as a statement that raises a question in the reader's mind, because it tells him something he does not already know. The ideas below answer that question, using one of a few standard forms: why, how or what, which are answered by reasons, steps or a list of points. This sets up a question and answer dialogue that pulls the reader through your reasoning, and it explains why writing that says only what the reader already knows is dull. Minto warns against what she calls intellectually blank assertions, statements like "The company has two problems" or "There are three reasons", which promise an idea but do not state one. They anchor nothing, and they let the writer avoid the work of saying what the points have in common. A reader cannot respond to a blank assertion, and neither can the writer's own mind push further.

**The horizontal relationship.** Ideas in a grouping answer the question raised by the point above, and they must do it logically. Minto says there are only two types of logical relationship: deductive and inductive. A deductive grouping is an argument in three steps, where the first point states a fact about the world, the second comments on its subject or predicate and the third states the implication of the two together. An inductive grouping is a set of ideas that you can describe with the same plural noun, such as reasons, problems or steps, and the idea above states what their likeness implies. The two forms are very different, and you must pick one or the other for any grouping, never both at once. Chapter 5 discusses the difference in detail.

![A deductive chain of three linked statements next to an inductive grouping of three similar ideas under one summary](/figures/pyramid-principle/deduction-induction.svg "Redrawn: deduction links statements in a chain, induction groups like ideas under a summary.")

**The introductory flow.** The third substructure is the introduction, which Minto calls the most important piece of thinking you do. A reader will find your conclusion interesting only if it answers a question that is already present in his mind, so the introduction retells a story he already knows. It has a situation (something he knows and accepts), a complication (what changed or went wrong), and the question the complication raises, and the document then supplies the answer, which is the main point at the top of the pyramid. When the reader nods at the situation and thinks "so what?", the complication follows and the question appears of its own accord. The author's job is to make sure the question you answer is the one the reader would ask.

![A flow from situation to complication to question to answer, with the first three making up the introduction](/figures/pyramid-principle/scq.svg "Redrawn: the situation, complication, question and answer sequence of an introduction.")

The point of the three structures together is that they give you the means to discover your ideas rather than only to arrange them. Once you know the rules for how ideas relate up, down and sideways, you can start from the answer, the question or even a single idea and work out the rest, which is exactly what Chapter 3 shows how to do. The chapter also explains the Key Line, the row of ideas immediately below the main point, which doubles as the plan for the whole document.

> **Key takeaway.** Each idea relates upward as a summary, downward as the answer to a question and sideways as part of a logical grouping, and the introduction's situation, complication and question set up the answer at the top.


### Chapter 3: How to Build a Pyramid Structure

The trouble when you sit down to write, Minto says, is that you roughly know the subject but not exactly what you want to say. Even so, you know a good deal about your end product. There will be a sentence at the top with a subject and predicate, its subject will be the subject of the document, and it will answer a question that already exists in the reader's mind because of a situation he knows. Chapter 3 turns these givens into two procedures, one that works from the top down and one that works from the bottom up.

**The top-down approach.** This is generally easier because you begin with the things you are most sure of, the subject and the reader's knowledge of it. The procedure uses the structure of the introduction to pull the right ideas out of your head one at a time:

1. Draw a box for the top of the pyramid and write the subject in it, if you know it.
2. Decide the question: picture the reader and ask what he wants to know about the subject.
3. Write down the answer you plan to give, even if it is provisional.
4. State the situation, the first thing you can say about the subject that the reader will accept as true.
5. Develop the complication. The reader says "I know that, so what?", and you ask what happened in that situation to raise his question.
6. Recheck the question and answer: the complication should lead naturally to the question you wrote down, and if it does not, change one of them.
7. Work out the key line. The answer raises a new question, usually why or how, and the points below answer it.
8. Move down and spell out the support for each key line point. For a short document you may simply start writing, since the supporting ideas will come to mind as you reach each section.

The method forces you to draw from your head only what is relevant to the reader's question. It also tells you what type of key line you need: if the question is "how", the answer is steps, and if it is "why", reasons.

**The bottom-up approach.** Sometimes you do not know your point and only have a pile of material. The procedure is to list all the points you want to make, work out the relationships among them, draw conclusions from each group, and then state the main point that all the groups imply. Minto says this is slower and more difficult but sometimes unavoidable. A worked example takes a rambling memo about the costs of a composing room and sorts its points into problems and solutions, uncovers the cause-and-effect links between them and arrives at a structure where raising wages and eliminating unnecessary steps follow from a statement that costs could be cut.

**Caveats for beginners.** The rules of the pyramid let you start with an idea anywhere and find the others, but practice comes with advice. Always try top-down first, because the minute an idea is written down it takes on a deceptive beauty and you become reluctant to change it, so do not dictate a whole draft in the hope of finding structure later. Do not skip the introduction, because it forces you to confirm the question. Use the bottom-up approach when you have a stack of facts or an unclear top point, and check what you derive against the rules at each level. Finally, be prepared to iterate, since the first pyramid is a hypothesis you then test against the rules.

The chapter also shows how the pyramid translates to the page. A key line should be set out near the start of a long document so the reader gets the whole thinking in half a minute and can decide whether to read on. Each key line point then gets its own heading and its own short introduction.

> **Key takeaway.** Build the pyramid from the top by fixing the subject, the reader's question and the answer, then check the situation and complication that lead to it, and use the bottom-up method only when you must discover your point from raw material.


### Chapter 4: Fine Points of Introductions

Minto believes more mistakes are made in introductions than in any other part of a document, and Chapter 4 is a long treatment of how to get them right. The introduction does more than get the reader's interest: it controls what he is thinking when he meets your main point. Its story form gives a feeling of plausibility to the writer's interpretation of the situation, and because the selection of facts is always somewhat biased, that influence is something to use with care.

**Why a story.** A reader will focus on an unfinished story, as people do when they hear that two strangers met on a bridge at midnight. Every good story has a beginning, a middle and an end, which in a document are the situation, the complication and the resolution. The resolution is always the main point, because you write either to resolve a problem or to answer a question. The three elements go in the introduction in a standard order, though you can vary the order for tone, as in a version that starts with the complication for urgency or with the answer when the reader is impatient.

**Rules for the content.** The introduction should say only things the reader will agree are true. A reliable test is whether the point could be checked by an objective observer, or would be accepted if it appeared in a business magazine. It should not explain cause-and-effect relationships, since those were discovered through analysis and the reader has not yet agreed to them, and it should not include simple historical occurrences that are not logical ideas. Information the reader does not already know distorts the question, so keep it out. The opening situation sentence anchors the reader to a specific time and place and states something about the subject, which gives the story its base.

**Length and pattern.** The length of an introduction is not related to the length of the document. It is related to what the reader needs to be told in order to grasp the significance of the main point and want to read on. Over time you will see the same patterns recur. They answer one of a few standard questions: what should we do, should we do what we plan, how do we do it, or why did it happen. Some documents give a direction, and then the author plants the question rather than reminding the reader of it. Others report on a request, a proposal, an audit or a decision.

**Key line points need introductions too.** Each key line point can start with its own small story, so that the reader knows where he stands as he moves from one section to the next. In an example on management techniques, the initial introduction reminds the reader of what he knows about current management tools, while the next section begins by recalling what he has just learned and showing why the next tool is the logical follow-on. Minto asks writers to link sections by their ideas rather than their labels, for example by carrying a key phrase from the end of one section into the start of the next.

**Difficult forms.** Two situations need extra care. When you must propose steps to solve a problem, as in a consulting proposal or project plan, the introduction defines the problem and the document is structured around the steps of the analysis. When you must deal with alternatives, remember that strictly speaking there is no such thing as an alternative solution to a problem, because either what you recommend gets the reader from the undesired result to the desired one or it does not. Alternatives appear only when the desired result is vaguely stated, and then the right approach is to restate the objective or structure the choice around which objectives matter. A final section on **next steps** may close a long document, but only if the steps are logically obvious to the reader, since this is the one place a conclusion is useful.

> **Key takeaway.** Write the introduction as a short, agreed-upon story of situation, complication and question, include only what the reader will accept as true, and give every major section its own mini-introduction.


## Part Two: Logic in Thinking

Part Two starts from the point that applying the pyramid to a specific writing task is usually easy at the top, but that the lower levels need checking. Its three chapters give the logic tools for that check.


### Chapter 5: Deduction and Induction: The Difference

Clear writing, Minto says, results from stating exactly how a group of ideas relates, and ideas relate in three ways: up (a summary), down (an explanation) and sideways (a logical order). What counts as logical order depends on whether the group was formed deductively or inductively, and these are the only two patterns available. Understanding the difference lets you recognize either form, pick the right one and test your own groupings.

**Deductive reasoning.** A deductive argument is often described as a syllogism, but Minto avoids the technical terms and says it must do three things: state a fact about the world, state a related fact that comments on the subject or predicate of the first, and state the implication of both. The classic example is that men are mortal, Socrates is a man and therefore Socrates is mortal. The idea above a deductive grouping is a simple summary resting heavily on the last point. Arguments can be chained when the reader will accept the missing steps, but only with limits: no more than four points in an argument, and no more than two "therefore" points chained, or the summary becomes too heavy.

**Inductive reasoning.** An inductive grouping is a set of ideas that can be described with the same plural noun (reasons, problems, steps, ways). The idea above states what the likeness implies. If French, German and Russian tanks are all at the Polish border, you can infer a warlike movement against Poland. The key technique is to find the single plural noun that describes the grouping and test that every member fits it. A grouping without a common noun is a list of unrelated facts, and "news" has no place in a document meant to make a point. Checking the reasoning works from the bottom up: given the points, can you really infer the statement above? A claim that managers mismanage because they want to fails because the inference goes beyond the evidence.

**How they differ.** In deduction each point depends on the one before, and in induction the points are parallel and independent. This leads to a practical difference. Deductive reasoning is the form the mind prefers for thinking, partly because it is easier to build, but it is a ponderous way to write, since the reader must take in the first point, hold it through the second and only then see the point of the third. Inductive reasoning, by contrast, lets the reader take each point as a complete thought. Minto therefore urges that, on the key line, you present your message inductively. The exceptions are when the reader will not accept the conclusion without the argument, as when you tell someone to sell the business instead of cutting costs, and when the reader needs the reasoning to understand a technical approach. Push deductive arguments as low in the pyramid as possible.

**Spotting a disguised argument.** A deductive argument is often dressed up as an inductive one. When you have only one piece of evidence for a point, you are forced to deal with it deductively. The memo about the high-cost printing house is an example: the supposed list of reasons is really a chain in which low productivity leads to overtime, which leads to uncompetitive prices. The test is simple. If the second point comments on the first, it is deductive, and if instead you can classify all the points under the same plural noun, the grouping is inductive. Minto shows that a deductive-looking chain of three points can often be restated as a short inductive set, and a good writer knows which they are using.

![A deductive chain of three linked statements next to an inductive grouping of three similar ideas under one summary](/figures/pyramid-principle/deduction-induction.svg "Redrawn: deduction links statements in a chain, induction groups like ideas under a summary.")

> **Key takeaway.** Use inductive groupings, identified by a shared plural noun, on the key line, and reserve deductive chains of no more than four points for lower levels or for conclusions the reader would otherwise reject.


### Chapter 6: Imposing Logical Order

The second rule of the pyramid is that ideas in any grouping must be in a logical order. The rule is a check as well as a style: if you cannot put a group in one-two-three order, you cannot be sure that its members all belong together and that none is missing. In a deductive grouping the argument itself dictates the order. In an inductive grouping you have a choice, and Chapter 6 explains how to make it and how to defend it.

**Only four orders exist.** Minto's central claim is that the mind can perform only four analytical activities with ideas, and each produces a group with its own natural order. You can work out the causes of an effect, which gives a process and time order. You can divide a whole into its parts, which gives structural order. You can classify like things together, which gives degree order. And you can reason deductively, which gives argument order. The way you made the grouping therefore tells you how to order it.

![Four cards for time, structural, degree and argument order, each showing what kind of grouping it comes from](/figures/pyramid-principle/four-orders.svg "Redrawn: the four logical orders and the kind of grouping that produces each.")

**Time order.** Whenever you tell a reader to do something, you do so because you believe the action will produce an effect. When several actions together produce the effect, they form a process or system, and the natural order is the sequence in which they must happen. Cause and effect also give time order: the cause precedes its result. Time order is the most important for action ideas, and Chapter 7 shows how to word the steps. If you cannot state the sequence, it is likely that some steps are missing.

**Structural order.** To divide a whole into its parts, you must make sure that the pieces are mutually exclusive of each other and collectively exhaustive in terms of the whole. Minto abbreviates this to MECE. A division of a tire-and-rubber company into three divisions is MECE if what happens in one division is not repeated in another and the three together cover everything the company does. Once the structure is drawn, the order of the pieces follows the diagram, usually top to bottom and left to right, and you describe each part as it appears. How the boxes are arranged reflects the principle of division: activities go in process order, locations in structural order, and products or customers in order of importance. The same technique describes a map, a machine, an organization chart or an industry, and it also works when recommending changes to a structure, because you can describe the steps in the order you would redraw the diagram.

![A clean three-way division of company sales next to a flawed one with an overlap and a missing segment](/figures/pyramid-principle/mece.svg "Redrawn: a mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive division beside a flawed one.")

**Degree order.** When you say that a company has three problems, you do not state the literal truth, because it has many problems and you have classified three as noteworthy. Each must therefore share a characteristic that the others lack. To prove that the group is mutually exclusive, you define that characteristic precisely, search your knowledge to make sure you have included every known item that has it, and then rank the members by how strongly each shows the trait, strongest first. Many people ask whether it is acceptable to start with the weakest and build up. It is more dramatic, Minto concedes, but drama is a stylistic matter, not a logical one, and the strongest-first order is the logical default.

**Imposing order exposes nonsense.** The most useful consequence is diagnostic. When a writer's list resists every order, the reason is usually that the points were never properly thought through. Minto walks through lists that look reasonable until she forces them into a structure, a process or a ranking and then sees that two of the points repeat each other, a step is absent or the group mixes causes with effects. An item that could be sorted into several groups, or a group that contains an unrelated outlier, is a signal that the grouping has no real basis. The reverse is also true: once the structure is imposed, flaws and omissions become visible, as when an author's reasons for not entering a new market leave out the effect on sales of the thing being rejected.

> **Key takeaway.** The way you formed a grouping (a process, a division, a classification or an argument) dictates its order, and forcing the order, with MECE checks, shows whether the group is complete and coherent.


### Chapter 7: Summarizing Grouped Ideas

The first rule of the pyramid says that an idea at any level must summarize the ideas grouped below it. When a group is deductive, the summary is easy: it leans on the final conclusion. When a group is inductive, the summary must state what the relationship below implies, and so summarizing a group is the act of completing the thinking. Most writers simply group ideas without completing the thought, and so, Minto says, they never quite say what they mean and never quite grasp the essence of their own thinking.

**Two kinds of ideas.** Every idea in business writing is either an action idea, which tells the reader to do something, or a situation idea, which tells him that something is the case. Each kind needs its own method. You summarize action ideas by stating the effect of carrying out the actions, and you summarize situation ideas by stating what their similarity implies. A summary that merely labels the group, such as "the company has two organization problems", is an intellectually blank assertion, because it promises an idea without stating one.

**Action ideas: state the end product.** Statements of actions are the most common ideas in business writing, and relating and summarizing them is, in Minto's words, the hardest thinking she knows. The difficulty is that actions all look alike, since each reads as "you should" or "we will" followed by a verb. You cannot tell whether one goes with another except in light of the effect you intend them to produce, but the effect depends on the actions you bring together. The way out is to word each action as an end product, visualizing what you will be holding when it is done. "Strengthen regional effectiveness" becomes "assign planning responsibility to the regions", and "improve financial reporting" becomes "install a system that gives early notice of change". A phrase like "develop a world consciousness" fails the test because no one could tell when it had been done. Wording as end products makes it possible to judge whether the steps are sufficient and nothing is missing. If the end product is a system for following up overdue accounts, you can see that a follow-up step is absent.

**Levels of abstraction and cause and effect.** In a long process there are many levels of cause and effect, and people mix them in one list. The remedy is to sort the steps into those that are being done and the purpose they serve. Labeling levels with words such as task, objective and benefit does not help, because actions cannot be classified by kind. Instead, group them by the effect they jointly achieve, state the effect in the summary and check that each action is necessary and the set is sufficient. The summary therefore says what the steps will accomplish, for example "rapidly transfer strategic planning know-how to the company", and it is stronger than a vague heading.

**Situation ideas: look for the similarity.** For ideas that describe the way things are, the process has three steps: find the structural similarity that ties the ideas together, look for closer links between the similarities, and then make the inductive leap to a new idea. Minto's example is a long list of complaints about an information system. She sorts them by the kind of problem each describes, notices that some say the information does not exist and others say it is wrong, and then asks what it means that the problems cluster in that way. The step of deciding what the similarity means is the thinking, and it is the part most often skipped. If you cannot make the inference, the grouping may be wrong, and you should check whether the ideas are from the same class or are a deductive argument masquerading as a list.

**Revealing the underlying process.** People often write lists of conclusions that allude to a process rather than state it. If the points are really stages of a cycle, say, perception of a need, strategy, implementation and assessment of reaction, saying so changes the list into a summary with a logical order. Spotting the process behind a list lets you complete it and see what is left over or missing.

> **Key takeaway.** Complete the thinking by summarizing each group: word actions as end products and state the effect they produce, and for situation ideas state what the similarity among them implies.


## Part Three: Logic in Problem Solving

Long documents such as reports, proposals and presentations are usually written to give the solution to a problem, or to say how you will find one. Here the process is less straightforward, because there has been a long stage of data gathering, and the writing may involve several authors. Part Three gives two tools for those cases.


### Chapter 8: Defining the Problem

Chapter 8 starts from the idea that a problem is a gap between the result you get now and the result you want. That gap did not arise in a vacuum: it came out of a particular situation, and understanding how it developed is the way to pinpoint it. Minto calls the tool she builds from this the Problem Definition Framework. It serves two purposes at once. It is the first step in solving the problem, and it is the first step in building the pyramid that communicates the solution.

**The framework.** A situation yields a result. When the writer or reader decides that a problem exists, they are perceiving a gap between the Undesired Result (R1) the situation now gives and the Desired Result (R2) they would rather have. The solution is whatever gets you from R1 to R2. The situation has these parts:

- **The Opening Scene (the starting point)** is a structure or process you can picture at a specific point in space and time, such as an organization chart, a sales operation, a computer configuration, a distribution system or a manufacturing process. Sketch it as you would for a friend to whom you are starting to tell the story.
- **The Disturbing Event** is what happened, or could happen, to disrupt that structure and trigger the undesired result. It may be external (a new competitor, a new technology, a change in government policy), internal (a new process, a new market, a system change) or recently recognized (a fact that was true all along but only now noticed).
- **R1 and R2** are stated as briefly as possible, for example a falling market share against a hoped-for growth rate.

![The opening scene, a disturbing event and the undesired result leading to the desired result, with the question of how to bridge the gap](/figures/pyramid-principle/problem-definition.svg "Redrawn: the elements of the problem definition framework.")

**Sequential analysis.** Defining the problem in this way begins a process of answering five questions in order: Is there, or is there likely to be, a problem or opportunity? Where does it lie? Why does it exist? What could we do about it? What should we do about it? The first two define the problem, the third structures the analysis and the last two lead to the solution. In a document, the answers to the first two become the introduction and the rest become the pyramid.

**Finding the reader's question.** The question the reader asks depends on how far along the solution the reader has already progressed. A common error is not specifying whether action has already been taken. Minto identifies seven standard cases, which range from the reader knowing the problem and wanting to know how to get from R1 to R2, through the reader having a solution and asking whether it is the right one or how to implement it, to a solution that has been tried and failed, alternatives to choose among, and a situation so new that the objectives themselves are unclear. In each case, moving from left to right and down through the framework gives the introduction: the last thing the reader already knows becomes the complication that triggers the question. Triple-layer problems can be handled the same way. A packaged-foods company that tested products on supermarket shelves, found the stores would not allow it, and then offered a fee, can be laid out layer by layer and explained in a few sentences.

**A worked example.** A retail distributor had three distribution centers plus rented space, meant to serve 490 stores, though they were strained to serve the present 438. With growth of 4 to 5 percent a year and a large number of new stores planned, the company expected to run out of capacity within two years. Laying this out in the framework gives an introduction that states the situation, the complication of the capacity gap and the question of which of three options (a new warehouse, upgraded material handling or continued reliance on third parties) makes sense. This structure matches the standard case of choosing among alternatives.

The chapter also warns about the idea of alternatives. Strictly speaking there is no such thing as an alternative solution to a problem, because either a recommendation gets the reader from R1 to R2 or it does not. Alternatives arise when R2 is ambiguously stated.

> **Key takeaway.** Define a problem as a gap between an undesired and a desired result arising from a specific situation and disturbing event, then use the framework to find the reader's question and write the introduction.


### Chapter 9: Structuring the Analysis of the Problem

Between defining a problem and finding the solution lies the analysis. It usually proceeds in a standard way: gather data, state the findings, draw conclusions and recommend actions. Minto's point in Chapter 9 is that you will do it far more efficiently if you structure the analysis before you gather the data, and that the same structure makes the final pyramid easy to build. Most people do the opposite and collect whatever data are available, postponing real thought until they have all the facts together. That works, but it produces extra work and lengthy documents that reflect the insight poorly.

![Two process flows: gathering data first and then drawing conclusions, versus defining the problem, structuring the analysis and gathering only targeted data](/figures/pyramid-principle/analysis-process.svg "Redrawn: the common approach to analysis beside the structured approach.")

**A short history.** In the early days of consulting, in the 1950s and 1960s, firms would start every engagement with a full analysis of the industry and the client: key success factors, the client's strengths and weaknesses and a comparison between them. The results were an overwhelming number of facts and exhibits from which it was hard to draw conclusions. One major firm estimated that about 60 percent of its fact-finding was wasted. Better firms now structure the analysis first, which resembles the scientific method: generate hypotheses about likely causes, plan an experiment or analysis that could rule some out and carry it out to get a clean result. They think up the likely reasons the problem exists, a technique explained in Appendix A, and focus data gathering on proving them right or wrong.

**Diagnostic frameworks.** The tool is the diagnostic framework, a MECE logic tree that visualizes what could be going on in the area where the problem occurred. Minto's small example is a headache. Causes divide into physical and mental, physical causes into external (a bump, allergies, weather) and internal (a cold, a tumor), and you assess the possibilities in the order in which they are easiest to eliminate, so you do not book a test for a tumor when you have a sinus headache. The same pattern applies to business. There are several kinds:

- **Structure and process trees** show the physical structure of an operation, such as the stages from awareness through purchase to repurchase, with the elements the company can influence.
- **Cause-and-effect trees** trace the elements that go into a result. A financial structure tree decomposes return on investment into margins and asset turnover, a task structure states each element as a managerial task and an activity structure traces the activities that produce an undesirable effect such as long installation times.
- **Classification trees** divide likely culprits by similarity, for example sales that are down because of semi-fixed factors or because of variable ones.
- **Choice structures** lay out the sequence of decisions a customer or company makes, such as a sequential marketing structure that follows a brand from positioning to awareness, trial and repurchase, with the order in which to analyze each element.

![A logic tree splitting falling profit into revenue and cost causes, then into narrower yes or no questions](/figures/pyramid-principle/diagnostic-tree.svg "Redrawn: a diagnostic logic tree for falling profit.")

**Yes or no questions.** The tree is not complete until its branches can be phrased as questions answered yes or no. Minto points out that the word issue properly means a question phrased so that it demands a yes or no answer, so "How should we reorganize?" is not an issue, while "Should we reorganize functionally?" is. She recommends calling a mere list of topics concerns. Framing yes or no questions lets you ask, for each, what you need to find out to answer it, which identifies the data, the source, the person responsible, the schedule and the cost before work begins. The consultant also knows in advance the relevance of every piece of data he collects.

**Applying the frameworks.** Which framework to use is implied by the opening scene of the problem definition, and how deep to go depends on knowledge of the field, since good problem solving cannot be done in the abstract. Decision trees and PERT diagrams show only the need for action, which makes them more useful for planning than for diagnosis. Logic trees can also generate possible solutions, such as breaking cost per cigarette into cost per hour and hours per cigarette and listing the ways each can fall, and can reveal flaws in a list of so-called key issues that mixes concerns, questions and process steps. A worked example about a manufacturer that was growing faster than it could fill orders shows a consultant choosing a flow diagram of the production planning system, guessing where weakness lies and writing the data questions accordingly.

> **Key takeaway.** Structure the analysis before gathering data by laying out MECE diagnostic frameworks and yes/no issues, so that the data you collect tests specific hypotheses and the findings fall into a pyramid.


## Part Four: Logic in Presentation

Once you have worked out the logic of the pyramid, you must arrange it so that the reader can see its divisions of thought, whether he is reading a page or watching a screen. The reader's eye sees the logic before his mind grasps it, so what the eye sees should reinforce what the mind receives.


### Chapter 10: Reflecting the Pyramid on the Page

Most documents are prose on a page read by one person sitting alone. Whether it is long or short, the reader should be able to see the major ideas as quickly as possible: ideally the whole thinking (the introduction, the main point and the key line) in the first thirty seconds, and then how each subordinate group relates. Chapter 10 describes the ways to show hierarchy on the page.

**Five formats.** For a long report you can reflect the structure with hierarchical headings, numbered and underlined points, decimal numbering, indented display or dot-dash outlines. Opinions differ about the first three, but all share the same job: a heading is a sign that tells the reader a new division of thought begins, and it should be placed further to the right the lower the idea in the pyramid. In practice:

- **Hierarchical headings** put major headings at the left margin, subsection headings below them and numbered paragraphs under those. Items at the same level take the same visual form.
- **Numbered and underlined points** state the whole of each support point and underline it, with lower-level points distinguished by form and indentation. The format gives speed and ease of reading but demands discipline: each point must answer the question raised by the one above and no more, with no graceful liaisons or amplification.
- **Decimal numbering** uses numbers such as 1, 1.1 and 1.1.1 to show levels, and should reflect the real divisions of thought, so you do not number paragraphs in introductions or linking comments.
- **Indented display** suits documents so short that neither headings nor decimals would help. Short, direct statements, one per level, are indented to show subordination, and progress reviews are often set out this way when the writer is present and wants to provoke discussion.
- **Highlighting** is enough for very short documents (fewer than two paragraphs per key line point), where simply underlining the points makes them jump out at the eye.

**Rules for headings.** Match headings to the hierarchy of ideas. Never use only one element at any level, since a single sub-item cannot be a division. State headings so that they reflect the idea and are parallel in form. Regard headings as outside the text: they are for the eye more than the mind, are not read carefully, and your first sentence under each must show that you are turning to a new topic, so the document reads smoothly without them. Introduce each group of headings by stating the major point it will explain, and never let a major heading come immediately after the title, or a subsection heading immediately after a section heading. The most important rule of all is not to overdo it: use headings only if they make the reader's job easier, and often nothing below the major sections is needed.

**Words that mislead.** Do not use section labels such as "Background" or "Introduction", which let the writer list information without making a point, and do not set out the structure with a list of topics ("Section 1 describes..."). The key line points should be ideas, so that the reader can take in the message from the opening alone. Minto also advises that if you formulate headings properly, they will form the table of contents as a precis of the report.

**Transitions.** Show transitions between groups. Where a long group of points ends and another begins, the reader's mind may not be where you want it, so a short bridging passage pulls it back. The trick is to relate what the sections say, not what they do, looking back to the last idea and forward to the next at once, often by carrying a key word or phrase across the boundary. This should be done gracefully, and only where needed. The chapter closes with next steps and conclusions: a closing summary is rarely required, but a section on next steps is useful when the reader must act soon.

> **Key takeaway.** Show the pyramid on the page with headings, numbering, underlining or indentation that match the real levels of the ideas, put the key line up front, and use transitions that link ideas rather than labels.


### Chapter 11: Reflecting the Pyramid on a Screen

Given a choice, most people would present their ideas orally. They assume that a visual presentation is a report in slide form: translate the pyramid into clean text slides, add a few exhibits and talk them through. Minto says the real situation is different. A live audience is seated in uncertain comfort, wishes to be elsewhere, responds unpredictably and is open to any distraction. A big part of the job is to anticipate that, keep their attention and make them eager to hear. In other words, you have to entertain, and a show needs a script, a storyboard, strong visuals and attention to timing and suspense.

**The usual failure.** The typical business slide is a long list of guiding principles or findings, set in text, that the speaker reads aloud. This is what slide-design specialist Gene Zelazny calls a visual recitation, not a presentation, and authors defend it with "this way we don't forget anything" and "we'll hand out a copy". Minto recommends a minimum standard instead: text slides that contain only the most significant ideas, properly grouped and summarized and stated as briefly as possible, supported by clear exhibits (charts, tables or diagrams), and reflecting the pyramid.

**Designing text slides.** Slides should follow the pyramid roughly, with the situation and complication points first, then the main point and key line points, then each key line point with its support. The guidelines she gives are:

1. Present and support one idea at a time, except to list points that later slides will develop.
2. Use statements, not captions, so the audience cannot make a wrong assumption: "Sales outlook is favorable", not "Sales outlook".
3. Keep the text brief, at most about six lines or thirty words.
4. Use simple words and numbers: $4.9 million is easier than a precise figure.
5. Make the type readable from the back of the room.
6. Make slides visually interesting, for example by turning a list into a diagram of linked circles and boxes.
7. Use "build" slides that reveal parts one by one, to heighten interest or deal with complexity.

The slide is a visual aid, and its function is to keep the presentation moving. Make a clear distinction between what you say aloud and what you show on the screen, because the script carries the detail that the slide only summarizes.

**Designing exhibit slides.** Text slides use words, but exhibits (charts, graphs, tables and diagrams) communicate through visual relationships, and they let you present masses of data without words. They should convey their message as simply and readably as possible, because the viewer has no chance to study them and a cluttered chart wastes time explaining. You rarely want more than one or two complex diagrams. Choose the chart type for the relationship you want to show: how amounts compare to the whole, to each other or over time; what has changed; how items are distributed; and how items correlate. Above all, give each chart a title that states its message as a sentence with a verb, such as "Western Region accounts for almost half the profits", not "Share of profits by region", which leaves viewers to focus on whatever they like.

**Storyboarding.** The method for moving from the pyramid to a presentation has several steps. First write the introduction in full, every word in order. Take a blank storyboard and write across the top of each blank slide the points from the introduction, the key line and one level below that you want to illustrate. Rough out how you will show each point visually, without real numbers at first. Then check that the exhibits support the points, write the script, and rehearse. Minto also points readers to Antony Jay's book on effective presentation for the details of staging, delivery and audience handling, and she quotes his remark that a presentation is a favour done by those who attend to those who present.

> **Key takeaway.** A screen presentation is a show, not a report: keep slides to the main points as brief statements, use charts titled with their message, and storyboard from the pyramid so what is said and what is shown reinforce each other.


### Chapter 12: Reflecting the Pyramid in Prose

The last chapter returns to the individual sentence. Minto reminds readers that she said at the start that writing clearly consists of two steps: decide the point you want to make, then put it into words. Once the pyramid is built and its groupings checked, you know exactly the points and their order, and only the words remain. In theory that should be easy, since one ought to be able to turn pyramided points into concise, graceful sentences. In practice the average sentence in business is long-winded and heavy with jargon, which makes paragraphs hard to read.

**Bad prose from bright people.** Minto quotes several passages produced by able, articulate people who can explain the same ideas out loud perfectly well. In writing, they seem to believe that a drier style and more technical vocabulary earns more respect. She disagrees: good ideas should not be dressed up in bad prose, and technical subjects can be treated with clarity, as many famous scientists and philosophers have shown. A reader's mind has limited capacity, so every unnecessary effort in decoding the words is capacity taken from understanding.

**Why images matter.** The key to lucid prose, she argues, is the image. When a reader takes in well-written prose, he builds a picture in his mind as he goes, phrase by phrase, and the picture is what he remembers. A passage from a classic nature narrative shows how easily images arise: a few concrete nouns, a place, an action, and the reader can see it. If you close the book and try to recall the passage, you cannot repeat it verbatim, but you can read it off the image, and memory studies show that images improve recall. Abstract passages are harder to visualize, and a reader may substitute a vague picture or none.

**Finding the image in bad prose.** To rewrite a muddy sentence, find the nouns and look for relationships among them, and then draw them as an image. In a sentence about improving the cost-effectiveness of field sales-force deployment to reflect redefined selling missions, the concrete nouns are the sales force, the store and the changed trading environment. Sketching how they relate shows that the real message is that the sales force must be redeployed to match the new trading environment. Another passage about contingency plans and prioritized guides turns out to say: set out the order in which activities will be curtailed if the plan needs adjusting. A third, about cash flow analyses and information, reveals a system into which the right information must be inserted to give accurate analyses. The process is to find the image, check that it makes sense and write it plainly. If you cannot draw it, the author may not have had a clear idea, which means the problem is the thinking again.

**The payoff.** Forcing yourself to visualize the relationships inherent in your ideas lets you translate them straight into a clear sentence that the reader can interpret in one step and store as an image. It also moves you from knowing words to transferring knowledge in large chunks, which is more efficient for the reader's mind and makes the message easier to recall. Writing clearly, in the end, is nothing more than seeing clearly and saying what you see.

> **Key takeaway.** Write lucid prose by visualizing the relationships among your ideas, finding the concrete nouns that carry the image and stating it plainly, so the reader can store the idea as a picture.


## Appendices

Two appendices follow the chapters. They are summarized here in book order, as part of the twelve chapters' material rather than as separate numbered chapters.

### Appendix A: Problem Solving in Structureless Situations

Chapter 8 treated problem solving as a logical process of finding and displaying the structure behind an undesired result, on the theory that the solution always lies in changing that structure. Appendix A deals with a second kind of problem, in which you do not dislike the result but cannot explain it. There are three reasons you might be unable to explain it: the structure does not yet exist, as when someone is inventing a telephone or an underwater tunnel; the structure is invisible, as in the brain or in DNA, so you have only results to work from; or the structure exists but is unknown. This is the territory of scientific and creative problem solving.

Minto uses the philosopher C. S. Peirce's analysis of reasoning. Any reasoning process involves three things: a rule (a belief about how the world is structured), a case (an observed fact) and a result (an expected occurrence, given the rule in this case). **Deduction** starts with a rule and a case and predicts the result. **Induction** starts with a case and a result and infers a rule. **Abduction** starts with a result and a rule and proposes a case, and in scientific problem solving it is the step in which the investigator guesses at the missing structure. The form you are using depends on where you start in the process, and in any complex problem you rotate among all three. The difference between analytical and scientific problem solving is that in analytical work you know the structure that creates the result, while the scientist does not and must hypothesize it. Examples include Galileo testing hypotheses about the force on a falling body by rolling balls down an inclined plane, and Alexander Graham Bell's analogy between the delicate human ear and a thicker membrane that might move a piece of steel, which led to the telephone. How one sees such analogies cannot be spelled out as a recipe. A comparison table sets out the basic five-question process for both kinds of problem, with analytical and scientific steps side by side.

### Appendix B: Introductory Structures

Appendix B collects the standard introductory structures and shows them in full text. Most introductions fall into the same few patterns because they answer one of a small number of questions: what should we do, should we do what we plan to do, how do or did we do something, and why did something happen. A chart sets out the most common structure for each, with the situation, complication and question in outline, and the appendix then expands the examples from Chapter 8 into real introductions.

The patterns include how to solve a problem, how to get a desired result, choosing among alternatives, an audit that asks whether changes are needed, and recommending a change in a practice the reader has not questioned. Consulting documents have their own forms. In a letter of proposal, the introduction may state the problem and ask why the reader should hire you, in which case the document is structured around reasons: you understand the problem, you know how to approach it and your timing, staffing and fees make sense. Where the client is well known or the proposal is a formality, the problem is described in the introduction and the body is organized around the approach. Minto discourages the common template of headings (introduction, background, objectives, scope, issues, approach, work plan, benefits), because the headings encourage lists that overlap and obscure the thinking, and the first group of headings is really the definition of the problem while the second is the steps for solving it.

The appendix ends with extended examples of documents whose process is described badly. When the reader knows both the old process and the new one, the introduction can simply say how the new one differs. When the reader knows little, the temptation is to describe how the system works now and everything wrong with it before reaching the recommendation, but the rule that the introduction can include only what the reader will accept as true still applies. A document about a document-delivery system and one about producing graph books for a finance department are used to compare an under-described process, which leaves the reader unable to see the problem, with an over-described one, which buries it. The cure for both is to visualize the process before and after, lay out the differences and use those to set the key line.

> **Key takeaway.** Appendix A extends the method to problems with no known structure by adding abduction to deduction and induction, and Appendix B supplies model introductions for the standard questions readers ask.


## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The first theme is that structure is thinking. Minto treats the pyramid as a test of the quality of the ideas, not just a layout: if a point cannot summarize the group below it, or the group contains unlike ideas or a sequence with a gap, the thinking is incomplete (Chapters 1, 6 and 7). Writing is the place where this shows. The second theme is the reader. The introduction exists to mirror a question already in the reader's mind (Chapters 2 and 4), the key line is induction because it is easier on the reader (Chapter 5), and the headings, slides and prose all serve to let the eye and mind take in the logic quickly (Chapters 10 to 12).

The third theme is logic with a short list of tools. Only four orders exist (Chapter 6), only two kinds of reasoning (Chapter 5) and a small set of introductory questions (Chapter 4 and Appendix B). That economy makes the method teachable. The fourth theme is that problem solving and communication are the same process. The Problem Definition Framework and the diagnostic frameworks (Chapters 8 and 9) turn into the introduction and the pyramid with little extra work, so the thinking done to solve a problem is the structure you later present. Wording actions as end products (Chapter 7) is the fifth, and a particularly practical, theme, because it exposes missing steps.

### Strengths and limits

The strengths are rigor and teachability. The three rules and the introductory flow can be checked in advance and applied to any document, and the book is full of worked examples where a muddled list is turned into a clear point. MECE has become a standard working vocabulary in consulting, and the habit of answering first and supporting afterwards is useful far beyond business writing.

The limits come from the book's consulting origins and its date. The examples are drawn from memos, proposals and reports in a corporate world of slides on overhead projectors, and the advice on presentation is written for an earlier technology. The method assumes that the reader has a question that the writer can identify, which may not hold for a mixed or hostile audience or for narrative writing, where a pyramid is a poor fit. Minto's claim that the mind can perform only four analytical activities is stated more confidently than it is supported. The edition summarized here is a page-image scan, so the exact wording of some chapter and part titles is inferred from the book's cross-references.

*Where I'd push back:* the insistence on inductive key lines is a sound default for busy readers, but it can hide a real argument. When the reasoning is the point, as when you reject the question the reader asked, the deductive form that Minto allows as an exception will often be what the reader needs most.

### Applying it

1. **Start with the question and the answer** (Chapters 2 and 3). Write the subject, the question the reader would ask and your answer before you draft anything else.
2. **Write the introduction as a story** (Chapter 4). Put only agreed facts in the situation, add the complication and check that the question follows.
3. **Test each group** (Chapters 5 to 7). Ask whether its members share a plural noun, whether they form a MECE set in a logical order and whether a summary above them says something new.
4. **Define the problem before you analyze** (Chapters 8 and 9). State the opening scene, disturbing event, undesired result and desired result, and build a diagnostic tree of yes or no questions.
5. **Show the logic** (Chapters 10 to 12). Use headings and transitions that mirror the pyramid, brief statement slides with titled charts and prose that you can draw as an image.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 1 to 4 for the core method, Chapter 7 for wording actions, and Chapter 8 if you write about problems and solutions.
