## Overview

*Deep Work* is Cal Newport's argument that the ability to concentrate without distraction on cognitively demanding tasks is becoming simultaneously rarer and more valuable, and that building it is one of the best bets a knowledge worker can make. Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown, defines deep work as professional activity performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes your cognitive abilities to their limit. It creates new value, improves your skill and is hard to replicate. Shallow work, by contrast, is logistical, noncognitively demanding and often done while distracted, such as e-mail, status meetings and social media. His thesis is that the first is thriving in the economy's rewards while the second fills most people's days.

The book has two parts. Part 1, "The Idea," makes the case across three chapters: deep work is valuable because it lets you learn hard things quickly and produce at an elite level, it is rare because of forces like the metric black hole, the principle of least resistance, busyness as a proxy for productivity and the cult of the Internet, and it is meaningful because neuroscience, psychology and philosophy all connect focused attention to a good life. Part 2, "The Rules," offers four rules for turning the idea into practice: work deeply, embrace boredom, quit social media, and drain the shallows. Each rule bundles several strategies, backed by stories about Carl Jung, Neal Stephenson, Adam Grant, J.K. Rowling, Teddy Roosevelt and Radhika Nagpal.

To keep the structure of the book visible, the summaries below treat Part 1's three chapters as Chapters 1 to 3 and the four rules as Chapters 4 to 7. The introduction and conclusion are summarized as short sections of their own.

Three ideas hold it together.

1. **Depth is a skill that wins.** Learning hard things fast and producing at an elite level both depend on undistracted concentration, and organizations undervalue it, which leaves an opening for individuals.
2. **Willpower is finite, so build structure.** Routines, rituals, scheduled distraction and fixed endpoints beat good intentions, because the urge to switch away is constant.
3. **Concentration can be trained and protected.** Practice focusing, treat network tools with a craftsman's skepticism, and cap shallow work so deep hours get the best of your day.

## Introduction

Newport begins with Carl Jung, who in 1922 started building a stone retreat called the Tower in Bollingen, Switzerland. Jung kept a private office there to which only he held the key, wrote undisturbed for two hours each morning, and spent afternoons meditating or walking, with no electricity. It was not a vacation home. Jung, who had just published *Psychological Types* and broken with Freud, built it to think more deeply than his busy Zurich life allowed, even at the cost of seeing patients. Newport defines **deep work** as professional activity performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes your cognitive abilities to their limit, creating new value, improving your skill, and being hard to replicate. The opposite is **shallow work**: noncognitively demanding, logistical tasks, often done while distracted, that create little new value and are easy to replicate.

He lists others who made deep work central, including Montaigne in his château tower, Mark Twain in a shed at Quarry Farm, Woody Allen writing forty-four films on a manual typewriter, J.K. Rowling avoiding social media, Bill Gates with his twice-yearly "Think Weeks," and Neal Stephenson, who is nearly impossible to reach electronically. Most modern knowledge workers have lost this habit because of network tools such as email, social media and infotainment sites. A 2012 McKinsey study found that the average knowledge worker spends more than 60 percent of the workweek on electronic communication and internet searching, close to 30 percent on email alone. Workers are busy but replacing depth with shallowness, and Newport cites Nicholas Carr's *The Shallows* to suggest that spending enough time in a shallow state can reduce your capacity for depth. He says he will not argue this point or the larger social debate; his interest is the personal and economic opportunity for the few who resist.

The case study is Jason Benn, a financial consultant who automated most of his job with Excel macros, realized his skills were replaceable, and decided to become a programmer without knowing how to code. He had trouble focusing, once spending 98 percent of a day surfing the web instead of writing a business plan. So he locked himself in a room with textbooks, notecards and a highlighter, built up to five or more disconnected hours a day, and read about eighteen books. Two months later he entered the Dev Bootcamp, where only half the students graduated on time, and finished as top student. He moved from a $40,000 consulting salary to a $100,000 developer job at a funded start-up, and now works about four hours of focus before the first meeting and another three or four in the afternoon.

Deep work is valuable for two reasons. First, in an information economy with rapidly changing complex systems, you must learn hard things quickly, and that requires depth. Second, digital networks make rewards for excellent work enormous and for mediocre work small, because audiences can find better alternatives online. This leads to the **Deep Work Hypothesis**: the ability to do deep work is becoming increasingly rare at exactly the time it is becoming increasingly valuable, so the few who cultivate it and make it the core of their working life will thrive.

The book has two parts. Part 1 argues that the hypothesis is true, and Part 2 teaches four rules to train your brain and transform your habits. Newport describes his own practice: a theoretical computer scientist trained at MIT, he has never had a social media account, rarely works past five or six p.m., and structures his day around three to four hours of uninterrupted work with shallow tasks batched at the edges. In ten years he published four books, earned a PhD, and became a tenure-track professor at Georgetown. He closes with Jung's example and the claim that "a deep life is a good life."

> **Key takeaway.** The ability to concentrate without distraction is becoming rarer and more valuable at the same time, so building it into your working life is a competitive advantage.

## Part 1: The Idea

### Chapter 1: Deep Work Is Valuable

Newport opens with three winners of the digital economy. Nate Silver's blog drew over 70 percent of New York Times traffic before the 2012 election. David Heinemeier Hansson created Ruby on Rails and works from Spain and Malibu. John Doerr, a venture capitalist, funded Google, Amazon and Twitter and is worth over $3 billion. He turns to MIT economists Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee, whose book *Race Against the Machine* describes a "Great Restructuring" in which technology divides jobs instead of eliminating them, and which Tyler Cowen's *Average Is Over* echoes. Three groups benefit. The **high-skilled workers** (Silver) work creatively with intelligent machines. The **superstars** (Hansson) are the best in their fields, and Sherwin Rosen's 1981 work on winner-take-all markets shows that talent cannot be bought in bulk, so when clients can reach anyone, the best take most of the market. The **owners** (Doerr) hold capital, and a firm like Instagram sold for a billion dollars with only thirteen employees. Newport can't help with the third, but the first two are accessible.

To join them, he says you need two abilities: to quickly master hard things, and to produce at an elite level in quality and speed. Intelligent machines are complex (Silver uses SQL databases and the statistics package Stata), and they keep changing, so learning never ends. Producing matters too: Hansson turned his skill into Ruby on Rails, and Silver turned data skill into election forecasting. Both abilities, he argues, depend on deep work.

On learning, he cites Antonin-Dalmace Sertillanges, a Dominican friar who wrote *The Intellectual Life* in the early twentieth century and said the mind must become a lens that focuses attention. K. Anders Ericsson's research on **deliberate practice** later confirmed this: improvement requires attention focused tightly on a specific skill, with feedback, and "diffused attention is almost antithetical" to it. Neuroscience adds that skills reduce to brain circuits, and focused repetition triggers cells called oligodendrocytes to wrap neurons in **myelin**, which lets circuits fire faster and cleaner. Learning a complex skill while distracted fires too many circuits at once to strengthen the right one, so to learn hard things quickly you must focus intensely without distraction.

On producing, he profiles Adam Grant, the youngest tenured and then youngest full professor at Wharton, who published seven major-journal articles in 2012 and a bestselling book in 2013. Grant batches his teaching into the fall and his research into spring and summer, and within research periods isolates himself for days with an auto-responder on his email. Newport states the law: **High-Quality Work Produced = (Time Spent) × (Intensity of Focus)**. He recalls that the straight-A students he interviewed studied less than the next group down but concentrated harder. Sophie Leroy's research on **attention residue** explains why: when you switch from Task A to B, part of your attention stays stuck on A, especially if A was unfinished, and performance on B suffers. Even a quick inbox check leaves residue. To produce at your peak, you need long periods of full concentration on one task.

The chapter closes by addressing "What about Jack Dorsey?" Dorsey, who co-founded Twitter and Square, thrives with a fractured schedule of thirty to forty meeting notes a day. Newport says top executives are hard-to-automate decision engines for whom distraction is part of the job, and the lesson does not carry over to other roles. A rule of specificity applies: don't extend such counterexamples to your own job without evidence. In one study, consultants forced to disconnect found connectivity mattered less than they assumed, and managers using Scrum-style structured meetings had more time to think. Depth is not the only valuable skill, but the niches where distraction pays are increasingly rare.

> **Key takeaway.** Learning hard things quickly and producing at an elite level are the skills that win in the new economy, and both depend on deep, undistracted concentration.

### Chapter 2: Deep Work Is Rare

Having argued that depth is valuable, Newport notes a paradox: organizations are not promoting it, and many fashionable trends actively undermine it. Facebook's new headquarters is built around "the largest open floor plan in the world," and Jack Dorsey seats Square's developers at long shared desks to encourage serendipity. IBM sends 2.5 million instant messages a day, and start-ups like Hall push "real-time collaboration," with the most "efficient" developers wired to jump into chat with practiced keystrokes. The New York Times urges its journalists to tweet, and when Jonathan Franzen called Twitter a "coercive development" for novelists, he was ridiculed as out of touch. Yet each trend carries a cost to focus. Open offices bring "massive distraction," according to a neuroscientist who tested them for a British TV special. Gloria Mark's research shows that even short interruptions delay task completion by a significant fraction. George Packer, who avoids Twitter, wrote *The Unwinding* and won the National Book Award. Newport sets out to explain why a valuable habit is so rare, and concludes the reasons are flimsy.

**The metric black hole.** Atlantic Media's CTO Tom Cochran noticed that he handled 511 e-mails received and 284 sent in a week, so he built a model from company-wide e-mail counts, word counts, typing and reading speeds and salaries. He found the company spent over a million dollars a year paying people to process e-mail, about ninety-five cents of labor per message, a "free and frictionless" tool with the "soft costs" of a small corporate jet. The lesson is how hard that measurement was. As knowledge work becomes more complex, individual contributions are harder to measure, as Thomas Piketty's analysis of executive pay assumes. Newport calls this opaque region the **metric black hole**: the bottom-line impact of depth, and of distraction, can't easily be seen, so behaviors escape scrutiny.

**The principle of least resistance.** Leslie Perlow found professionals spending twenty to twenty-five hours a week outside the office monitoring e-mail, expecting to answer within an hour. She persuaded the Boston Consulting Group to make one team take a day a week completely off. The team resisted, and the partner feared telling the client. But the consultants enjoyed work more, communicated better, learned more and delivered a better product. Why does connectivity persist? Newport's answer: *without clear feedback on the impact of behaviors on the bottom line, we tend toward whatever is easiest in the moment.* Being instantly answerable makes your own life easier, and running your day from your inbox spares you the hard planning that David Allen's fifteen-step flowchart demands. Standing meetings and "Thoughts?" forwards persist for the same reason: they are quick for the sender, whatever they cost everyone else.

**Busyness as a proxy for productivity.** Professors have a clear metric, the h-index (Newport reports being a 21), so Richard Feynman could declare himself "irresponsible" to dodge committees and protect the long stretches physics needs. Most knowledge workers lack that clarity, and Matthew Crawford left a think tank to repair motorcycles for the concrete satisfaction of a bike driving out of the shop. In the absence of indicators, workers fall back on the industrial standard of Frederick Taylor's stopwatch: visibly doing lots of stuff. Fast e-mail replies, constant meetings and instant-message responses all look busy. Yahoo's Marissa Mayer banned remote work after seeing that VPN logs showed too few log-ins, which is busyness-as-proxy applied to a whole company. Yet Adam Grant, shutting himself off to write, would look unproductive under such a rule while producing enormous value.

**The cult of the Internet.** Alissa Rubin, the Times's Paris bureau chief, who reported from Kabul and wrote hard-hitting stories about French complicity in the Rwandan genocide, is nudged to post occasional tweets. Why does this seem normal? Newport turns to Neil Postman's warning about **technopoly**, a culture in which high-tech is assumed to be good, and alternatives are not made illegal or unpopular but "invisible and therefore irrelevant." Evgeny Morozov updates the idea, describing "the Internet" as today's uber-ideology. Franzen's ridicule, and a refrigerated-trucking fleet carrying a "like us on Facebook" graphic, show the mind-set at work. Deep work, which rests on old-fashioned values of craftsmanship and quality, suffers in such a culture, and not for any empirical reason.

**Bad for business, good for you.** Depth is hard, shallow work is easy, busyness is self-preserving, and Internet-centrism is unquestioned, and all of this is hidden by the metric black hole. That is bad for companies but good for you as an individual: if the trends continue, depth will become both rarer and more valuable.

> **Key takeaway.** Deep work is rare not because it lacks value but because immeasurable costs, the pull of least resistance, busyness-as-productivity and faith in everything Internet crowd it out, which leaves a lasting advantage for those who protect it.

### Chapter 3: Deep Work Is Meaningful

The final chapter of Part 1 turns from economics to meaning. It opens with Ric Furrer, a blacksmith in rural Wisconsin who re-creates medieval metalwork by hand. A PBS documentary follows him as he smelts crucible steel and spends eight hours of controlled hammering to shape a Viking-era sword, where a slip in concentration could ruin dozens of hours of work. "I don't need a sword," he says. "But I have to make them." Newport observes that nobody doubts a craftsman's work is meaningful, but for knowledge workers the link between depth and meaning is muddied. The work is ambiguous, and a chorus of voices urges more shallow, Internet-flavored busyness. Nobody faults Furrer for skipping Facebook, but a knowledge worker who does so is thought eccentric. He promises three arguments, moving from narrow to broad: neurological, psychological and philosophical.

**The neurological argument.** After a grim cancer diagnosis, science writer Winifred Gallagher resolved to focus on her life rather than the disease, on movies, walks and a "6:30 martini," and found this worked. Her book *Rapt* concludes that "who you are, what you think, feel, and do, what you love—is the sum of what you focus on." Circumstances matter less than what you attend to. Barbara Fredrickson's research shows that choosing where to focus after a setback acts as an emotional "reset button," and Laura Carstensen's fMRI study found older adults' amygdalae respond only to positive imagery, evidence that they had trained their attention. By this logic, a life of deep work builds a mind that sees its world as rich and important, and it also crowds out the trivial. Newport audits his last five e-mails: a domain scam, a family chat, retirement advice, scheduling, and office politics, all petty, stressful or irritating. A day spent in the inbox builds a world of stress and triviality, and an idle mind, Gallagher warns, fixes on what could be wrong.

**The psychological argument.** Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi and Reed Larson developed the experience sampling method, in which subjects carrying pagers recorded what they were doing and feeling whenever beeped. The results supported his theory of **flow**: "The best moments usually occur when a person's body or mind is stretched to its limits in a voluntary effort to accomplish something difficult and worthwhile." Contrary to common belief, people were happier at work than in leisure, because jobs have built-in goals, feedback and challenge, while free time is unstructured. Where Gallagher stresses the content of attention, flow is agnostic about content: the feeling of going deep is rewarding in itself. Deep work is well suited to produce flow, so building your working life around it is a proven route to satisfaction. Csikszentmihalyi suggests redesigning jobs to resemble flow activities, but since that is hard, he says individuals should seek flow out.

**The philosophical argument.** Hubert Dreyfus and Sean Dorrance Kelly, in *All Things Shining*, trace how the world lost its "sacred, shining things." Descartes's turn to the autonomous individual freed people politically but left meaning arbitrary and invited nihilism. Their answer is craftsmanship: a wheelwright respects the "subtle virtues" of each piece of wood, so the craftsman's task "is not to generate meaning, but rather to cultivate in himself the skill of discerning the meanings that are already there." Newport insists this is not limited to manual trades. Programmer Santiago Gonzalez compares beautiful code to a poem, and *The Pragmatic Programmer* quotes the quarry worker's creed, "We who cut mere stones must always be envisioning cathedrals." Writers, marketers, consultants and lawyers can do the same. You don't need a rarified job but "a rarified approach to your work," and since developing craft is deep work, depth is the way in.

**Homo sapiens deepensis.** The first two chapters were pragmatic, but a demanding program needs resonance, and these three paths all lead to the same place: our species flourishes in depth and wallows in shallowness. As Gallagher put it, "I'll live the focused life, because it's the best kind there is."

> **Key takeaway.** Neuroscience, psychology and philosophy agree that a life organized around deep, skilled attention is not just more productive but more meaningful and satisfying.

## Part 2: The Rules

### Chapter 4: Rule #1: Work Deeply

Newport opens Part 2 with David Dewane's Eudaimonia Machine, an architecture professor's design for a building devoted to deep work. Its five rooms run in a line with no bypass corridor: a gallery of past deep work to inspire, a salon for coffee and debate, a library, a low-intensity office, and finally soundproofed "deep work chambers" for ninety-minute sessions. It exists only as drawings, but it shows what an environment built for depth looks like. Since most of us work in distracting open offices, the rule offers strategies to simulate it.

**Why good intentions fail.** A study by Wilhelm Hofmann and Roy Baumeister gave 205 adults beepers and gathered over 7,500 samples of what they were craving. Desire "turned out to be the norm, not the exception," and the urge to check e-mail, browse or watch television was resisted only about half the time. Baumeister's earlier work shows that willpower is finite and depletes with use, like a muscle. So the key to a deep work habit is to build routines and rituals that minimize the willpower needed to start and sustain concentration.

**Decide on your depth philosophy.** Donald Knuth and doctoral student Brian Chappell both value depth but integrate it differently, and a mismatch can derail the habit. Newport offers four philosophies:

- **Monastic.** Eliminate or radically minimize shallow obligations. Knuth has had no e-mail address since 1990, with a postal address handled by an assistant and batch replies every three months. Neal Stephenson's policy is "All of my time and attention are spoken for," and he argues that fragmenting his time collapses his output as a novelist. It suits people whose value is a clear, discrete output, which limits who can use it.
- **Bimodal.** Divide time into clearly defined deep stretches, ideally a full day or more, and an open remainder. Carl Jung retreated to his Bollingen tower while keeping a busy Zurich life; Adam Grant stacks his teaching into one semester and goes fully monastic for two to four days at a time. People usually respect inaccessibility if it is well defined and advertised, as Perlow's consultants found.
- **Rhythmic.** Turn depth into a simple regular habit so no energy goes on deciding when. Jerry Seinfeld's "don't break the chain" calendar is the model. Chappell wakes at five-thirty (later four forty-five) to write until seven-thirty, producing four to five pages a day and a thesis chapter every two or three weeks. It reaches less intensity than bimodal but often logs more total hours, and it fits standard office jobs.
- **Journalistic.** Fit deep work into any free moment, as Walter Isaacson did when he wrote *The Wise Men* in short sessions during a shared beach rental. It requires the ability to switch quickly and confidence in the work's value, so it is not for novices. Newport admits it is his own main approach, though he plans each week in advance.

**Ritualize.** Robert Caro governs every inch of his office by rules, and Charles Darwin kept a strict daily structure, in line with Mason Currey's advice to ignore inspiration. David Brooks puts it as great minds "think like artists but work like accountants." A ritual should answer three questions: where you'll work and for how long, how you'll work once you start (rules such as no Internet or a words-per-interval metric), and how you'll support the work (coffee, food, a walk). Experiment until something sticks.

**Make grand gestures.** A radical change of environment, perhaps with significant money or effort, raises the perceived importance of the task and cuts procrastination. J.K. Rowling finished *Deathly Hallows* in a suite at Edinburgh's Balmoral Hotel. Bill Gates took "Think Weeks" in a cabin, Alan Lightman retreats to a Maine island, Dan Pink and Michael Pollan built writing cabins, William Shockley locked himself in a Chicago hotel room to improve on the transistor, and Peter Shankman flew round-trip to Tokyo in business class to finish a manuscript in thirty hours. The commitment unlocks the mental resources: "Sometimes to go deep, you must first go big."

**Don't work alone.** Open offices rest on the theory of serendipitous creativity, supported by MIT's Building 20 and Bell Labs, whose long hallways forced scientists to run into each other. But both used private offices (MIT professors even demanded soundproofing gaskets in the Stata Center), a **hub-and-spoke** model: meet ideas in hubs, then work deeply in spokes. Collaboration can also deepen the work itself. The transistor came from Brattain and Bardeen working side by side, the **whiteboard effect**, in which another person waiting for your insight can push you past the instinct to avoid depth. Optimize hubs and spokes separately rather than mixing them into a sludge.

**Execute like a business.** Andy Grove told Clayton Christensen that he knew what he should do but not how. Newport adapts the four disciplines of the book *The 4 Disciplines of Execution* to his own deep work: (1) **focus on the wildly important**, with a few ambitious goals (his was five peer-reviewed papers in a year); (2) **act on lead measures**, tracking hours spent in deep work rather than lagging results such as papers published; (3) **keep a compelling scoreboard**, a card on the wall with tally marks, circling the hours that produced milestones; (4) **create a cadence of accountability** through a weekly review. The result was nine accepted papers, more than double his previous best, mostly by making his depth regular rather than clustered near deadlines.

**Be lazy.** Tim Kreider fled to an "undisclosed location" because idleness is "as indispensable to the brain as vitamin D." Newport's version is a hard shutdown at the end of the workday, for three reasons. Downtime aids insights, since Ap Dijksterhuis's unconscious thought theory finds the unconscious sifts complex, vague problems better than the conscious mind. It restores the finite directed attention that attention restoration theory describes, so a walk in nature beat a city walk by up to 20 percent on a concentration task. And the work it displaces is of low value, because Anders Ericsson found that even experts manage at most about four hours of deliberate effort a day (elite violinists averaged three and a half). Because of the Zeigarnik effect, unfinished tasks nag, but Baumeister and Masicampo's "Consider It Done!" study shows that making a plan frees the mind. So Newport ends each day with a shutdown ritual: a last inbox check, moving tasks to lists, skimming lists and calendar, sketching tomorrow's plan, and saying "Shutdown complete."

> **Key takeaway.** Because willpower is finite, going deep depends on choosing a scheduling philosophy, building rituals and grand gestures, using collaboration wisely, tracking lead measures, and shutting work off completely each evening.

### Chapter 5: Rule #2: Embrace Boredom

Rule #2 begins at six a.m. in a synagogue in Spring Valley, New York, where Adam Marlin and others study the Talmud, attempting to decipher a page a day, often with a study partner. Marlin, a businessman who came to the practice in his twenties, calls it "the hardest brain strain I do." It stretches the mind "to the reaches of your mental capacity," and colleagues with far less formal schooling could "dance intellectual circles" around his three Ivy League degrees. Years of daily practice, he believes, have built the mental muscle behind his creative insights at work. The lesson is that the ability to concentrate is a skill that must be trained, not a flossing-style habit you can start overnight with enough motivation.

There is a corollary: training fails if you spend the rest of your time fleeing boredom. Stanford's Clifford Nass found that heavy multitaskers "can't filter out irrelevancy," are "chronically distracted," and are "pretty much mental wrecks." Worse, they believe they can turn on laser focus when needed, but their habits of mind have made that impossible. If every idle minute, such as waiting in line, is filled by a glance at a phone, your brain isn't ready for deep work, however much you schedule. So the strategies here train two things: the ability to concentrate intensely and the ability to resist distraction.

**Don't take breaks from distraction. Instead take breaks from focus.** The popular Internet Sabbath, made famous by William Powers's *Hamlet's BlackBerry*, cannot rewire a distracted brain any more than eating healthily one day a week causes weight loss. Newport proposes the reverse: schedule in advance when you'll use the Internet, and stay off it completely outside those blocks, keeping a notepad near the computer with the next permitted time. It is the constant switching to high-stimulus activity at the first hint of boredom that teaches the mind to refuse boredom, and the wait before the next block becomes "concentration calisthenics." Three points matter. First, it works even if your job demands heavy connectivity, since what counts is the integrity of the offline blocks, as in a two-hour stretch with e-mail checks every fifteen minutes that still leaves about ninety minutes offline. Second, hold the line: if you get stuck, switch tasks or move the next Internet block earlier, but keep at least a five-minute gap, which separates the urge from its reward. Third, apply the same scheduling at home, with exceptions for time-sensitive texts and directions, and be willing to simply wait and be bored in a queue.

**Work like Teddy Roosevelt.** As a Harvard freshman in 1876–77, Roosevelt had an "amazing array of interests" (boxing, dancing, taxidermy, and a published bird book), and he studied no more than about a quarter of the day. But he used the fragments left between classes and exercise with "blistering intensity," and Edmund Morris notes that his concentration was so intense and his reading so rapid that he "could afford more time off." The strategy: pick a high-priority deep task, estimate how long it would normally take, and set a hard deadline that drastically shortens that time, announcing it publicly or using a visible countdown timer. Then work with no breaks of any kind. Do this about once a week at first, keeping deadlines at the edge of feasibility. Roosevelt dashes work like interval training for attention, and they leave no room for distraction.

**Meditate productively.** During his postdoc at MIT, Newport walked and ran between his Beacon Hill apartment and Cambridge. He used that time to focus on one well-defined professional problem, such as outlining a chapter or advancing a proof, and kept returning his attention when it wandered, as in mindfulness meditation. Aim for two or three sessions a week in otherwise idle time such as walks, drives or showers. It takes a dozen or so sessions to see results. Two suggestions help: watch for distraction (a tempting thought, like composing an e-mail) and for **looping**, in which the mind rehashes what it already knows to avoid the harder next step. Then structure your thinking: load the relevant variables into working memory, define a specific next-step question, answer it, consolidate the answer, and repeat.

**Memorize a deck of cards.** Daniel Kilov, who struggled academically as a teenager, trained as a memory athlete and later graduated with first-class honors and entered a PhD program. Henry Roediger's team found that what separates elite memorizers is not memory itself but "attentional control," so memory training strengthens concentration generally. Newport teaches Ron White's method. Pick five familiar rooms and fix ten large items in each (plus two more) in a set order, to make fifty-two locations. Assign a memorable person or thing to each of the fifty-two cards, such as Donald Trump for the King of Diamonds since diamonds signify wealth. Then walk through your rooms, placing each card's image doing something vivid at each location, and recite the deck by walking through again. The deck itself is incidental: any structured task that requires unwavering attention will do, such as Talmud study or learning a guitar part by ear.

> **Key takeaway.** Concentration is a trainable skill, so strengthen it by quarantining distraction into scheduled blocks, working against tight deadlines, meditating productively, and practicing demanding exercises like memorization.

### Chapter 6: Rule #3: Quit Social Media

In 2013, Baratunde Thurston, once described as "the most connected man in the world," unplugged for twenty-five days after logging more than fifty-nine thousand Gmail conversations and fifteen hundred Facebook wall posts in a year. He wrote about it in *Fast Company*, finding the quiet rhythm of his disconnected days far less strange than expected, then went back online and was tweeting a dozen times a morning within months. Newport draws two points. First, network tools (social networks and infotainment sites such as BuzzFeed) fragment time and attention, and since willpower is finite, more tempting tools make focus harder. Second, our discussion is crude: a drastic sabbatical or acceptance of distraction. This rule proposes a third option, to hold such tools to a much more stringent standard, which means most people should use far fewer of them.

**The any-benefit mind-set versus the craftsman approach.** When Newport explained why he never joined Facebook, readers justified using it with minor reasons: entertainment, lightweight friendships, high-school acquaintances. This shows the **any-benefit approach**: you're justified in using a tool if you can identify any possible benefit, or anything you might miss. It ignores the costs of services engineered to be addictive. In contrast, Forrest Pritchard, who runs the sustainable Smith Meadows farm in Virginia, sold his hay baler even though hay is free from his own grass, because after counting fuel, repairs and especially opportunity costs (the time was better spent raising chickens whose manure feeds the soil, and heavy machinery compacts fields), buying hay gave healthier fields, his baseline. From him Newport derives the **craftsman approach**: identify the core factors that determine success and happiness in your professional and personal life, and adopt a tool only if its positive impacts on these substantially outweigh its negative ones.

**Apply the law of the vital few to your Internet habits.** Malcolm Gladwell, Michael Lewis and George Packer all avoid Twitter, even as David Carr defended it, because they judged its benefits too small for their circumstances. Lewis worries that accessibility is "impoverishing." To apply the method:
1. List the main high-level goals in your professional and personal life, general enough to avoid specific targets.
2. For each, list the two or three most important activities that serve it, such as "regularly read and understand the cutting-edge results in my field."
3. For each network tool, judge whether it has a substantially positive, negative or little impact on those activities, and keep it only if its substantial positives outweigh the negatives.

For Lewis, whose key activities are researching deeply and writing carefully, Twitter would at best do nothing. For a lesser-known author, Newport computes that ten personalized tweets a day, five days a week, with half becoming loyal fans, yields only about two thousand sales over two years. For a personal goal of close friendships, whose activities (meaningful time together, nontrivial sacrifices) are offline and effortful, Facebook's real conveniences don't matter enough. A college freshman building friendships or a deployed soldier might conclude differently. The logic comes from the **law of the vital few**: 80 percent of an effect comes from 20 percent of causes. Since all activities consume the same limited time and attention, low-impact ones displace high-impact ones, and a company that fires unproductive clients does better. Dropping a tool means getting more from the activities that already matter.

**Quit social media.** Ryan Nicodemus got rid of his stuff by packing everything into boxes and unpacking only what he needed in the next week, and most stayed in the boxes. Newport's equivalent: ban all social media for thirty days without deactivating accounts or announcing it. Afterward ask: would the last thirty days have been notably better with this service, and did people care that I wasn't using it? If both answers are no, quit permanently; if both are a clear yes, return; if ambiguous, lean toward quitting. These services deserve the test because they offer personalized information on an unpredictable schedule, which makes them addictive, and marketing has convinced people they would miss out. The silent exit also tests a related delusion, that people want your commentary. Social media replaced the hard work of earning attention (Newport's early blog had stretches with no readers) with a mutual "you like mine, I'll like yours" exchange that gives "a simulacrum of importance." The news may be sobering, but for most people the services are products designed to capture and sell attention, "a lightweight whimsy."

**Don't use the Internet to entertain yourself.** Arnold Bennett's 1910 *How to Live on 24 Hours a Day* told London office workers to treat the roughly sixteen hours outside work as a "day within a day" and use them deliberately. Newport sets aside Bennett's classist cultural program but keeps his logic. Entertainment sites such as BuzzFeed, Reddit and Business Insider can't be quit like a network, are always a click away, and thrive when you have nothing planned, a crutch against any boredom that also undermines Rule #2. The remedy is to decide in advance how to spend evenings and weekends, with structured hobbies, a reading program, exercise or good company (Newport reads three to five books at a time after his kids' bedtime). Bennett also answers the objection that planned leisure is tiring: mental faculties "do not tire like an arm or a leg. All they want is change—not rest, except in sleep."

> **Key takeaway.** Judge each network tool by whether it substantially helps what matters most to you, test social media with a thirty-day exit, and fill your leisure with planned, worthwhile activity so entertainment sites lose their grip.

### Chapter 7: Rule #4: Drain the Shallows

In summer 2007 the software company 37signals (now Basecamp) cut its workweek to four days from May through October and found the same amount got done. Founder Jason Fried replied to a critical *Forbes* piece that the point was "doing less work," with four normal eight-hour days: "Very few people work even 8 hours a day," and fewer hours make people "stingy with their time." The cut disproportionately removed shallow work. The company then went further, giving employees all of June to work on their own projects, with no status meetings or memos, and a "pitch day" produced two projects that went into production. "How can we afford not to?" Fried asked. The lesson is that shallow work is less vital than it seems, and recovered time can be redirected to depth.

Newport adds a caveat: the goal is to tame shallow work, not eliminate it, since a job needs some of it. And capacity is bounded. Anders Ericsson's research suggests about one hour a day for a novice and about four for experts. That might seem to leave half the day free, but meetings and appointments consume it quickly. A sample day of Newport's, with two meetings and a class, left only about four hours before any e-mail.

**Schedule every minute of your day.** Britons aged 25–34 estimated fifteen to sixteen hours of weekly television when meters showed twenty-eight, and Americans overestimate their work hours and underestimate their sleep. We live on autopilot. The fix: each morning, on a fresh notebook page, write the hours down the left side and divide the day into boxes of at least thirty minutes, assigning each a job (batching small tasks into generic blocks). When estimates fail or interruptions hit, which they will, rebuild the schedule from that moment, even half a dozen times. The aim is not to hold to the plan but "to maintain, at all times, a thoughtful say in what you're doing with your time." Tactics include conservative estimates, **overflow conditional blocks** (a block that continues the task if needed and otherwise has an alternate use), and generous task blocks for surprises. To a reader's worry that it is obsessive and kills creativity, Newport says spontaneity is encouraged: an important insight is a valid reason to abandon the day's plan. Structure, he says, prevents drift into e-mail and web surfing and leaves room for real thinking.

**Quantify the depth of every activity.** Shallow work is noncognitively demanding, logistical, often distracted and easy to replicate, but many tasks are ambiguous. Ask: how many months would it take to train a smart recent college graduate with no specialized training to do this task? Editing an academic paper takes fifty to seventy-five months, so it is deep. A quarterly-sales PowerPoint takes about two, and a project-status meeting about three, so both are shallow. Bias your time toward what scores high.

**Ask your boss for a shallow work budget.** Ask what percentage of your time should go to shallow work. For most non-entry-level knowledge jobs, the answer lies between 30 and 50 percent. Then keep to it, saying no to shallow-heavy projects and trimming existing ones, for example replacing weekly status meetings with results-driven reports. A shared budget gives you cover, since you can show the real ratio, and the numbers shrink the guilt for self-employed people who feel obliged to be on Twitter. If your boss answers "as much as is needed," you have learned the job doesn't support deep work, and you should plan a move.

**Finish your work by five thirty.** In one week Newport joined sixty-five e-mail conversations and sent exactly five e-mails after five-thirty. This is **fixed-schedule productivity**: set a firm cutoff and work backward to find habits that meet it. The contrast is a junior professor, "Tom," who spent twelve hours at the office with only one and a half on real work, and who accepted that he would work weekends. Newport published about twenty peer-reviewed articles in three years while rarely working nights. Radhika Nagpal of Harvard capped herself at fifty hours a week, travelled only five times a year (junior colleagues made twelve to twenty-four trips), limited reviewing, earned tenure on schedule, and was promoted to full professor three years later. The tactics: guard the word "yes" ("Talk to me after tenure"), refuse clearly but vaguely, offer no consolation prize, and plan carefully because time is capped. The scarcity mind-set raises the bar for anything shallow, and also tests assumptions about after-hours e-mail from the boss.

**Become hard to reach.** John Freeman's *The Tyranny of E-mail* warns that we are losing the ability to question the tool. Newport gives three tips.
- **Make senders do more work.** Instead of a general address he lists specific contacts and a single "interesting" address, with the explicit condition that he will respond only to proposals that match his schedule and interests. This **sender filter** cuts volume and resets expectations so an unread pile creates no obligation. Clay Herbert uses an FAQ, a survey and a small fee, and Antonio Centeno posts questions publicly and makes senders tick three promises before a message box appears.
- **Do more work when you send or reply.** Instead of a quick reply that bounces the ball back, ask "What is the project represented by this message, and what is the most efficient process for bringing it to a successful conclusion?" Then write a **process-centric** reply that lays out the steps (for example, offering six concrete meeting slots and treating any reply as confirmation). It cuts messages and "closes the loop" on the project, freeing mental space.
- **Don't respond.** Following professors at MIT, put the burden on the sender to make a convincing, low-effort case. Skip any e-mail that is ambiguous, doesn't interest you, or where nothing good happens if you reply and nothing bad if you don't. As Tim Ferriss says, "Develop the habit of letting small bad things happen."

> **Key takeaway.** Shallow work matters far less than it feels, so budget it, schedule your time deliberately, cap the workday, and make yourself harder to reach to protect the limited deep hours you have.

## Conclusion

Newport closes with the legend of Microsoft's founding. In 1974 Bill Gates, a Harvard student, saw the Altair on the cover of *Popular Electronics* and spent eight weeks hacking together a BASIC for it, sometimes collapsing asleep on his keyboard mid-line and waking to carry on. Paul Allen called it "a prodigious feat of concentration," and Walter Isaacson wrote that what set Gates apart was focus: "a serial obsessor." For Newport this is the strongest argument for deep work. He has no stake in the debate between curmudgeons nostalgic for unhurried focus and digital hipsters who treat that nostalgia as Luddism, the idea that "the medium is morality." Deep work is a pragmatic skill, not a moral stance, and it matters not because distraction is evil but because it let Gates start a billion-dollar industry in less than a semester.

He then reports on his own experience. In graduate school, deep work let him write two good papers a year without working past five on weekdays. Anticipating a busier life as a professor, he trained at MIT: artificially limiting his schedule, taking long midday runs, blocking out and defending deep hours, thinking while walking, hunting for quiet places (the Barker library dome in summer, the Lewis Music Library in winter) and even buying a $50 lab notebook to make himself careful. When he joined Georgetown in 2011, his output rose from two good papers to an average of four a year. Then in his third year, with a book to write, tenure approaching and a rejected grant application to answer, he pushed further: refusing commitments, working in isolated places, posting a tally of deep hours near his desk, and working on problems in his head on subway rides, while shoveling snow and while stuck in traffic. He published nine peer-reviewed papers that year while writing the book and keeping his no-evenings rule. He admits the year was too extreme and exhausting and he will moderate it, but the point stands: deep work is far more powerful than most people realize.

He ends with a frank warning. The deep life is not for everyone. It takes hard work and drastic changes, it means giving up the comfort of artificial busyness, and it forces you to confront that your best may not yet be that good: "It's safer to comment on our culture than to step into the Rooseveltian ring." But those willing to give up those comforts will find that depth makes a life "rich with productivity and meaning." He returns to Winifred Gallagher's line, "I'll live the focused life, because it's the best kind there is," and adds: "I agree. So does Bill Gates."

> **Key takeaway.** Deep work is a practical, trainable skill that multiplies what you can accomplish, and it asks you to give up comfortable distraction in exchange for a more productive and meaningful life.


## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The book's central claim is economic and pragmatic: the new economy rewards people who can master complicated things quickly and produce excellent work, and both abilities rest on deep concentration. Newport backs this with the deliberate-practice research of Anders Ericsson, myelin and attention residue, then explains the paradox that organizations nonetheless push shallow behavior. Because the value of depth sits in the "metric black hole," companies drift toward whatever is easy or looks busy.

A second theme is that concentration is limited and trainable. Roy Baumeister's finite willpower, Ericsson's cap of about four hours, the Zeigarnik effect and attention restoration theory all lead to the same advice: arrange your life with rituals, shutdowns and rest rather than relying on motivation. A third theme is that depth is meaningful. Gallagher, Csikszentmihalyi, and Dreyfus and Kelly each argue in their own way that focused craft is a route to a good life, which turns a productivity book into something closer to a philosophy of work.

### Strengths and limits

The strengths are variety and concreteness. Newport offers several working philosophies (monastic, bimodal, rhythmic and journalistic) and specific tools such as the lead-measure scoreboard, the shutdown ritual, Roosevelt dashes, the sender filter, process-centric e-mail and the one-question depth test. The case studies are memorable, and the 4DX adaptation in particular is easy to try.

The limits are mostly about who can use it. Many of the examples are professors, writers and programmers whose output is discrete and who can refuse requests. The advice to quit social media, answer fewer e-mails and finish by five-thirty assumes autonomy that people in service roles, junior positions or collaborative cultures may not have. Much of the evidence is anecdote and secondhand summary of research. *Where I'd push back:* Newport treats his own result of nine papers in a year as proof, though it is a single case and he admits the year was too exhausting to repeat, and the dismissal of social media's benefits can undersell its value for some careers and communities. Take the principles seriously and test the rules against your own job.

### Applying it

1. **Choose a depth philosophy** (Chapter 4). Pick the monastic, bimodal, rhythmic or journalistic approach that matches your job, then fix a time and place.
2. **Track a lead measure.** Count hours of deep work toward one wildly important goal, and review the tally weekly (Chapter 4).
3. **Train your focus.** Schedule Internet blocks and practice a Roosevelt dash or productive meditation session (Chapter 5).
4. **Test your tools.** Apply the craftsman approach and a thirty-day social media break (Chapter 6).
5. **Drain the shallows.** Block out your day, set a shallow work budget, end work at a fixed time, and finish with a shutdown ritual (Chapters 4 and 7).

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 1, 4, 5 and 7. Chapter 1 supplies the core argument, Chapter 4 the scheduling philosophies and shutdown ritual, Chapter 5 the focus-training exercises, and Chapter 7 the techniques for cutting shallow work.
