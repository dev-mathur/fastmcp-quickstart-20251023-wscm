## Overview

*Problem Solving 101: A Simple Book for Smart People* is a short, heavily illustrated guide written by Ken Watanabe, a former McKinsey consultant who left the firm to teach problem solving to Japanese schoolchildren. The book was first published in Japanese in 2007, where it became the country's top business bestseller, and appeared in English in 2009. It presents the consultant's toolkit (logic trees, yes/no trees, hypotheses, design plans, hypothesis pyramids, prioritization matrices, pros and cons, and criteria and evaluation) as habits that a school child can use, and it teaches them through cartoon characters and three short case stories. The book calls its four sections "classes"; this article treats each as a chapter, numbered in book order.

In the preface Watanabe explains why he wrote it. Problem solving, he says, is a mind-set as much as a skill: proactive people shape their environment instead of accepting the status quo. The tools need no software or analysts, only ways to broaden and organize thinking so that more solutions become visible. He also describes teaching children to run a food business from a renovated 1965 VW van before showing them any tools, on the principle that learning from real experience comes first.

Three ideas hold the book together.

1. **Problem solving is thinking plus acting.** The four steps are to understand the situation, find the root cause, develop an effective action plan and execute, adjusting as you learn. Skipping any one produces a recognizable failure.
2. **Form a hypothesis, then test it.** A hunch about the likely cause or solution, written down with its rationale, tells you exactly which analyses and information you need, so you collect only what matters. The results often contradict the hunch, which is the point.
3. **Broaden before you narrow.** Logic trees, goals with a gap, and options compared on weighted criteria all force you to list everything first, then prune, so decisions rest on more than the first idea or first impression.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Problem-Solving Kid Basics

The first class opens with the claim that you are never too old to become a problem-solving kid. Watanabe says problem solving is not a talent given to the lucky few but a skill and a habit that anyone can learn, and he previews three case studies that run through the book: the Mushroom Lovers, a school rock band trying to raise its concert attendance; John Octopus, who wants to buy his first computer on the way to a career in computer-generated animation; and Kiwi, a young football player choosing a school in Brazil. He then introduces four caricatures of how not to approach a problem, so that the right attitude can be defined by contrast.

Miss Sigh gives up as soon as something looks hard, fears being laughed at and blames her parents, society and everyone else. Mr. Critic is never afraid to speak up, shoots down other people's plans and says "I told you so", but he never does anything himself. Miss Dreamer comes up with grand ideas and never turns them into plans, preferring them in her head to the reality. Mr. Go-Getter is the most appealing of the four, because he acts at once and never gives up, but he treats every failure as a lack of effort, never looks for the root cause and keeps running in wrong directions at full speed. The problem-solving kid shares his energy but balances thinking and acting: she finds the root cause, sets a specific goal, plans before and during action, monitors progress and learns from successes and failures.

A path chart makes the contrast visible: Sigh circles the start, Critic's dotted lines are other people's plans he shot down, Dreamer stares at the goal, Go-Getter zigzags, and the problem-solving kids reach the first goal quickly and head for the next.

![Five paths from start to goal: Miss Sigh circles, Mr. Critic shoots down plans, Miss Dreamer stares at the goal, Mr. Go-Getter zigzags, and the problem-solving kid heads straight there, checking progress](/figures/problem-solving-101/five-characters.svg "Redrawn: the book's comparison of the five characters' paths from start to goal.")

Watanabe adds that problem-solving kids also evolve faster, because they take action and learn from the feedback. His example is three fruit stands that each sell 100 watermelons a month. After three years the stand growing 1 percent a month sells 143, the one growing 5 percent sells 579 and the one growing 10 percent sells 3,091, twenty-two times as many as the first. Small, steady improvement compounds.

![Three curves of monthly watermelon sales over three years at 1, 5 and 10 percent monthly growth, ending at 143, 579 and 3,091](/figures/problem-solving-101/growth.svg "Redrawn from the book's growth chart; the end values are the ones given in the text.")

The chapter then defines problem solving as four steps: understand the current situation, identify the root cause, develop an effective action plan and execute until the problem is solved, modifying as needed. These come as a package, and the book's example is a student whose maths grades are falling. Saying "I have to raise my grades", or quitting the football team to study more, does not work if the real problem is how effectively he studies. A problem-solving kid breaks the grades into algebra, fractions and geometry, finds that only geometry is falling, then breaks geometry into area, angles and volume and discovers the specific topics (trapezoid area, cylinder volume, the Pythagorean theorem) that cause the trouble. Only then does he ask whether to study longer, study better or both, and choose actions such as waking up thirty minutes early, finding a better textbook or hiring a tutor. The point is to keep asking "why" and "how" until the plan is custom-made.

The first tool box introduces the **logic tree**, a visual tool for finding all the potential root causes of a problem or a wide variety of solutions. Its rule is to break a topic into categories without leaving anything out and without overlap, and to group similar items on the same branch. A Year Four class can be broken down by gender, by height or by dominant hand, and the reader is asked to build one by school club. Trees are built by listing ideas first, then asking from the right "what can I call this group?" and from the left "specifically what or how?". A second exercise asks how to get more pepper out of a shaker without shaking harder or faster; the tree splits into a bigger top surface and more pepper from the same surface, then into more holes or more pepper per hole, then into bigger holes or finer grains, and the book notes that enlarging the holes is how one spice company raised its sales.

![A logic tree for getting more pepper out of a shaker, branching from the goal to bigger top surface, more holes, bigger holes and finer grains](/figures/problem-solving-101/logic-tree.svg "Redrawn: the book's pepper-shaker logic tree.")

> **Key takeaway.** Problem solving is a four-step habit of understanding the situation, finding the root cause, planning and acting, and the logic tree is the basic tool for laying out every cause or solution before choosing one.


### Chapter 2: Rock Bands and Root Causes

The second class teaches the diagnostic half of problem solving through the Mushroom Lovers, a rock band of three friends. Watanabe starts from a doctor's method: ask about symptoms, run tests, reach a diagnosis and only then prescribe, and keep apart the symptom (a headache), the root cause (a fever) and the prescription (cold medicine). The class follows two steps: diagnose the situation and identify the root cause, then develop the solution, each with sub-steps that the story illustrates.

Miss Mushroom, the singer, is bossy, energetic and hates hearing "that's impossible". Her bandmates, Eggplant on guitar and Tofu on drums, usually do whatever it takes to make her ideas work. After three monthly concerts in the school gymnasium that drew only ten, fifteen and fifteen people, she is furious and tells the two to fill the room next time, so they set out to find the root cause.

**Step 1a: List all the potential root causes.** To attend, people must be aware of the concert, want to go and come again. The band maps this as a logic tree: some people are not aware, others are aware but do not come, others came once and stopped. The tree is turned into a **yes/no tree**, a tool box item that sorts everyone into buckets by answering questions in order (Are they aware? Have they ever been? Do they attend consistently?) so everyone falls into exactly one bucket. A second example works through oversleeping, asking whether the alarm rang, was set for 6 a.m. and had a working battery.

**Step 1b: Develop a hypothesis.** A hypothesis is a hunch about the most likely explanation, with the reasoning that underlies it, so it can be checked. Watanabe's everyday example is visiting grandmother on the day after Thanksgiving: the hypothesis is that traffic will be bad because of the sales, so take the train, and a radio traffic report or a call to a friend tests it quickly. Eggplant and Tofu hypothesize that the root cause is awareness: only about 5 percent of the school knows about the shows, 60 percent of those who know would come because 60 percent like rock, and 100 percent of attendees return.

**Step 1c: Determine the analyses and information required.** Before collecting anything they write a **problem-solving design plan**, a tool box table listing the issues, current hypotheses and rationale, analyses and activities, and information sources. This keeps them from drowning in data. The first analysis is a three-question hand-raising survey run by teachers during Monday registration, a cheap way to cover 500 people instead of the 167 days that one interview a day would take. The second and third are interviews with about five people each: those aware who never came, and those who came and then dropped off.

**Step 1d: Analyze and identify the root cause.** The survey shows that 30 percent, not 5 percent, are already aware, but only 10 percent of those aware had ever come and 80 percent of attendees return. The hypothesis is mostly wrong: the main problem is that aware people do not come.

![Bars comparing the band's guesses of 5, 60 and 100 percent with the survey results of 30, 10 and 80 percent](/figures/problem-solving-101/hypothesis-vs-result.svg "Redrawn from the book's comparison of the original hypothesis and the actual survey results.")

Watanabe stresses that acting on the first hunch with posters and flyers would have added only a few spectators, which is why a test is worth the effort. Interviews reveal three reasons people stay away: they do not know what kind of music the band plays or how good it is, the noon Saturday time clashes with their own commitments (hockey games, for instance) and some are simply not interested in music. Fans who attend love the show but say the band plays the same songs the same way every time, which is also why those who stopped coming left. Praise and criticism are both useful.

**Step 2a: Develop a wide variety of solutions.** With the causes known, the band builds a logic tree of ways to reach the school (performing in each registration, the school newspaper, posters, flyers, message boards, the school radio, a CD, e-mail, a website and word of mouth from attendees), and holds back from judging ideas at this stage. They tick whether each idea makes people aware, makes them want to come, or both, and add actions from the interviews: move the show to 5 p.m., change 20 percent of the songs and the order, and have Tofu tell jokes between songs. That makes thirteen ideas.

**Step 2b: Prioritize actions.** They plot each idea on an **impact versus ease of implementation** matrix. The top right (high impact, easy) comes first, the top left and bottom right next, the bottom left last. Performing in every classroom is high impact but hard; posting on message boards is easy but weak.

**Step 2c: Develop an implementation plan.** Prioritizing assumes the band does everything alone, but asking others moves an action across the grid. Nobody in the band can draw, so a poster is hard, until Tofu remembers that John Octopus can make a great one easily; someone in the class can burn a CD and someone can build a website.

![A two by two matrix of impact against ease of implementation, with an arrow showing the poster moving to high impact and easy once John helps](/figures/problem-solving-101/impact-ease.svg "Redrawn: the book's impact versus ease of implementation matrix.")

At the next concert, more than 200 people come, awareness is 90 percent, half of those aware attend and 90 percent of attendees return. The three bandmates cry, and Miss Mushroom hugs her two friends and thanks them.

> **Key takeaway.** Diagnose before you prescribe: list the possible causes, state a hypothesis, test it with the cheapest analysis that works, and only then brainstorm, prioritize by impact and ease, and borrow help to make hard actions easy.


### Chapter 3: Fishy Goals and Solid Achievements

The third class is about setting goals and closing the gap to them. Watanabe says problem-solving kids do not just have big dreams: they break them into smaller milestones, asking what they should do this year, in the next three months or today. The story follows John Octopus, the poster designer from the previous class, who dreams of Hollywood and computer-generated animation after seeing a CGI movie with his friend Sarah. He does not own a computer, wants one by early December so that he can design his family's Christmas card, and has six months. The process has four steps: set a clear goal, determine the gap between the goal and the current situation, form a hypothesis about how to close the gap, and check the hypothesis and develop an action plan, with a final push on execution.

**Step 1: Set a clear goal.** "I want a computer" and "Buy a computer" are ambiguous. A good goal answers three questions, what, when and how, and includes any special conditions: "I want to buy a $500 used Apple computer within six months without borrowing money from others." The time frame matters because a goal of three years could be met by saving, while six months may require new income sources. The more specific the goal, the more specific the action plan.

**Step 2: Determine the gap.** John has $150 in savings, receives a $20 allowance and earns $3 an hour walking a neighbor's dog one hour a week, about $12 a month, and spends about $15 a month. At that rate his savings in six months are $150 + 6 x ($20 + $12 - $15) = $252, so he is $248 short of $500. A diagram compares the projected savings to the goal, and the gap is what the plan must fill.

**Step 3a: List as many options as possible.** His first ideas (ask Mom for a higher allowance, earn more, win something) are weak because they are vague, rely on others or depend on luck, and he could give up. A logic tree gets him out of that rut. The goal sits on the left and splits into "increase income" and "reduce spending." Income splits into money from others and self-generated income (a higher hourly rate, more working time, selling assets, investing), and spending splits into entertainment, food and beverages, and others. To grow the tree vertically he asks "are there other ways?", and to grow it horizontally, "specifically how or what?".

**Step 3b: Select the best ideas as the hypothesis.** He prunes branches that are infeasible or against his values: money from others (he wants to do it himself), more working time (hockey and school need his time), investing and the lottery. The hypothesis is that he can buy the computer in six months by switching to a higher-paying job, selling used books and giving up CDs and games. He shows it as a **hypothesis pyramid**, with the main claim at the top and the specific conditions, the "hows", as the base. It remains a hypothesis because, for example, he does not know what job pays Kevin $8 an hour.

The tool box on pyramids distinguishes two types. In a **grouping structure**, separate supports (school sports, lunchtime and classes are fun) back a claim ("school is fun") and the claim can survive if one support is false. In an **argument structure**, linked statements ("salmon are fish", "fish are good swimmers") lead to a conclusion, and if one fails the conclusion falls. Writing the pyramid before collecting data improves productivity and helps communicate the argument to others.

![Two pyramids: a grouping structure where three parallel reasons support one claim, and an argument structure where two linked statements lead to a conclusion](/figures/problem-solving-101/pyramids.svg "Redrawn: the book's two types of hypothesis pyramid.")

**Step 4: Check the hypothesis.** John writes a design plan with three questions (how much can I cut spending, how much more can a better job earn, what can I sell) and the analyses to answer each. His first analysis, a spending breakdown from three months of receipts and memory, disproves his hunch. He spends most on sports drinks and comic books, not CDs and games, because they are bought more often. Another matrix, average monthly spending against whether he can give an item up, shows that CDs and games are easy to cut with large impact, sweets are small and not worth fighting over, and sports drinks and comics are big but hard to give up. He solves that by mixing powdered sports drink at home and sharing comics with a friend, which halves them. Spending falls from $15 to $5 a month, saving $60 over six months and leaving $188.

Selling unneeded things comes next: unused books bring $25 and a spare golf bag from his father brings $25, and the old glove and clothes go to a friend's brother, leaving $138. Interviews with five friends show jobs pay $2 to $8 an hour, and the highest pay goes to special skills, such as web design or a second language. Neighbors offer nothing above $3. John needs $23 more a month, and a creative leap solves it: walking three dogs at once earns $9 an hour, and the owners agree, delighted to help a future animator.

![A bar of the $500 goal built from $252 projected savings, $60 of spending cuts, $50 of asset sales and $138 from walking extra dogs](/figures/problem-solving-101/gap-bridge.svg "Redrawn: how John's four sources of money add up to the $500 goal.")

The class ends on execution. Impact equals plan effectiveness times quality of execution, so a good plan and weak action, or the reverse, will not produce results. Write down what to do and when, monitor progress and revise as conditions change, for example if a neighbor stops needing a dog walker. You need not panic, because you know the process.

> **Key takeaway.** A specific goal (what, when, how), a measured gap and a pruned logic tree give you a testable hypothesis, and the plan only delivers when you test it against data, adjust, and then execute.


### Chapter 4: Football School Pros and Cons

The last class is about decisions. Problem-solving kids, Watanabe says, rarely regret their choices, because they consider their options and the information behind them first. The story follows Kiwi, the starting striker of the under-seventeen national team although she is still in her first year at secondary school. After her team loses 10-0 to the Brazilian under-seventeen side, she wants to train in Brazil, and to become bicultural and bilingual. Her parents agree if she balances football with school work and the tuition stays under $3,000 a year.

Kiwi quickly researches the two best-known schools, noting that all of the Brazilian team's starters come from them. Rio High School, last year's champion, has a flashy multilingual website, is in Rio de Janeiro, where she could surf, and costs $3,000. Amazon High School, last year's runner-up, is hard to find online (she gets there by typing the Portuguese "Amazonas Escola"), has only a Portuguese site, has no foreign students and no tailored program, is far from Rio in the mountains, and costs $5,000. In a pros-and-cons grid Rio wins, and she stays up until 4 a.m. filling in the application.

The chapter then adds two tools for evaluating options. **Pros and cons** has four steps: list all options, list the pros and cons of each, weight each point (for example plus or minus three for very important points like quality of education and tuition, one for minor ones like the age of the building) and pick the most attractive. Watanabe warns that first impressions bias us, so we collect evidence for what we already like, and that it is critical to ask what the negatives and other positives might be. In his example with three schools, Beaver High, with 14 pluses against 2 minuses, beats Armadillo (10 against 5) and Cougar (2 against 9). **Criteria and evaluation** asks you to list the options, list the evaluation criteria, assign each criterion a degree of importance (high, medium or low, or a 10-point scale), rate each option on each criterion (with real data such as minutes of travel or yes/no where possible) and select the winner while remembering the weights.

![A table rating Armadillo, Beaver and Cougar high schools against seven weighted criteria, with Beaver best on the high-importance rows](/figures/problem-solving-101/criteria-evaluation.svg "Redrawn: the book's criteria-and-evaluation example, with the same ratings.")

Both tools, Watanabe notes, are more than ways to organize thoughts: they reveal missing information, make you question what you really think is important, and may suggest ways to reduce an option's shortcomings.

Kiwi's story takes a turn when she watches a sports show on why the Yankees cannot win despite their superstars. A commentator says the owner hires ready-made stars, so rookies never become starters and cannot develop, and that "a strong team doesn't always equal a great growth environment." Kiwi realizes she has been choosing the best team rather than the best environment to become the best player she can be. She rewrites her criteria: a great environment to improve at football (high importance), a great environment to become bicultural and bilingual (medium), cost within $3,000 (high) and being close to Rio (very low), and she starts challenging each. What are the key factors of a great football environment? Is a tailored program with thirty-plus American students really good for immersion? Is there any funding? She lies awake again until she realizes that worrying will not help, and turns the questions into a to-do list of actions.

Taking action changes everything. Coach Jones, head coach of the U.S. under-seventeen team, tells her the key is to be surrounded by great players she both plays with and competes against every day, to get as much playing time as possible, to avoid teams that bench second- and third-string players and to value the quality of coaching. He cannot judge the two schools, so he puts her in touch with Coach Zico, a Brazilian coach who knows both. Zico recommends Amazon: Rio lets only its starting lineup play matches and its celebrity coaches spend little time at the school, while Amazon plays all its players equally and has coaches who give lots of face time. Kiwi also e-mails three American students at Rio, who all say that the tailored program is good but that Americans stay together and do not mingle, so someone who wants true immersion should go to a school with only local students; exchange students at her own school agree. Her evaluation table flips: Amazon wins on football environment and immersion, and Rio wins only on cost and closeness to the city.

The remaining problem is the $5,000 tuition, $2,000 over budget. Amazon offers no aid to foreign students, the scholarships she finds have passed their deadlines and U.S. Soccer has none for secondary students. She is dejected until Coach Jones makes a phone call to the chief marketing officer of Nike, who had been asking about the first-year striker, and the company offers to sponsor her tuition, living costs and gear. A month later, at JFK airport, she realizes that this is the scene from the dream she had the night she wrote her to-do list.

Watanabe closes by saying it was not just luck. Kiwi challenged her own preconceptions, acted proactively, asked for advice and had practiced hard for years, in line with Seneca's saying that luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity. The lessons: spend less time worrying and more on actions, ask for advice, and challenge your own thinking by asking whether your pros and cons are really pros and cons, whether you have the right criteria and weights, and whether your evaluation rests on accurate, up-to-date, unbiased information. Even Kiwi could have compared more schools, and in other countries, but everyone faces deadlines. The book ends by urging readers to make problem solving a habit, to solve their own problems and those of their school, business and community.

> **Key takeaway.** Evaluate options with weighted pros and cons or criteria, then challenge the criteria themselves and go and get better information, because Kiwi's best decision came from changing what she was measuring and asking experts, not from tallying plus signs.


## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The first theme is the balance of thinking and acting. The five characters in Chapter 1 each fail by one-sidedness: Sigh, Critic and Dreamer never act, Go-Getter acts without thinking. The cure is to alternate between them, finding the root cause before acting and monitoring progress while acting. The second theme is the hypothesis-driven approach. In Chapters 2 and 3, a hypothesis is stated, a design plan names the analyses and information needed to test it, and the data corrects the hunch (the band's awareness guess, John's belief about CDs and games). Hypotheses save effort precisely because they can be wrong.

The third theme is breadth before depth. The logic tree, the yes/no tree and the brainstormed list of thirteen ideas all push the reader to enumerate without overlap and without early judgment, then prioritize using a matrix. The fourth theme is that decisions are only as good as their criteria and their information. Kiwi's turning point in Chapter 4 is not a better calculation but a better question, "what makes a good environment for me?", followed by calls and e-mails to people who know. A final thread is attitude: problem solving is a proactive mind-set, and small improvements compound, as the watermelon stands show.

### Strengths and limits

The book is easy to read and concrete. The tools are simple enough for a child to draw on paper, and each is tied to one memorable story, so readers can try them straight away. It teaches by example how to set a measurable goal, quantify a gap and write a design plan, and it is honest that hypotheses are often wrong.

The limits come from its format and its audience. The cases are neat: the survey is cheap and unambiguous, interviews of five people suffice, a generous coach and a sponsor appear when needed. Real problems have messier data, tougher trade-offs and more people with their own interests, and the book says little about statistical care, sampling bias or organizational politics. The weighted pros-and-cons and criteria tools can give a false sense of precision, since the weights themselves are judgments. As a primer for older readers it also repeats a few ideas and is lighter on advanced techniques than the consulting practice it draws from.

*Where I'd push back:* John's plan treats a single successful dog-walking arrangement, based on a New York sighting, as a solved problem, and Kiwi's decision is rescued by a sponsorship that could not be planned. The method makes luck more likely, as Watanabe says, but readers should not conclude that good analysis guarantees the ending.

### Applying it

1. **State the problem as a specific goal** (Chapter 3). Write what you want, by when and under what conditions, and measure the gap to where you are now.
2. **Build a logic tree** (Chapter 1). List every possible cause or solution without overlaps, going wide before you go deep.
3. **Write your hypothesis and design plan** (Chapters 2 and 3). Say what you think is the answer and why, then list the analysis and information that would prove or disprove it before collecting anything.
4. **Prioritize and recruit help** (Chapter 2). Rate actions on impact and ease, and ask whether someone else could make a hard action easy.
5. **Evaluate options on weighted criteria, then challenge the criteria** (Chapter 4). Ask what you may be missing, and go to experts or people who have lived it.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapter 2 for the full diagnosis-to-action process on one case, and the Chapter 4 tool box if you are choosing between options.

### Teach it back

Try to explain the book in five sentences, from memory:

1. Problem solving is thinking and acting: understand the situation, find the root cause, plan and execute while adjusting.
2. Break problems into non-overlapping branches with logic trees so you consider every cause and solution.
3. State a hypothesis and its rationale, then run the cheapest analysis that tests it, because your first guess is often wrong.
4. Set goals that say what, when and how, measure the gap, and prioritize actions by impact and ease.
5. Choose between options with weighted pros and cons or criteria, challenge your own criteria and ask the people who know.
