## Overview

*The Prince* has the reputation of a handbook for villains. Read chapter by chapter, it is closer to a field report from someone who watched rulers rise and fall in the Italy of his day and wanted to write down what separated the ones who held on from the ones who did not.

Niccolò Machiavelli wrote it around 1513, after he lost his post in the Florentine republic when the Medici returned to power, and he dedicated it to a Medici ruler. It circulated in manuscript and was printed in 1532, five years after his death. Short quotes here follow W. K. Marriott's 1908 translation, which is in the public domain.

The book has a plain structure. Chapters 1 through 11 sort kinds of states and ask how each is won and held. Chapters 12 through 14 turn to armies. Chapters 15 through 19 are about the prince's own conduct and reputation. Chapters 20 through 23 handle the practical machinery of rule: fortresses, renown, ministers, and advisers. The last three chapters ask why Italian rulers keep losing their states, how much fortune governs events, and whether someone can finally free Italy.

Three ideas hold it together.

1. **Judge by what happens, not by what ought to happen.** A ruler who plans for how people should behave is ruined by how they do behave.
2. **Power rests on your own arms and on the people, not on borrowed strength.** Mercenaries, allies, and luck are loans that come due.
3. **Skill means matching your method to the times.** Fortune governs part of events, and character and preparation decide how much damage or benefit it does.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: The Kinds of Princedom

Machiavelli opens with a single sorting move. All states that have held power over people, he says, have been either republics or principalities, and principalities are either hereditary, where the ruling family has held power for a long time, or new. New ones are either wholly new, like Milan under Francesco Sforza, or members added to the hereditary state of a prince who has conquered them, like Naples under the King of Spain.

He then sorts how a new territory is acquired: by the conqueror's own arms or by other people's, and by fortune or by ability. The rest of the book follows this map. He says he will leave republics aside, having discussed them elsewhere, and will concentrate on how principalities are governed and kept.

> **Key takeaway.** The book begins with a taxonomy: how a state was acquired decides how hard it is to hold.

### Chapter 2: Hereditary Principalities

Hereditary states, Machiavelli says, are far easier to hold than new ones, because people are accustomed to the ruling family. A prince of ordinary ability keeps his place so long as he does not break with the ways of his ancestors and adapts prudently to events. Even if a stronger power drives him out, he can return when the usurper stumbles.

His example is the Duke of Ferrara, who survived the Venetians in 1484 and Pope Julius II in 1510 only because his house was long established. The natural prince has fewer reasons and less need to offend, and so he is naturally more loved. The antiquity and continuity of rule, Machiavelli adds, smooth over the memory of earlier changes, since one change always leaves a toothing for the next.

> **Key takeaway.** Custom is a ruler's cheapest asset. A hereditary prince only has to avoid breaking it.

### Chapter 3: Mixed Principalities

The difficulties begin with a new state added to an old one. People gladly change masters in the hope of improving their condition, and that hope misleads them: they take up arms against the ruler, and then find they are worse off. The new prince has made enemies of everyone he injured in taking the state, and he cannot keep as friends those who put him there, because he cannot satisfy them as they expected.

Machiavelli's cures are concrete. Conquest is easier where the new territory shares a language and customs with the old one, because you need only extinguish the old ruling family and leave laws and taxes alone. Where the language, customs, and laws differ, the best remedy is for the conqueror to live there in person, so that disorders are seen at birth. The next best is to plant colonies. They cost little, and they injure only the few who lose land and houses, who are poor and scattered and so cannot do harm, while everyone else is untouched. Garrisons, by contrast, are costly and offend the entire territory.

He advises making oneself the leader and protector of the weaker neighbors, weakening the powerful, and never allowing a strong foreigner in. The Romans did all this in Greece and elsewhere, seeing trouble far off and curing it in time. Machiavelli compares political trouble to a consumptive illness, which is easy to cure and hard to diagnose at the beginning, but once it is easy to diagnose it is nearly impossible to cure.

He then lists five errors that cost Louis XII of France his Italian gains: he destroyed the minor powers, increased the strength of an already strong one (the Church, through Pope Alexander VI), brought in a very powerful foreigner (Spain), did not come to live there, and did not send colonies. The chapter ends with a hard rule: people should either be caressed or annihilated, since they can take revenge for small injuries but not for great ones, so any injury to a man should be so severe that vengeance is impossible.

> **Key takeaway.** Hold new territory by living there or planting colonies, back the weak against the strong, and treat problems like a disease: act early, while they are still hard to see.

### Chapter 4: Why Darius's Kingdom Did Not Rebel

This chapter asks why Alexander the Great's conquest of Asia held together after his early death, when the kingdom of Darius had been so large. The answer is structural. Principalities are governed in two ways: by one prince, with everyone else his servants, or by a prince with barons who hold their positions by ancient lineage and have their own states and subjects.

A kingdom run by one prince with servants, like Turkey under its sultan, is hard to conquer, since no one in it has a reason to defect and the defenders cannot be corrupted from within. But once conquered, it is easy to hold, because the old ruling line has been extinguished and no one else commands the people's loyalty. Darius's Persia was of this kind, so Alexander's successors held it in peace.

A kingdom with barons, like France, is easy to enter, because some baron is always discontented and can be won over. But it is hard to hold, because the barons remain and will rise again when an opportunity comes. Machiavelli says the Romans found this in Spain, Gaul, and Greece, where many small princedoms kept rebelling, and held them securely only when the memory of the old rulers was gone.

> **Key takeaway.** How a state is organized decides how it can be conquered and held. A state with many powerful notables is easy to enter and hard to keep.

### Chapter 5: Governing Cities Used to Their Own Laws

A conquered state that has lived under its own laws and in liberty can be held in three ways: destroy it, go and live there, or leave it under its own laws, take tribute, and set up a friendly government of a few who will keep it loyal. The few, owing their position to the prince, will do all they can to keep him, and a city used to freedom is more easily controlled through its own citizens than in any other way.

Machiavelli's cases are Sparta, which held Athens and Thebes by installing oligarchies and still lost them, and Rome, which destroyed Capua, Carthage, and Numantia and lost none of them, but which failed in Greece by trying to hold it while leaving it its laws. The lesson is that nothing short of destruction is safe. Whoever becomes master of a city used to freedom and does not destroy it should expect to be destroyed by it, since it always has the name of liberty and its ancient customs to rally around, and neither time nor benefits make it forget them.

> **Key takeaway.** A people used to freedom never forgets it. Destroy such a city, live in it, or accept that it will rebel.

### Chapter 6: New Principalities Won by Your Own Arms

Here Machiavelli turns to the highest case: new states won by one's own ability and one's own forces. His models are Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, and Theseus. Each had nothing from fortune but the opportunity, which gave them the matter to shape into a new form. Without that opportunity their abilities would have been wasted, and without their abilities the opportunity would have come in vain. Moses needed the Israelites enslaved in Egypt, Romulus needed to be abandoned at birth, Cyrus needed the Persians discontented with the Medes, and Theseus needed the Athenians scattered.

Such men get their principality with difficulty but keep it with ease. The difficulties come from the new rules and methods they must introduce to found the state. There is nothing, he says, more difficult to handle, more doubtful of success, or more dangerous than to take the lead in introducing a new order of things. The reformer has as enemies all who profit from the old order, and only lukewarm defenders in those who might gain from the new, partly from fear of their opponents and partly from a doubt about whether anything is real until it has been tested. So the question is whether reformers can stand alone or depend on others, and whether they must beg or can use force. If they must beg, they always fail. If they can compel, they are rarely in danger.

> "All armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed ones have been destroyed."
>
> — Chapter 6

He cites Savonarola in Florence as the unarmed prophet who was ruined when the crowd stopped believing him and he had no means of forcing belief. A lesser example closes the chapter: Hiero of Syracuse, who rose from private life, disbanded the old army and raised a new one of his own, and so had nothing to fear from either.

> **Key takeaway.** Reformers need force as well as ideas, because the people who lose from change are certain and the people who gain from it are lukewarm.

### Chapter 7: New Principalities Won by Others' Arms or Fortune

The opposite case is rulers who rise by the arms of others or by good luck. They rise with ease but have much trouble holding on, since they have no roots. They depend on the will and fortune of those who gave them the state, two very unstable things, and they neither know how nor are able to keep their position, since without great ability a man who has always lived as a private citizen is unlikely to know how to command.

Machiavelli's case study is Cesare Borgia, who gained the Romagna through the fortune of his father, Pope Alexander VI, and lost it when that fortune ended. He praises the foundations Cesare laid. Cesare weakened the Orsini and Colonna factions by buying off their followers with offices and pay, pretended friendship to lure the Orsini leaders to Senigallia and had them strangled, and set out to win the people of the Romagna. Finding the province disordered, he appointed a harsh man, Remirro de Orco, with full powers, who restored peace in a short time. Then, because such excessive power was no longer necessary and was making him hated, Cesare set up a civil court and had Remirro cut in two and left in the public square at Cesena, with a block of wood and a bloody knife beside him. The sight left the people at once satisfied and stunned.

Cesare's weaknesses were that he had not secured his position for the day his father died, and that he could not control the choice of the next pope. He made his fatal error in allowing the election of Julius II, whom he had injured, since a new injury does not cause old ones to be forgotten. Machiavelli's verdict is that he would not know what better precepts to give a new prince than the example of Cesare's actions, and that if they did not succeed, it was through no fault of his, but through the extraordinary and extreme malice of fortune, since Cesare was himself gravely ill when his father died.

> **Key takeaway.** Borrowed power is unstable. Cesare Borgia did nearly everything right and still fell when his father's luck ended.

### Chapter 8: Those Who Gain Power by Wickedness

Two further ways of becoming prince are by crime and by the favor of fellow citizens. For crime, Machiavelli gives two examples. Agathocles of Syracuse, the son of a potter, rose through the army to be its praetor, then had the senate and the richest citizens assembled and killed on a signal, and so seized the city. He held it for life, and defended it against Carthage. Oliverotto of Fermo, raised by his uncle, returned to the city with a retinue, invited his uncle and the leading citizens to a banquet, had them murdered, and made himself ruler. A year later he was strangled at Senigallia, together with Vitellozzo Vitelli, by Cesare Borgia.

Machiavelli asks how Agathocles could be cruel for so long and yet live secure, while others, even in peace, could not hold their states. He explains it by the difference between cruelty well used and badly used. It is well used if it is done once, at the beginning, from the necessity of securing oneself, and then not continued but turned as far as possible to the benefit of the subjects. It is badly used when it is small at first and grows with time. Injuries should be inflicted all at once so that tasting them less, they give less offense, and benefits should be given little by little so that they are tasted longer. He adds that such acts bring power but not glory.

> **Key takeaway.** If cruelty is ever necessary, do it once, at the start, and then stop. Give benefits gradually so they are remembered.

### Chapter 9: The Civil Principality

A civil principality is one where a private citizen becomes prince by the favor of his fellow citizens, either of the people or of the nobles. In every city these two humors are found: the people do not wish to be ruled and oppressed by the nobles, and the nobles wish to rule and oppress the people. A prince who gains power with the help of the nobles has more trouble holding it than one who gains it through the people, because he is surrounded by men who consider themselves his equals, and he cannot rule or manage them as he pleases.

The prince who has the people's support stands alone, and has around him no one, or very few, who are not ready to obey. Nor can one honestly satisfy the nobles without injuring others, while the people can be satisfied, since the people's aim is more honest than the nobles', the latter wanting to oppress and the former only wanting not to be oppressed. The worst a prince can fear from a hostile people is to be abandoned by them, but from hostile nobles he has to fear that they will also act against him. And a prince can do without a given set of nobles, who can be made and unmade daily, but he cannot do without the same people.

Machiavelli adds practical rules. Nobles should be considered in two classes: those who bind themselves to you and are not rapacious, whom you should honor and love, and those who do not, whom you should watch as open enemies. A prince must also keep the people dependent on him in good and bad times alike, because a prince who has to rule through magistrates is more at risk in adversity, when citizens in office may quickly turn against him. He answers the proverb that he who builds on the people builds on mud by saying it holds for a private citizen, but not for a prince who can command and is not easily deceived.

> **Key takeaway.** Build on the people, not on the nobles. The people only want not to be oppressed, and a prince cannot replace them.

### Chapter 10: Measuring the Strength of Principalities

A prince's strength is measured by whether, if necessary, he can stand alone with the men and money he has, or must always depend on others. Those who can raise enough troops and money to meet any aggressor in the field are self-reliant, and those who cannot, and must shelter behind their walls, are not. In the second case, the prince should fortify his city and not worry about the countryside.

His model is the cities of Germany, which are free, with little territory, and which obey the emperor only when they please. They have strong walls and ditches, sufficient artillery, and public stores of food, drink, and fuel for a year, and they keep the common people employed in trades that are the strength and life of the city, with the army exercised and held in honor. A prince with a strong city who is not hated cannot easily be attacked, and if he is attacked, the besieger will go away with shame, since the world is so changeable that it is nearly impossible for someone to keep an army idle in the field for a year.

He answers the objection that the people, seeing their country houses burned, will lose patience and their self-interest will make them forget their prince. A prudent prince will always keep up the citizens' spirits by making them hope the evil will not last long, by terrifying them with the enemy's cruelty, and by securing himself against those who seem too bold.

> **Key takeaway.** A strong prince can stand alone. Walls, supplies, a trained militia, and a people who are not hated give a city the ability to wait out a siege.

### Chapter 11: Ecclesiastical Principalities

Church states are the only ones that are safe and happy from the start, because they are upheld by ancient religious customs so powerful that they keep the prince in power whatever he does and however he lives. Only these princes have states and do not defend them, subjects and do not govern them, and yet their states are never taken from them, and their subjects, though ungoverned, neither care nor are able to alienate themselves. Being governed by a higher power which the human mind cannot reach, Machiavelli says, he will not discuss them, since it would be the work of a presumptuous man.

He asks instead how the Church became so great in temporal power. Before Alexander VI, the Italian rulers, even petty barons, regarded it little. Alexander showed how much a pope could do with money and force, using Cesare Borgia as his instrument and the French invasion as his opportunity. Julius II followed, intending to increase the Church's power rather than any individual's. He gained Bologna, ruined the Venetians, and drove out the French, and he kept the Orsini and Colonna factions weak. Machiavelli ends by saying that Leo X, the present pope, finding the Church very powerful, may with his goodness and infinite other virtues make it still greater and more venerated.

> **Key takeaway.** Religious custom holds a church state in place, so it needs neither ability nor luck. Its power grew through ruthless popes who used both.

### Chapter 12: Kinds of Soldiery, and Mercenaries

Machiavelli opens with a claim that frames the rest of the book: the principal foundations of every state are good laws and good arms, and since there cannot be good laws where there are not good arms, he will speak of arms. The arms by which a prince defends his state are either his own, or mercenaries, or auxiliaries, or a mixture.

Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous. A prince who rests his state on mercenaries will never be secure, since they are disunited, ambitious, undisciplined, and unfaithful: brave among friends, cowardly among enemies, with no fear of God and no loyalty to men. Ruin is deferred only as long as the attack is deferred. In peace he is robbed by them, in war by the enemy. The reason is that they have no love or motive to keep them in the field beyond a small wage, which is not enough to make them want to die for him.

> "Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous."
>
> — Chapter 12

He argues that the ruin of Italy came from relying on mercenaries for many years. The captains are either excellent or not. If they are, you cannot trust them, since they aim at their own greatness by oppressing you or others. If they are not, they ruin you in the ordinary way. Venice and Florence, he says, did well while they used their own citizens, and the Venetians fell into trouble when they went to the mainland and used condottieri like Carmagnola, whom they ended by killing in order to be safe from him. The Florentines were saved from Hawkwood only by luck. Mercenary captains even encouraged the habit of avoiding battle, so that Italy was overrun by Charles VIII, who, as the saying went, took it with chalk in his hand, to mark the lodgings.

> **Key takeaway.** Hired soldiers fight for pay, not for you. Good arms are the foundation, and mercenaries are the quickest road to ruin.

### Chapter 13: Auxiliaries, Mixed Soldiery, and One's Own

Auxiliaries are the arms of a powerful ally lent to you for your defense. They are as useless as mercenaries, and more dangerous, because if they lose, you are ruined, and if they win, you are their prisoner. Machiavelli gives examples. Pope Julius II, in the Ferrara campaign, put himself in the hands of the King of Spain's auxiliaries and was saved only by a chance that destroyed both his own plans and his enemy's. The Florentines, having no arms of their own, brought 10,000 French troops against Pisa and suffered more danger than in any earlier trouble. The Emperor of Constantinople brought 10,000 Turks into Greece, who never left.

The wise prince, he says, always avoids these forces and turns to his own, preferring to lose with his own than to win with others, since a victory won with other men's arms is no real victory. He returns to Cesare Borgia, who first used French auxiliaries, then the Orsini and Vitelli as mercenaries, and then, finding them uncertain and unfaithful, destroyed them and relied on his own men. Machiavelli also tells the story of David, who offered to fight Goliath, and Saul gave him his own armor. David tried it on and refused it, saying he could not move freely, and took his sling and knife instead. Other people's arms either fall off, weigh you down, or tie you up.

He ends with Hiero of Syracuse, who, finding the old troops mercenary and useless, had them all cut to pieces and afterwards made war with his own arms, not with others'. The chapter's last point is on the French monarchy, which is weak because it had abandoned its own infantry for Swiss mercenaries.

> **Key takeaway.** Borrowed troops hamper the borrower. If they lose, you lose, and if they win, you are in their power.

### Chapter 14: A Prince and the Art of War

A prince should have no other object or thought, nor take up anything else for his study, but war and its organization and discipline, because that is the only art expected of a ruler. It is so powerful that it not only maintains those born princes but often raises private citizens to that rank, while rulers who think more of luxury than of arms lose their state. The first cause of losing it is neglecting this art, and the first way to acquire one is to be master of it.

He describes two kinds of exercise: with actions and with the mind. For action, a prince should keep his men well organized and trained, and should hunt, both to inure his body to hardship and to learn the nature of the ground: how mountains rise, valleys open, and plains lie, and what rivers and marshes are like. This knowledge helps in two ways, in knowing his own country better and in understanding any other he may need to explore, since the hills, valleys, and rivers of Tuscany resemble those of other provinces. Philopoemen, he says, always thought about the art of war in peace, asking his friends what they would do if the enemy appeared on a certain hill.

For the mind, a prince should read history, studying the actions of great men in war, seeing how they conducted themselves and why they won or lost. Above all, he should imitate some previous great man who was praised and honored, and keep his deeds before him, as Alexander imitated Achilles, Caesar imitated Alexander, and Scipio imitated Cyrus.

> **Key takeaway.** A ruler's first discipline is war, in peacetime as much as in war: train the body, study the ground, and learn from history.

### Chapter 15: What Men, and Princes Especially, Are Praised or Blamed For

Here the book states its method. Many writers have imagined republics and principalities that have never been seen or known to exist, Machiavelli says, but the way people live is so far from the way they ought to live that whoever neglects what is done for what ought to be done learns his ruin rather than his preservation. A man who wants to make a profession of goodness in everything must come to grief among so many who are not good. A prince who wants to keep his position must therefore learn how not to be good, and use that knowledge or not according to need.

Leaving aside imagined things, he turns to what is true. All men, and especially princes, who are set at a greater height, are noted for qualities that bring them praise or blame. One is considered liberal, another mean, one generous, another rapacious, one cruel, another compassionate, one faithless, another faithful, and so on. It would be most praiseworthy for a prince to have all the good qualities, but human conditions do not allow it. So he should be prudent enough to avoid the infamy of those vices that would lose him the state, and, if possible, of those that would not. He should also not trouble himself about incurring a reproach for vices without which the state can only be saved with difficulty, because some things that look like virtue will ruin him, and some things that look like vice will bring him security and well-being.

> **Key takeaway.** Plan for how people actually behave. A ruler who insists on being good in all things will be destroyed by those who are not.

### Chapter 16: Liberality and Meanness

It would be good to be reputed liberal, Machiavelli says, but liberality used in a way that gains you the name will harm you. If you exercise it virtuously and as it should be exercised, it may not be recognized, and you will not escape the reproach of the opposite vice. To keep up the name, a prince must spend lavishly until his resources are exhausted, and he will then be obliged to burden his people with extraordinary taxes. This makes him hateful to his subjects, and when he becomes poor, he is little valued by anyone. Having harmed many and rewarded few, he is open to every danger.

A prince should not mind being called mean, since it is one of those vices that enable him to govern. Machiavelli gives examples from his time. Pope Julius II used a reputation for liberality to reach the papacy, but did not try to keep it, so he could wage many wars without imposing extra taxes, since his long thrift supplied the extra expenses. The present King of France has done the same, and the King of Spain, had he been thought liberal, would not have undertaken or completed so many enterprises.

The rule is that a prince, to avoid robbing his subjects and to defend himself, needs to be able to use others' property freely. He should therefore spend what belongs to others or to enemies, as Caesar, Cyrus, and Alexander did, since spending others' wealth adds to his reputation, and only spending his own injures him. Nothing consumes itself like liberality, which by being used loses the power to be used.

> **Key takeaway.** Spend your own money carefully and other people's freely. A reputation for generosity bought with your own funds ends in heavy taxes and hatred.

### Chapter 17: Cruelty and Clemency, and Whether It Is Better to Be Loved or Feared

Every prince should wish to be considered merciful and not cruel, but he must take care not to misuse mercy. Cesare Borgia was considered cruel, yet his cruelty restored order to the Romagna, united it, and brought it to peace and loyalty. This, Machiavelli says, was much more merciful than the Florentine people's course, who, to avoid being thought cruel, allowed Pistoia to be destroyed. A prince should not mind the reproach of cruelty if it keeps his subjects united and loyal, since a few examples will be more merciful than too much leniency, which allows disorders that harm the whole community. New principalities in particular cannot avoid the name of cruel because they are full of dangers.

On whether it is better to be loved or feared, the answer is that one would like to be both, but since it is difficult to unite them, it is much safer to be feared than loved if one of the two has to be chosen. Men, he says, are ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly, and covetous, and while you succeed they are yours entirely, offering their blood, property, lives, and children when the need is far off, but turn against you when it approaches. A prince who relies on their words and has not made other preparations is ruined, because friendships bought with a price and not with greatness of mind are earned but not secured, and can fail in time of need. Men have less hesitation in offending someone who is loved than someone who is feared, since love is held by a chain of obligation which, men being selfish, is broken whenever it serves their interest, but fear is maintained by a dread of punishment that never fails.

> "It is much safer to be feared than loved."
>
> — Chapter 17

The limit is that a prince must make himself feared in such a way that, if he does not gain love, he escapes hatred. This he can do by abstaining from the property of his subjects and from their women. If he must take someone's life, he should do it when there is a proper justification and a manifest reason, but above all he must abstain from the property of others, because men more quickly forget the death of a father than the loss of a patrimony. Hannibal's inhuman cruelty held together an enormous mixed army in foreign lands without a revolt, while Scipio's leniency led to the mutiny of his soldiers in Spain, for which he was reproached in the Senate. The chapter ends with the point that a wise prince should rely on what is in his own control, not on what is in the control of others.

> **Key takeaway.** If you cannot be loved and feared, choose fear, but never invite hatred. Taking people's property is the surest way to earn it.

### Chapter 18: How Princes Should Keep Faith

How praiseworthy it is for a prince to keep his word and live with integrity, Machiavelli says, everyone understands, yet the experience of his time shows that the princes who have done great things have little regard for keeping faith, and have known how to trick men's minds with cunning. There are two ways of fighting: by law and by force. The first is proper to men, the second to beasts, but since the first is often not enough, one must have recourse to the second. A prince must therefore know how to use both the beast and the man. The ancient writers taught this in veiled form when they said that Achilles and other princes were given to the centaur Chiron to be raised, which means that the prince must be half man and half beast.

Among the beasts, he must choose the fox and the lion, because the lion cannot protect itself from traps, and the fox cannot defend itself from wolves. One must therefore be a fox to recognize traps, and a lion to frighten wolves. A prudent ruler cannot and should not keep his word when it would be to his disadvantage and the reasons for which he made his promise no longer exist. If men were all good, this advice would not be good, but because they are bad and will not keep faith with you, you need not keep faith with them. Legitimate reasons for breaking a promise are never lacking.

The prince must also appear to have the qualities of mercy, faith, humanity, integrity, and religion, and in particular the last. Everyone sees what you appear to be, and few know what you are, and those few dare not oppose the opinion of the many, who have the majesty of the state to defend them. In the actions of all men, and especially of princes, where there is no court of appeal, one looks at results. So let a prince conquer and maintain his state, and the means will always be judged honorable and praised by everyone. Machiavelli closes with an unnamed prince of his own day, whose name he withholds, who preaches peace and faith and is the enemy of both.

> "Everyone sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are."
>
> — Chapter 18

> **Key takeaway.** A ruler needs the lion's force and the fox's cunning, and he is judged by results and by appearances far more than by his inner motives.

### Chapter 19: Avoiding Contempt and Hatred

A prince must avoid whatever makes him hated or despised, and if he does so, he has done his part and will find no danger in other reproaches. Hatred comes chiefly from being rapacious and from usurping the property and women of his subjects, which he must abstain from. Contempt comes from being considered changeable, frivolous, effeminate, cowardly, and irresolute, which a prince must guard against as a rock, and he must show greatness, courage, gravity, and strength in his actions. His judgments about his subjects' private affairs should be irrevocable, so that no one thinks of deceiving or getting around him.

A prince who has this reputation is highly esteemed, and it is hard to conspire against someone so esteemed. A prince has two fears: one internal, about his subjects, and one external, from foreign powers. Against the second he is defended by good arms and good friends. Against the first, if the external is quiet, conspiracies are the main danger, and the best remedy is not to be hated or despised and to keep the people satisfied. A conspirator always fears the people's reaction after the deed, and so is deterred.

He gives the example of France, where the king is secured by the parliament as an independent institution, which receives the blame for acts that offend the great and the credit for those that please the people, so the king neither favors one nor offends the other. Princes should leave unpopular matters to others and keep the favors for themselves, and should honor the nobles without making the people hate them.

Machiavelli then tests the argument against the Roman emperors from Marcus to Maximinus. The emperors faced a double difficulty: they had to satisfy both the soldiers and the people, whose interests were opposed. Marcus, Pertinax, and Alexander Severus were mild, just, and humane, yet all but Marcus ended badly. Marcus lived and died honored because he inherited the throne and owed it to neither soldiers nor people. Pertinax was made emperor against the soldiers' wishes and was killed by them, and Alexander Severus was despised for being thought effeminate and ruled by his mother. Commodus, Caracalla, and Maximinus were cruel and rapacious, and all fell, while Septimius Severus succeeded by acting as a fierce lion and a cunning fox. The lesson is that a new prince cannot imitate Marcus, who inherited his place, and need not follow Severus in everything, but should take from Severus what is necessary to found a state and from Marcus what is fitting to preserve one.

> **Key takeaway.** The two things a ruler cannot survive are hatred and contempt. Keep the people satisfied and show strength, and conspiracies lose their appeal.

### Chapter 20: Are Fortresses and Other Common Expedients Useful?

Some princes disarm their subjects, some foster divisions, some create enmities against themselves, some win over those they suspected at the beginning, and some build fortresses or tear them down. Machiavelli says the right course depends on the situation, but he gives general rules. A new prince has never disarmed his subjects. On the contrary, when he found them unarmed, he armed them, because by arming them their arms become his, those he suspected become faithful, and those faithful remain so, turning subjects into partisans. Disarming them offends them, since it shows he mistrusts them, which breeds hatred.

On factions, he says it was once thought wise to keep subject cities divided, but this does not serve in practice, since divided cities fall quickly when an enemy approaches. Princes who build up enmities and overcome them make themselves greater, and so a wise prince, when he has the chance, may cleverly nourish some opposition so that crushing it will increase his reputation. He adds that princes, especially new ones, have found more loyalty and use in those they originally distrusted than in those who were trusted at the start.

On fortresses, he says they are useful or not depending on the times. A prince who fears his people more than foreigners should build them. One who fears foreigners more than his people should not. The Castle of Milan, built by Francesco Sforza, has caused and will cause more trouble to the Sforza family than any other disorder in the state. The best fortress of all is not to be hated by the people, because if you have them against you, no fortress will save you, since once the people take arms, foreigners are always ready to help them. In our times, the Countess of Forlì was not saved by hers against the people who killed her husband.

> **Key takeaway.** Arm your subjects, and do not rely on walls. The best fortress is the people's goodwill.

### Chapter 21: How a Prince Should Gain Renown

Nothing makes a prince so esteemed as great enterprises and rare examples of his own conduct. In Machiavelli's time the model is Ferdinand of Aragon, King of Spain, who from a weak king became the first in Christendom in fame and glory. If his actions are examined, they are all great and some extraordinary. Early in his reign he attacked Granada, and began by doing so in peace, keeping the barons occupied with it so that they did not think of innovations, gaining reputation and mastery over them without their noticing. He supported armies with money from the Church and the people and laid the foundation of his military power during the long war. Then, with a pious cruelty, he drove the Moors from his kingdom, and attacked Africa, Italy, and France, always planning great things that kept his subjects in suspense and admiration, so that one action grew out of another and left no time for anyone to plot against him.

It also helps a prince to give rare examples in internal affairs, as Bernabò Visconti of Milan did by rewarding or punishing in an extraordinary way anyone who did something notable in public life. A prince is also esteemed when he is a true friend or a true enemy, that is, when he declares himself openly for one side, because it is always better than remaining neutral. If two neighboring powers fight, and one wins, you either have to fear the winner or not. In both cases, it is better to declare yourself, because if you do not, you will be the prey of the winner, to the pleasure and satisfaction of the loser, and you will have no reason to be defended or sheltered. The neutral is hated by the one who wins and despised by the one who loses.

A prince should also never ally himself with someone stronger to attack others, unless necessity compels him, since if you win, you are his prisoner. He should show himself a lover of ability, honoring those who excel in the arts, encourage citizens to pursue their trades in peace, and entertain the people with festivals and shows at suitable times of the year.

> **Key takeaway.** A prince earns renown by great undertakings and by taking sides. Neutrality pleases no one, and it leaves you at the mercy of whoever wins.

### Chapter 22: A Prince's Secretaries

The choice of ministers is of no small importance to a prince, since they are good or not according to his prudence. The first impression of a ruler's intelligence is formed by seeing the men he has around him: when they are capable and faithful, he can always be considered wise, because he has known how to recognize their ability and keep them faithful. When they are otherwise, one can always form an unfavorable opinion of him, since the first error he makes is in this choice.

Machiavelli tells of Antonio da Venafro, minister to Pandolfo Petrucci, prince of Siena, whom everyone considered an exceptionally clever man for having such a minister. There are, he says, three kinds of intellect: one that understands things by itself, another that discerns what others understand, and a third that neither understands by itself nor through others. The first is excellent, the second good, and the third useless. If a prince is not in the first class, he must at least be in the second, since if he has the judgment to know the good and bad in what another does or says, he will recognize the good and bad acts of his minister, and the minister, knowing he cannot deceive him, will behave well.

There is one infallible test of a minister. When you see him thinking more of himself than of you, and seeking his own profit in all his actions, such a man will never be a good minister, nor can you trust him. For a man who has the state of another in his hands should never think of himself, but always of the prince, and never remind him of anything that does not concern him. On the other side, the prince, to keep his minister loyal, should honor him, enrich him, put him under obligations, and share honors and responsibilities with him, so that he sees he cannot stand without the prince, and with many honors, he does not desire more, and with much wealth he does not desire more, and with many responsibilities he fears changes.

> **Key takeaway.** The first test of a ruler's judgment is the people he picks. A minister who puts himself ahead of his prince cannot be trusted.

### Chapter 23: How to Avoid Flatterers

Machiavelli says he does not want to leave out an important point and an error from which princes defend themselves with difficulty, unless they are very prudent or have a good eye for people: the flatterers of whom courts are full. Men are so pleased with their own affairs and deceive themselves so easily about them that they defend themselves from this plague only with difficulty, and in defending themselves, they run the risk of becoming despised.

The only way to guard against flattery is to let men understand that they do not offend you by telling you the truth. But if everyone can tell you the truth, you lose respect. A prudent prince should therefore take a third course. He should choose wise men for his government, and give only to them the freedom to speak the truth, and only on matters he asks about, and on nothing else. But he should ask them about everything and hear their opinions, and then decide for himself, in his own way. With these counselors, and with each of them, he should behave in such a way that each knows that the more freely he speaks, the more he will be liked. Apart from them, he should listen to no one, go straight to the thing resolved, and be firm in his decisions. Whoever does otherwise either falls among flatterers or changes his mind often because of varying opinions, which causes him to be little esteemed.

Machiavelli gives the example of Maximilian, the present emperor, whose man says he never takes counsel from anyone and never does what he wants, because he does not act in the way described: he is a secretive man who does not communicate his plans, and when he starts to carry them out, they are discovered and opposed by those around him, and he, being easily influenced, is diverted from them. The result is that what he does one day he destroys the next, and no one can know what he wants. He closes by saying that good counsel, from wherever it comes, must arise from the prince's prudence, and not the prince's prudence from good counsel.

> **Key takeaway.** Ask a few wise advisers for the truth, and only when you ask. Then decide for yourself and hold to the decision.

### Chapter 24: Why the Princes of Italy Have Lost Their States

If the things written above are observed prudently, they make a new prince appear old and give him at once more security and stability than if he had been long established. A new prince is more closely watched than a hereditary one, and when his actions are seen to be able, they win men over and bind them more than ancient lineage does, because men are more attracted by present things than past ones, and when they find the present good, they enjoy it and seek nothing more.

The princes of Italy who lost their states after long possession, like the King of Naples and the Duke of Milan, must blame their own failings and not fortune. First, they had a common defect with regard to arms, for the reasons discussed at length. Second, some had the people hostile, or if the people were friendly, did not know how to secure the nobles. Without these faults, states strong enough to keep an army in the field are not lost. He contrasts them with Philip of Macedon, not the father of Alexander but the one defeated by Titus Quintius, who had a small state compared to the Romans and Greeks attacking him, yet because he was a military man who knew how to win over the people and secure the nobles, sustained the war against them for many years.

Therefore, these princes, who after many years of possession lost their principalities, should not blame fortune but their own indolence. In quiet times they never thought that the weather could change, a common human fault of not reckoning with storms in calm, and when adversity came, they thought of flight rather than defense, and hoped that the people, weary of the insolence of the conquerors, would recall them. This course is good when others fail, but it is wrong to abandon every other remedy for it, since one would not wish to fall because one trusted that someone would pick him up. The only good, certain, and lasting defenses are those that depend on you yourself and your own ability.

> **Key takeaway.** Italian rulers fell through their own neglect of arms and of their people, not through bad luck. The only safe defenses depend on you.

### Chapter 25: What Fortune Can Do in Human Affairs

Machiavelli admits that many have held and still hold the opinion that worldly affairs are governed by fortune and by God, so that men with all their prudence cannot correct them, and that there is no remedy. He has sometimes been inclined to this view himself, especially in recent times, with the great variations of events seen every day beyond human conjecture. Nonetheless, so that our free will is not destroyed, he judges it may be true that fortune is the arbiter of half our actions, but that she still leaves the other half, or nearly, to us.

He compares fortune to a violent river, which when it is angry floods the plains, tears down trees and buildings, and carries soil from one place to another. Everyone flees before it, yielding without being able to resist. Yet when the weather is calm, men can make provision with dikes and embankments, so that when it rises again, either the river goes into a canal or its force is less wild and dangerous. It is the same with fortune, which shows her power where there is no organized valor to resist her, and turns her forces where she knows no dikes have been made to hold her.

He observes that a prince may prosper one day and fall the next without any change in his nature or qualities, which he believes arises from the causes already discussed: a prince who relies entirely on fortune is ruined when she changes. He thinks the one who succeeds is the one whose way of proceeding matches the times, and the one who fails is the one whose way is out of step with them. Men proceed in different ways: one with caution, another with impetuosity, one by force, another by cunning, and each may succeed. Two cautious men may reach different ends, and two men of different temper may succeed equally, because of the times. But a man cannot change his nature, and so fortune changes while men remain obstinate in their ways, and they succeed while the two agree and fail when they do not.

Julius II is the example. He acted impetuously in everything, and found times and conditions so suited to his way that he always succeeded. His attack on Bologna, while the Venetians hesitated and the King of Spain and France negotiated, was carried out in person with great fury, and the others, through fear, stood by. His short life prevented him from experiencing the opposite, for if the times had required caution, his ruin would have followed. Machiavelli concludes that fortune is changeable and men are rigid in their ways, so they succeed as long as they are in harmony with her and fail when they are not. He judges it is better to be impetuous than cautious, because fortune is a woman, and if one wants to keep her down, it is necessary to beat and strike her, and she lets herself be won more by those who do so than by those who proceed coldly. The chapter ends with a flourish better read as rhetoric than as argument.

> **Key takeaway.** Fortune rules about half of what happens. Prepare in calm weather, and recognize that success depends on how well your temperament fits the times.

### Chapter 26: An Exhortation to Free Italy

Machiavelli asks whether the times are now ready for a new prince, and whether there is a wise and able man who could give Italy a new form that would bring honor to him and good to the people. He thinks so, since so many things are now favorable. If it was necessary for the people of Israel to be enslaved in Egypt to see Moses's ability, for the Persians to be oppressed by the Medes to see Cyrus's greatness, and for the Athenians to be scattered to see Theseus's, then now, to discover the strength of an Italian spirit, Italy needed to be brought to its present condition: more enslaved than the Hebrews, more oppressed than the Persians, and more scattered than the Athenians, without a head, without order, beaten, despoiled, torn, overrun, and ruined in every way.

The Medici house, with its fortune and ability and with the favor of God and the Church, which it now governs, could take the lead. He advises first of all that the prince provide himself with his own arms, since there can be no more faithful, true, or better soldiers. Italians are superior in strength, dexterity, and ingenuity in single combat and in small groups, but fail in armies, because of the weakness of their leaders, since those who know are not obeyed, and each thinks he knows. The Spanish and Swiss infantry, though thought to be terrible, each has a defect that a third kind of infantry could not only resist but be sure of defeating. The Spanish cannot withstand cavalry, and the Swiss are afraid of infantry that fights as obstinately as they do. A new kind of troop and a new order of battle would give a new prince reputation and greatness.

The opportunity must not be allowed to pass, so that Italy may after so long a time see her redeemer. Machiavelli says he cannot express with what love he would be received in every province that has suffered from foreign floods, with what thirst for vengeance, with what stubborn faith, with what devotion, with what tears. No door would be closed to him, no people would refuse him obedience, and no envy would oppose him. He ends with a verse from Petrarch saying that virtue will take up arms against fury, and that the ancient valor in Italian hearts is not yet dead.

> **Key takeaway.** The book ends as a plea. Italy needs its own arms, a leader whose character fits the moment, and a prince bold enough to act before the chance passes.

## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The thread through the twenty-six chapters is dependence. Every kind of state is ranked by how much it depends on others, whether on fortune, allies, mercenaries, nobles, or the goodwill of the people. The durable cases are those where the prince's power rests on his own arms and on a population that prefers his rule to the alternatives.

A second thread is method. Machiavelli reasons from cases: he asks what happened, then what the ruler could have done, and then what a similar ruler should do. He is repeatedly more interested in outcomes than in the moral status of the actions. A third is timing. Chapters 3, 7, 8, and 25 all concern when to act, whether to be cruel at the start, how soon to treat a disease, and whether the times suit the ruler's temper.

### Strengths and limits

The book is strongest in its candor about incentives. People who gain from a regime defend it, and people who lose from a change resist it. Hired forces and borrowed allies have different interests from yours. These claims have aged well, and the analysis of why mercenaries fail and why the people are a sturdier base than the nobles is concrete and checkable against the cases he cites.

It is thinner in other respects. The evidence is a few cases chosen to fit the argument, mostly from Machiavelli's own time and the ancient world. The advice on cruelty and deceit sits uneasily with his own point that a prince must avoid hatred. And his model is a single ruler, so it says little about institutions that outlast one person, which he explored in his longer *Discourses on Livy*. *Where I'd push back:* the book's reputation for endorsing evil comes from how bluntly it describes what works. It is better read as a warning about what rulers do than a recommendation. Some readers, beginning with Rousseau, have argued that it is a veiled satire aimed at teaching peoples what rulers are like, though most scholars read it as sincere advice.

### Applying it

1. **Ask what you depend on.** List what your position rests on, whether people, contracts, a single customer, or borrowed credibility, and decide which dependencies you would regret.
2. **Spot problems early.** Chapter 3's consumptive illness is a good rule for any project: small issues are cheap to fix and hard to see, and big ones are easy to see and costly to fix.
3. **Judge by results, not by intentions.** Chapter 15 asks you to plan for how people behave, not how they should.
4. **Check who advises you.** Chapters 22 and 23 suggest the quality of your advisers is the first evidence of your own judgment, and that advice should be invited, not volunteered.
5. **Prepare in calm weather.** Chapter 25's dikes are a reminder that resilience has to be built before the flood.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 3, 6, 15, 17, 18, and 25. Chapter 3 gives the concrete rules for holding new territory, Chapter 6 gives the argument about reformers, Chapters 15, 17, and 18 are the famous ones on conduct, and Chapter 25 is the book's view of luck.
