*The Prince* has the reputation of a handbook for villains. Read it chapter by chapter and it is closer to a field report from someone who watched rulers rise and fall in the Italy of his day, and who wanted to write down what actually separated the ones who held on from the ones who did not.

Machiavelli wrote it around 1513, after he lost his post in the Florentine republic when the Medici returned, and dedicated it to a Medici ruler. It circulated in manuscript and was printed in 1532, five years after his death. Short quotes here follow W. K. Marriott's 1908 translation, which is in the public domain.

## The whole book in one pass

The book has a plain structure. The first eleven chapters sort kinds of states and ask how each is won and held. Chapters 12 through 14 turn to armies. Chapters 15 through 19 are about the prince's own conduct and reputation. Chapters 20 through 23 handle the practical machinery of rule: fortresses, reputation, ministers, and advisers. The last three chapters ask why Italian rulers keep losing their states, how much fortune governs events, and whether someone can finally free Italy.

Three ideas hold it together.

1. **Judge by what happens, not by what ought to happen.** A ruler who plans for how people should behave is ruined by how they do.
2. **Power rests on your own arms and on the people, not on borrowed strength.** Mercenaries, allies, and luck are loans that come due.
3. **Skill means matching your method to the times.** Fortune governs part of events, and character and preparation decide how much damage or benefit it does.

## How states are won and held

**Chapter 1** sorts states into republics and principalities, and principalities into hereditary ones and new ones. **Chapter 2** says hereditary states are the easiest to hold: a ruler of ordinary ability keeps his place by not breaking with his ancestors' ways and by adapting prudently to events, and even if he is ousted he can usually return when the usurper stumbles.

**Chapter 3** is about states you add to one you already have, and it is full of specifics. New subjects welcome a change in hope of improvement, and then find they were wrong. The conqueror should live in the new territory or plant colonies there, because colonies hurt only a few dispossessed people, who are poor and scattered, while garrisons burden everyone. He should ally with the weaker neighbors and cut down the powerful, and never let a strong outsider in. Machiavelli compares political trouble to a consumptive illness: easy to cure and hard to diagnose at the start, easy to diagnose and nearly impossible to cure later. He then lists five errors that cost Louis XII of France his Italian gains: he destroyed the minor powers, strengthened an already strong one, brought in a powerful foreigner, did not live there, and did not plant colonies.

**Chapter 4** asks why Darius's kingdom did not rebel after Alexander conquered it and died. His answer is structural. A state ruled by one prince with servants, like the Turkish sultan's, is hard to enter but easy to hold once taken. A state ruled by a prince with independent barons, like France, is easy to enter and hard to hold. **Chapter 5** covers cities used to their own laws. There are three options: destroy them, live in them, or leave them their laws and take tribute through a friendly local oligarchy. He warns that free cities never forget their liberty.

**Chapter 6** is about rulers who rise by their own arms and ability, with Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, and Theseus as models. They needed only opportunity, which gave them the material to shape. Machiavelli notes that introducing a new order of things is the hardest and riskiest thing a leader can attempt, because the reformer's enemies are everyone who profited from the old order, while his defenders are lukewarm. Hence the line about Savonarola and others:

> "All armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed ones have been destroyed."
>
> — Chapter 6

**Chapter 7** is the counterexample, rulers who rise by other people's arms and fortune, and its hero is Cesare Borgia, who rose through his father Pope Alexander VI. Machiavelli praises his foundations: he installed a harsh governor, Remirro de Orco, to bring order to the Romagna, then had him cut in two and left in the town square to satisfy and stun the people. Cesare fell when his father died and he was himself gravely ill, and when he backed the wrong man in the next papal election. Machiavelli's verdict is that he would give a new prince no better precepts than Cesare's actions.

**Chapter 8** covers those who gain power by crime, using Agathocles of Syracuse and Oliverotto of Fermo. Machiavelli separates cruelty well used, done once at the start and for security, from cruelty badly used, small at first and growing. **Chapter 9** covers the civil principality, where power comes from the people or the nobles. The people only want not to be oppressed, while the nobles want to oppress, so a prince who builds on the people has the sturdier base, and he can do without any given noble.

**Chapter 10** offers a test of strength: can the prince stand alone with his own troops and money? The German free cities are his model, with their walls, artillery, and stored supplies. **Chapter 11** is about church states, which are held by ancient religious custom and so need neither ability nor luck. Machiavelli recounts how Alexander VI and Julius II made the Church powerful and hopes the current pope will make it great by goodness.

> **Remember this.** Machiavelli ranks states by how they are held, not just how they are won. The most durable power rests on your own strength and on a people who do not want you gone.

## Arms

**Chapter 12** opens with a claim that frames the rest: the main foundations of any state are good laws and good arms, and since good arms matter more, he starts there. Mercenaries, he says, are disunited, ambitious, undisciplined, and unfaithful, brave among friends and cowards against enemies. They are paid to fight but not to die.

> "Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous."
>
> — Chapter 12

He blames the ruin of Italy on this habit, which let a foreign king take the country, as the saying goes, with chalk in hand.

**Chapter 13** covers auxiliaries and mixed forces, meaning troops borrowed from a stronger ally. He tells the story of David refusing Saul's armor because it hampered him, and of Cesare Borgia's progression from French auxiliaries, to mercenaries, to his own men. The point is that borrowed strength makes the borrower weaker. **Chapter 14** says a prince should have no aim or thought but war, in peace even more than in war. He should keep his troops trained, hunt to learn the terrain, and read history for models, as Scipio imitated Cyrus.

> **Remember this.** Machiavelli distrusts every kind of borrowed force. A state defended by hired or lent troops is only as secure as its landlord's goodwill.

## Reputation and conduct

**Chapter 15** is where the book's method becomes explicit. Many writers have imagined republics and princedoms that never existed, he says, and the gap between how people live and how they ought to live is so wide that whoever neglects what is done for what ought to be done learns ruin rather than preservation. A prince, then, must learn how not to be good when necessary, and need not worry about vices that are required to keep his state.

**Chapter 16** argues that a prince should accept being called stingy. Generosity with your own money drains it and forces heavy taxes, which breeds hatred. Be open-handed with other people's property gained in conquest, and thrifty with your own, so you can fight wars without burdening the people.

**Chapter 17** takes up cruelty and mercy and the question of whether it is better to be loved or feared. Ideally both, but if only one is possible, fear is safer, because people are fickle and loyal only while it costs them nothing. The one thing to avoid is being hated, which means never taking people's property or their women, since men forget the death of a father sooner than the loss of an inheritance. Cesare's cruelty united the Romagna and was kinder than Florence's inaction, and Hannibal's cruelty kept a mixed army together, while Scipio's leniency led to mutiny.

**Chapter 18** asks whether a prince should keep his word. Sometimes not, if keeping it works against him and the reasons for the promise are gone. A ruler needs the nature of both the lion and the fox, the lion to frighten wolves and the fox to recognize traps. He must appear merciful, faithful, humane, upright, and religious, and be able to act otherwise when needed. People judge by results, and most see only appearances.

> "Everyone sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are."
>
> — Chapter 18

**Chapter 19** says a prince must avoid being hated and despised. He should delegate unpopular tasks like punishment, keep favors for himself, and avoid looking changeable, frivolous, or irresolute. Machiavelli surveys the Roman emperors to show how those who offended the soldiers or the people fell to conspiracies, while those who held both in check survived.

> **Remember this.** Chapters 15 through 19 are about perception. A ruler is judged on results and on reputation, and the one thing that cannot be recovered is the people's hatred.

## The machinery of rule

**Chapter 20** asks whether fortresses are useful, and answers that it depends on the prince's situation, but the best fortress of all is not to be hated by the people. **Chapter 21** is on gaining reputation, and the model is Ferdinand of Aragon, who always had a great enterprise under way and kept his subjects occupied and admiring. Machiavelli adds that a prince is esteemed when he is a true friend or an open enemy, because neutrality usually pleases neither side, and that he should honor those with ability.

**Chapter 22** says the first guess at a prince's intelligence is the quality of the ministers around him. He describes three kinds of minds: one that understands by itself, one that recognizes what others understand, and one that does neither. A minister who thinks more of himself than of the prince is not a good one. **Chapter 23** deals with flatterers, whom a prince avoids by choosing wise advisers and letting them speak freely, but only when he asks. Then he decides for himself and holds to the decision.

## Fortune and Italy

**Chapter 24** is the verdict on Italy's rulers. They lost their states, Machiavelli says, through negligence about arms, hostility from their people, or failure to secure the nobles, not through bad luck. The only good defenses, he adds, are those that depend on you and your own ability.

**Chapter 25** takes on fortune. He compares her to a raging river that floods when it likes, and the remedy is to build dikes and channels in calm weather. Fortune, he suggests, governs about half of what we do and leaves the rest to us. Success comes from matching your temperament to the times, and the bold Julius II prospered because his impetuosity fit his moment. People who cannot change their character fail when the times change. The chapter ends with the notorious claim that fortune is a woman who yields to those who treat her roughly, a remark better read as a rhetorical flourish than as an argument.

**Chapter 26** closes with a call to the Medici to free Italy from foreign domination. Machiavelli argues that Italians excel in single combat, fail only through poor leadership and weak armies, and need a new prince, new military institutions, and their own troops.

> **Remember this.** The book ends with its method restated as a plea: your own arms, a well-chosen moment, and a leader whose character fits the times.

## The pattern underneath

The thread through the twenty-six chapters is dependence. Every kind of state is ranked by how much it depends on others, whether on fortune, allies, mercenaries, nobles, or the goodwill of the people. The durable cases are those where the prince's power rests on his own arms and on a population that prefers his rule to the alternatives.

A second thread is method. Machiavelli reasons from cases, and he asks what happened, then what the ruler could have done, and then what a similar ruler should do. He is repeatedly more interested in outcomes than in the moral status of the actions. A third is timing: Chapters 3, 7, 8, and 25 all concern when to act, whether to be cruel at the start, how soon to treat a disease, and whether the times suit the ruler's temper.

## Where the book is strong, and where it's thin

The book is strongest in its candor about incentives. People who gain from a regime defend it, and people who lose from a change resist it. Hired forces and borrowed allies have different interests from yours. These claims have aged well, and the analysis of why mercenaries fail and why the people are a sturdier base than the nobles is concrete and checkable against the cases he cites.

It is thinner in other respects. The evidence is a few cases chosen to fit the argument, mostly from Machiavelli's own time and the ancient world. The advice on cruelty and deceit sits uneasily with his own point that a prince must avoid hatred. And his model is a single ruler, so it says little about institutions that outlast one person, which he explored in his longer *Discourses on Livy*. *Where I'd push back:* the book's reputation for endorsing evil comes from how bluntly it describes what works. It is better read as a warning about what rulers do than a recommendation. Some readers, beginning with Rousseau, have argued that it is a veiled satire aimed at teaching peoples what rulers are like, though most scholars read it as sincere advice.

## What to do with this

1. **Ask what you depend on.** List what your position rests on, whether people, contracts, a single customer, or borrowed credibility, and decide which dependencies you would regret.
2. **Spot problems early.** Chapter 3's consumptive illness is a good rule for any project: small issues are cheap to fix and hard to see, and big ones are easy to see and costly to fix.
3. **Judge by results, not by intentions.** Chapter 15 asks you to plan for how people behave, not how they should.
4. **Check who advises you.** Chapters 22 and 23 suggest the quality of your advisers is the first evidence of your own judgment, and that advice should be invited, not volunteered.
5. **Prepare in calm weather.** Chapter 25's dikes are a reminder that resilience has to be built before the flood.

## If you only read parts of it

Read Chapters 3, 6, 15, 17, 18, and 25. Chapter 3 gives the concrete rules for holding new territory, Chapter 6 gives the argument about reformers, Chapters 15, 17, and 18 are the famous ones on conduct, and Chapter 25 is the book's view of luck. The whole book is short enough to read in a few sittings.
