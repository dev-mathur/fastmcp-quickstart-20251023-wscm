## Overview

*The 4 Disciplines of Execution* (4DX) is a book about the gap between knowing what to do and getting it done. Chris McChesney, Sean Covey and Jim Huling, all of FranklinCovey, argue that most strategies fail not because they are wrong but because the people who must change their behavior are buried in the urgent demands of their day jobs, which the authors call the *whirlwind*. Drawing on more than fifteen hundred implementations, they offer an "operating system" of four disciplines for achieving a few goals that really matter in spite of the whirlwind: focus on the wildly important, act on the lead measures, keep a compelling scoreboard and create a cadence of accountability.

The book's own sections shape the chapter numbering used here. The unnumbered opening material (foreword, a note on strategy and execution, and a letter from a CEO) is summarized first. Chapter 1 explains the real problem with execution. Section 1 gives one chapter to each discipline (Chapters 2 to 5). Section 2 is a field guide for installing 4DX with your own team: what to expect (Chapter 6), installing each of the four disciplines (Chapters 7 to 10) and automating the process (Chapter 11). Section 3 turns to whole organizations, with leader stories (Chapter 12), translating strategy into WIGs (Chapter 13), rollout (Chapter 14) and FAQs (Chapter 15). Chapter 16 applies the method at home, and Chapter 17 closes with next steps. The book does not number its chapters, so the numbers follow the order of its major headings. The glossary and notes are omitted.

Three ideas hold the book together.

1. **Focus beats breadth.** Teams that pursue one or two wildly important goals at a time, with a clear finish line ("from X to Y by when"), accomplish more than teams that pursue ten priorities at once.
2. **Lead measures change the outcome.** Lag measures report results that are already history, so teams should act on lead measures, which are predictive and within their influence, and track both on a simple scoreboard.
3. **Weekly accountability keeps the goal alive.** A short, regular meeting where each person makes and reports specific commitments is the mechanism that defeats the whirlwind, and it works best when people choose their own commitments.

## Foreword and Opening Notes

The book opens with a foreword by Harvard professor Clayton Christensen, who recalls Intel's Andy Grove snapping at him, "I asked you how to do it, and you told me what I should do." Christensen's own research, he admits, concerns the *what* of strategy. The authors' contribution is a theory of the *how* of execution, studied over many companies and years, which he compares to making "movies" rather than snapshots. A short opening piece, "Strategy and Execution," notes that leaders say execution is their biggest struggle, yet business education studies strategy far more, and that once you've decided what to do, the challenge is getting people to do it at the level of excellence you need. A letter from Alex Azar, then president of Lilly USA, recounts attending a three-hour meeting where a team reported results from piloting the 4 Disciplines. Lilly's managed healthcare unit exceeded both goals while employee engagement scores rose during a reorganization, and he says it changed the way he leads.

> **Key takeaway.** Strategy tells you what to do, but the hard and neglected part is how to get people to do it, and that is what this book sets out to teach.

## The Real Problem with Execution

### Chapter 1: The Real Problem with Execution

The chapter opens with three stories of leaders who faced the same challenge. B. J. Walker, the new commissioner of Georgia's Department of Human Services in 2004, inherited twenty thousand demoralized employees, constant media scrutiny over children's deaths and accidents, and was the sixth leader in five years. Her team cut repeat child-maltreatment cases by 60 percent in under eighteen months. The Bethesda Marriott near company headquarters earned the highest guest satisfaction scores in the hotel's thirty-year history, and Eli Lilly's managed healthcare team hit two demanding goals together. Despite different industries, the authors say, the challenge was the same: executing a strategy that required a significant change in the behavior of many people. Their common solution was the 4 Disciplines of Execution (4DX), tested in more than fifteen hundred implementations before the book was written.

**The real challenge.** Strategies fall into two kinds. *Stroke-of-the-pen* strategies, such as a capital investment, a new compensation system or a media buy, can be carried out just by authorizing them. *Behavioral-change* strategies need people to do something different, like greeting every customer within thirty seconds or adopting a new CRM system. A Bain study found that about 65 percent of initiatives required significant behavioral change from front-line employees, which managers often fail to plan for. Jim Stuart's line captures the point: "To achieve a goal you have never achieved before, you must start doing things you have never done before." Leaders tend to blame people, but following W. Edwards Deming, the authors argue that when most people behave a particular way most of the time, the problem is the system, which the leader owns. Their surveys found that only one employee in seven could name even one of the organization's top goals, only 51 percent were passionate about their team's goal, 81 percent were not held accountable for regular progress, and 87 percent had no clear idea what to do to achieve it. They did not want to tell leaders to "boil the ocean" by fixing everything, so they looked for a deeper cause, like "fish discovering water."

**The whirlwind.** The real enemy of execution is your day job, which they call the *whirlwind*: the energy needed to keep the operation running day to day, which also makes it hard to do anything new. Because the whirlwind is urgent and acts on you while important goals are ones you act on, "urgency will win every time." Initiatives typically die by "slow suffocation," and as one retail executive put it, the problem is not dragons but "gnats." Ignoring the urgent can kill you today, while ignoring the important can kill you tomorrow. A teacher too swamped with essays to listen to a new test-score goal shows that the person backing out of the room is not sabotaging anything, just surviving. The 4 Disciplines are not for managing the whirlwind but for executing the most critical strategy in spite of it.

**The four disciplines in brief.** The authors summarize each one:
- **Discipline 1: Focus on the wildly important.** The more you try to do, the less you accomplish. Pick one or two wildly important goals (WIGs), as Lance Armstrong devoted himself to the Tour de France. Like sunlight focused through a magnifying glass, focus ignites results.
- **Discipline 2: Act on the lead measures.** The discipline of leverage. Lag measures such as revenue or profit describe results that are already history, while lead measures track the new behaviors that drive them. A good one is predictive and influenceable, as in weight loss, where pounds lost is lag and calories and exercise hours are lead.
- **Discipline 3: Keep a compelling scoreboard.** The discipline of engagement. "People play differently when they're keeping score." A simple players' scoreboard shows at a glance whether the team is winning, unlike the complex coach's scoreboard.
- **Discipline 4: Create a cadence of accountability.** "Where execution really happens." The team meets at least weekly for twenty to thirty minutes, reports on last week's commitments, reviews the scoreboard and makes new commitments. The father whose teenage daughter asked "Are we still doing that?" about washing the car after he missed two Saturdays shows how quickly accountability fades.

A luxury hotel chain with a WIG of 97 percent guest retention illustrates all four, with housekeepers recording guests' preferences, including replacing a partly smoked cigar months later in another hotel. The disciplines rest on principles (focus, leverage, engagement and accountability), not practices. The book has three sections: the disciplines, step-by-step installation with your team, and rolling them out across an organization. The authors add three warnings: 4DX "says easy, does hard," it is counterintuitive, and it is an operating system, a matched set rather than a menu.

> **Key takeaway.** Executing a strategy that needs behavior change is hard because the whirlwind of daily work swallows it, and the 4 Disciplines are rules for executing in spite of that whirlwind.

## Section 1: The 4 Disciplines of Execution

Section 1 presents the four disciplines one by one. The framework is drawn so that teams "execute from right to left": regular accountability for lead measures drives achievement of the wildly important goal. The compelling scoreboard sits at the center, showing the results for all to see, and the cadence of accountability surrounds the rest and holds it together.

### Chapter 2: Discipline 1: Focus on the Wildly Important

The first discipline is to focus your finest effort on one or two goals that will make all the difference instead of giving mediocre effort to dozens. Leaders agree they need focus, yet an inability to focus is "epidemic." The authors stress that this is not about shrinking the whirlwind. It is about narrowing the number of goals you try to achieve *beyond* the whirlwind. The law of diminishing returns applies: a team can often achieve two or three goals beyond the whirlwind, but with four to ten it will achieve only one or two, and with eleven to twenty it will lose all focus.

The principle underneath is that human beings are wired to do one thing at a time with excellence. MIT neuroscientist Earl Miller says concentrating on two tasks overloads the brain's processing capacity. Clifford Nass warns that multitasking circuits strengthen while deep-thinking circuits erode, and Jordan Grafman says multitasking makes us less able to reason through a problem. The image is an air traffic controller who knows about every plane on the radar but, right now, lands one at a time. Other goals stay on the radar, and some should never have taken off.

**The leader's challenge.** Why do leaders keep expanding goals? Quoting *Pogo*, "We have met the enemy and he is us." Ambitious, creative people want to do more, not less. They hedge their bets so that no one can question their effort. Most of all, they have to say no to good ideas, which arrive one at a time and each look compelling. The authors' message is "There will always be more good ideas than there is capacity to execute." Tim Cook told Apple's shareholders, "We say no to good ideas every day," and a manufacturer with forty phone models competed against a rival with one. Stephen R. Covey is quoted: you say no by having "a bigger 'yes' burning inside." The second trap is trying to turn everything in the whirlwind into a goal. It is fine to spend 80 percent of energy sustaining the whirlwind, but pressing evenly on every dial is like making holes in paper with all your fingers rather than punching one finger through.

**Identifying your WIG.** Don't ask "What's most important?" because every urgent priority argues its case, as quality, production and safety do in a plant leadership team. Ask instead: "If every other area of our operation remained at its current level of performance, what is the one area where change would have the greatest impact?" A WIG can come from within the whirlwind (something badly broken or a strength worth leveraging, such as raising patient satisfaction from the 85th to the 95th percentile) or from outside it (a new product or strategic repositioning). Once achieved, a WIG goes back into the whirlwind, which becomes higher-performing. For a thrift-store chain, returning to its mission "to promote self-reliance among the disabled and displaced" produced a WIG of placing disabled workers in sustainable jobs outside the organization.

**Focusing the organization.** Four rules govern how a larger organization applies the discipline:
1. **No team focuses on more than two WIGs at once**, which acts as a governor on an engine.
2. **The battles you choose must win the war.** Lower-level WIGs must ensure the success of higher ones, not merely align with them. Ask for the fewest battles needed. In an Internet financial services firm needing revenue to rise from $160 million to $200 million, the technology team's battle was uninterrupted service.
3. **Senior leaders can veto but not dictate.** The top sets the overall WIG for clarity, and teams choose their own for engagement.
4. **All WIGs need a finish line in the form "from X to Y by when,"** such as raising the share of revenue from new products from 15 to 21 percent by December 31. Vague goals like "Improve inventory processing" have no way to say when you've won, whereas "increase inventory turns from eight to ten by December 31" does.

**Shooting for the moon.** NASA in 1958 had eight sprawling goals and no finish line, while the Soviets led the space race. In 1961 President Kennedy's "land a man on the moon and return him safely to the earth before this decade is out" supplied an X, Y and when. NASA chose three battles to win the war (navigation, propulsion, life support), and accountability and morale both rose, since narrowing focus throws the "Game on!" switch. Discipline 1 sharpens strategy by turning vague intent into specific finish lines. Quoting Steve Jobs, "I'm as proud of what we don't do as I am of what we do." It's "common sense, not common practice," like the boy in Aesop's fable whose fistful of hazelnuts would not fit through the neck of the pot.

> **Key takeaway.** Narrowing to one or two wildly important goals, each with a from-X-to-Y-by-when finish line, concentrates effort and raises both accountability and morale, while saying yes to everything guarantees mediocre results.

### Chapter 3: Discipline 2: Act on the Lead Measures

Discipline 2 is the discipline of leverage: apply disproportionate energy to the activities that drive your lead measures, which in turn move the lag measures. Discipline 1 breaks the top goal into targets until every team owns a WIG. Discipline 2 defines the leveraged actions that will achieve it. It does not produce a detailed long-term plan, which is rigid and ends up gathering dust. Instead, the team defines daily or weekly measures and each week identifies the most important actions to drive them, a just-in-time plan that adapts while staying on the WIG. The conventional approach is to watch lag measures, "stress out" and "bite your nails"; the 4DX principle is to move the lead measures.

**Lag versus lead.** A lag measure is the result you want, and by the time you see it the result has already happened. Lead measures "foretell the result" and have two characteristics: they are **predictive** and **influenceable** by the team without significant dependence on another team. For a WIG to raise corn production from 200 tons to 300 tons, rainfall is predictive but not influenceable, while soil quality and fertilization rates qualify. In weight loss (190 to 175 pounds by May 30), diet and exercise qualify. The car analogy: you can't control roadside breakdowns, but you can control routine maintenance.

**Why lead measures feel counterintuitive.** Leaders have spent careers fixated on lag measures, such as sales leaders on sales, parents on grades and dieters on the scale, because lag data is easier to get and they measure success. Lead data, like calories eaten and burned, is harder. The authors warn against dismissing it as "diet and exercise" platitudes: "Everyone knows you should diet and exercise, but the people who actually measure... are the ones actually losing weight." Deming likened managing by financial data to "driving a car by looking in the rearview mirror." Tracking leads also removes surprise: you already know the lag will move, the way you know your weight after meeting daily diet and exercise targets.

**Defining lead measures.** A beverage company's water-bottling plant, with a WIG of increasing annual production from 175 million to 185 million liters, first proposed monthly and then daily production, which are only more frequent lags. The production manager then named the real causes: shifts without full crews and machine downtime. The lead measures became increasing shifts with full crews from 80 to 95 percent and compliance with preventive maintenance from 72 to 100 percent, and output grew faster than expected. The point was that they already knew these things but weren't doing or measuring them. Lead measures act as a lever on a giant rock, since they are influenceable (you can move the lever) and predictive (when it moves, the rock moves). To choose levers, look at who has achieved the goal and what they did differently, and pick the 80/20 activities. In an Atlanta department store pressured by discounters and new chains, shoe-department managers studied their top salesperson, who sold three times the average, and set three tracked behaviors: show at least four pairs of shoes to every customer, write thank-you notes, and invite every customer to open a charge account. Staff tracked themselves on a simple spreadsheet. Revenue ended 2 percent above the prior year, a ten-point improvement in three months. The lead measures were already in the business; nobody tracked them. The Oakland Athletics, with a fraction of the Yankees' budget, ran the same logic: General Manager Sandy Alderson and then Billy Beane asked what produces wins, found that getting on base predicted runs better than home runs, recruited undervalued on-base players, and kept the fifth-best record in baseball while ranking twenty-fourth in payroll (the story of *Moneyball*).

**Tracking lead data.** Younger Brothers Construction set a WIG of reducing safety incidents from 7 to 1 percent. More training was influenceable but not predictive, since people had already been trained, so they measured compliance with six safety standards (hard hats, gloves, boots, eyewear, scaffolds and roof braces). Supervisors had to observe compliance physically in spite of the whirlwind, and the company achieved its best safety record in thirty years. The lesson: lead data is harder to get than lag data, but you must pay the price. A preflight checklist, adopted after Major Pete Hill's 1935 crash from forgetting to unlock the elevators, is the classic lead measure: predictive of a safe landing and influenceable by pilots. Leaders who insist on more than two WIGs change their minds once they see how hard it is to drive the leads.

**Lead measures and engagement.** Beth Wood, a grocery manager, asked her bakery manager Bob to help raise sales and got a sarcastic "Knock yourself out." Bob wasn't lazy; he could see the rock but not the lever. When Beth asked instead what one thing teams could do above the whirlwind to improve sales most, they agreed on reducing out-of-stocks, which Bob's team could influence with extra shelf checks, better back-room organization and reordering. Defining lead measures is a "strategic bet," and it engages people as strategic partners. At the *Savannah Morning News*, the ad team replaced a scattershot effort with three lead measures (new-customer contacts, reactivation calls, and upsell offers), closed its revenue gap, and its parent company rolled 4DX out to forty other newspapers.

> **Key takeaway.** Lead measures, which are both predictive of the goal and influenceable by the team, give you the leverage lag measures never can, and the data to track them is worth the effort.

### Chapter 4: Discipline 3: Keep a Compelling Scoreboard

Discipline 3 is the discipline of engagement: make sure everyone knows the score at all times, because "people play differently when they are keeping score." Teams that merely understand lead and lag measures conceptually differ sharply from teams that see their score, since measures not captured on a visual scoreboard disappear into the whirlwind. The authors open with a Northrop Grumman team that was building Coast Guard cutters just after Hurricane Katrina. Their local high school football game had been dull and silent because the scoreboard had blown down, so nobody knew the score, the down or the time. Without a scoreboard, energy dissipates and the team goes back to business as usual.

**Coach's scoreboard versus players' scoreboard.** Leaders already have scoreboards in the form of complex spreadsheets of lag data, historical trends and projections. That is a *coach's scoreboard*, useful to the leader, and what Discipline 3 requires is a *players' scoreboard* designed solely to engage the people doing the work, with a few simple graphs showing where we need to be and where we are now. In five seconds or less anyone should be able to tell whether the team is winning. Teenagers shooting baskets show the difference: when they keep score you see intensity, teamwork, aggressive defense and celebration. Jim Stuart put it simply: "The fundamental purpose of a players' scoreboard is to motivate the players to win." The emphasis matters: it is people keeping score themselves, not the leader keeping score for them.

**Four characteristics of a compelling scoreboard.**
1. **Is it simple?** A football scoreboard shows about six pieces of data, while the coach on the sideline tracks dozens. The players' board shows only what is needed to play the game.
2. **Can I see it easily?** A scoreboard that lives on a computer or the back of an office door is out of sight, out of mind. Visibility also drives accountability. A union shift at a juice-bottling plant in Michigan skipped lunch to move up the board, and a night shift looked at it first thing at midnight to compare against the day shift. Dispersed teams can use an electronic board.
3. **Does it show lead and lag measures?** Seeing both lets the team watch the bet play out and see the lag move because of their efforts.
4. **Can I tell at a glance if I'm winning?** This is the five-second rule. One client's trade-show team had a chart of exhibitors booked until it added a mountain goat showing where the team should be, which made it obvious they had been losing for two weeks and that the goal was getting harder. Winning requires knowing where you are now and where you should be now. If you can't tell, "it's not a game, it's just data." Capable leaders often reply, "I'll need a few minutes to pull it together," because they have too much data and little sense of which matters most.

**The game, not the scoreboard.** What compels is the game the scoreboard represents, as sports fans never say "What an amazing scoreboard!" Teams stuck in the whirlwind play "not to lose"; with a lead-and-lag bet, they get a winnable game. A remote, outdated plant in Canada that had missed its production target for twenty-five years and whose quality score was in the low seventies (the company's others were in the high eighties) was transformed when scoreboards "turned on the lights." In a hockey-playing culture, the night shift wanted bragging rights over the day shift. The quality score went from 74 to 94, and production beat the target by 4,000 metric tons, adding at least $5 million to profit.

**4DX and engagement.** The authors admit they did not initially grasp the engagement effect. In early implementations, resisters were eager to show results two months later, even though no WIG was about morale. They concluded that, although many believe engagement drives results, "results drive engagement." Winning can matter more than money, benefits or even liking the boss. They cite Frederick Herzberg ("People are most satisfied with their jobs... when those jobs give them the opportunity to experience achievement") and Amabile and Kramer's "The Power of Small Wins." Think of the time you were most excited at work and you probably felt you were winning. Discipline 1 creates a clear finish line, Discipline 2 makes it a game by giving the team a bet on lead measures, and Discipline 3 makes people care. Rah-rah morale exercises cannot compete with executing with excellence a goal that matters. The first three disciplines set up the game, but the team may still not be playing.

> **Key takeaway.** A simple, visible players' scoreboard that shows lead and lag measures and tells you within five seconds whether you're winning turns a goal into a game people want to win.

### Chapter 5: Discipline 4: Create a Cadence of Accountability

Discipline 4 is "where execution actually happens." The first three disciplines set up the game, but until this one is applied the team isn't in the game. Great teams operate with a high level of accountability, or each member goes off in a different direction and the whirlwind takes over. In most organizations accountability means the annual review or being "called on the carpet," but in a 4DX organization it means personal commitments to the whole team, followed by disciplined follow-through. The conventional model is top-down accountability to the boss. The 4DX principle is shared accountability: we are accountable to each other for the commitments we make.

**The WIG session.** The team meets at least weekly, for no more than twenty to thirty minutes, with a set agenda. The authors illustrate the stakes with two Everest expeditions. In 1996, Jon Krakauer's group came apart under blizzards and altitude, headstrong climbers struck out alone, and eight people died. In 2001, a team helping the blind climber Erik Weihenmayer held a nightly "tent meeting" to share lessons and commit to the next day's strategy. They cut a thirteen-hour crossing of the Khumbu Icefall's ladders to two hours, and eighteen team members summited in a day. Two rules are absolute. First, hold the session on the same day and time every week, because even a missed week loses momentum, and only 34 percent of employees say they meet monthly with their manager about goals. A week is a natural slice of life, short enough to stay relevant and long enough to get things done. Second, never let the whirlwind in: discuss only actions and results that move the scoreboard, and hold a staff meeting afterwards for everything else. The agenda has three parts:
1. **Account:** report on last week's commitments.
2. **Review the scoreboard:** learn from successes and failures.
3. **Plan:** clear the path and make new commitments.

**Staying focused despite the whirlwind.** Each person prepares by asking not "What is the most important thing I can do?" (which drags attention back to the whirlwind) but "What are the one or two most important things I can do this week to impact the lead measures?" Susan, a nurse manager, commits to reviewing pain-management procedures with a new supervisor and filling an open position. Tom, a salesman, commits to acquiring ten more prospects. Commitments are often not urgent or new, but they are what the whirlwind eats first. At MICARE, a Mexican coal producer, Monday-morning WIG sessions run in every department by videophone, and everyone can recite their team's WIG, a clarity Jack Welch described as being able to answer "Where are we going?" half asleep. Over seven years, lost-time accidents fell from nearly seven hundred to under sixty, and coal per worker rose from six thousand to ten thousand tons. "The more they talk, the less they did." At Younger Brothers a project manager reports on ordering scaffolding braces, reviews green compliance at 91 percent, and commits to meeting supervisors of three crews to ensure they have safety glasses. Each commitment must meet two standards: it must be a specific deliverable ("focus on" is too vague) and it must influence the lead measure. The session is like a science experiment, and leaders should ask "What can I do this week to clear the path for you?" The Minnesota Cystic Fibrosis Center, with a WIG of 100 percent lung function, tests ideas such as supplemental feedings and mist tents each week. Commitments must come from participants, since ideas that are theirs, not yours, build ownership.

**The black and the gray.** On a weekly calendar, commitments are black blocks and the whirlwind is gray. Most of the week stays gray, as it should, but the black must be driven into the gray. By Parkinson's Law, the whirlwind consumes any open time immediately. WIG sessions are the antidote to "all-gray weeks" in which you work hard and accomplish nothing.

**Engagement and accountability.** Mark McChesney's automotive design team members do the work they dreamed of and still have the department's lowest engagement. Patrick Lencioni's three signs of a miserable job (anonymity, irrelevance, immeasurement) explain it, and WIG sessions cure all three, since members are in the spotlight, can see how commitments move lead measures, and have a public scoreboard. Effects take three to four weeks to show. On the online system my4dx.com, over 75 percent of millions of commitments were kept. The accountability is personal: "Did we do what we committed to each other we would do?" At Nomaco's Tarboro plant, WIG sessions replaced manager-centered supervision, and in eighteen months the plant cut more than $1 million in costs and ran 30 percent under budget, with no lost-time accidents. People "will do almost anything to avoid disappointing their teammates." The importance you give the session decides whether it is a preseason game or a playoff.

**An innovative culture.** Structure plus novelty motivates (Edward Hallowell). Towne Park, a valet company, made the one unmeasured driver, retrieval time, a lead measure for customer satisfaction. Valets began asking guests to call ahead and moved cars forward as checkout neared. In Miami, assistant manager James McNeil committed in a WIG session to remove a four-foot concrete wall that forced valets to detour, and did so with a borrowed saw in a day. Command-and-control yields compliance, but 4DX draws on the desire to feel significant and to win.

**The power of 4DX.** Annual plans create a "planning high" and fall into obscurity, and they could never foresee that in week thirty-two crews nine, eleven and thirteen need safety glasses. Weekly just-in-time planning against lead measures can. Without a lead measure, goals look unachievable and teams retreat to the whirlwind, "playing not to lose." 4DX is an operating system that can run any strategy. The principles are timeless, as in Jean Nidetch's Weight Watchers, which has a lag goal, controllable lead measures, regular scorekeeping and a weekly meeting.

> **Key takeaway.** A short weekly meeting where each person reports on commitments, reviews the scoreboard and makes new commitments is what turns focus, leverage and engagement into actual results despite the whirlwind.

## Section 2: Installing 4DX with Your Team

Section 2 is a field guide for putting the disciplines to work with a team. It stresses that 4DX is "not a set of guidelines, but a set of disciplines," and it gives step-by-step instructions for each one, so that executives and team leaders can use it as a road map.

### Chapter 6: What to Expect

The chapter begins with Jim Dixon, general manager of Store 334, the worst of 250 stores in his grocery division. He felt like Sisyphus, pushing the same boulder up a hill each day. He "head slapped" over carts in the parking lot, broken bottles and gaps on shelves, worked sixteen days straight, and was about to be let go. His WIG was obvious (year-over-year revenue or the store closes), and his team chose lead measures for store conditions, scored daily on a ten-point scale. They posted scoreboards that morning, employees tore them down that night, and within two weeks the five departments averaged thirteen out of fifty on a scale they had created themselves. The authors promise to return to why.

**The five stages of change.** Teams generally move through five stages, illustrated by Marilyn, who leads a surgical nursing unit facing rising perioperative incidents.
1. **Getting clear.** The leader and team commit to a new level of performance and develop crystal-clear WIGs, lag and lead measures and a compelling scoreboard. Marilyn's team set a WIG of raising incident-free surgeries from 89 to 98 percent by December 31, with two lead measures: 100 percent compliance on pre-surgery audits at least thirty minutes before surgery, and double-counting surgical items after every surgery. The leader must model focus, identify high-leverage leads, create a players' scoreboard and hold WIG sessions at least weekly.
2. **Launch.** Like a rocket escaping gravity, the team needs intense leader involvement. On day one Marilyn's team struggled: the schedule change and a new checklist confused people, a nurse was out sick, senior nurses questioned the change, and newer ones slowed the audits. The authors name three groups: **models** (those who embrace it), **potentials** (those who struggle at first) and **resisters**. Trust the process.
3. **Adoption.** Resistance fades and enthusiasm grows as the leads work. Marilyn coached her potentials and counseled resisters, and the incident rate fell. Focus first on adherence to the process, then on results, make weekly commitments, track visibly, adjust, invest in potentials, and clear the path for resisters.
4. **Optimization.** The team shifts to a 4DX mindset and starts improving on its own. Marilyn's nurses proposed repositioning equipment trays, auditing the first and second operating rooms at once, and being alerted when patients were en route. Ideas that come from the team are embraced rather than resisted.
5. **Habits.** Eleven months later the unit had the lowest incident rate in the hospital's history. The behaviors became part of the whirlwind, which became more manageable. The aim of 4DX is a culture of excellent execution. Celebrate, move quickly to new WIGs, and keep the standard of sustained superior performance on lead measures.

**Moving the middle.** People respond in one of three ways: models, resisters ("there's no place to hide") and potentials, the middle 60 percent who could do more. Performance follows a normal curve, and ordinary teams are "left and loose." The aim is to make them "right and tight." Erasmus Medical Center in Rotterdam tackled hospital-acquired infections, which kill thousands annually in Europe, with a "Search and Destroy" set of lead measures, nearly eliminating them in five years, and the Dutch hospital system followed. Coach Adolph Rupp is quoted: "Whenever you see a man on top of a mountain, you can be sure he didn't fall there." Results typically improve quickly, plateau during adoption, then pay off once the habit forms.

> **Key takeaway.** Installing 4DX is a five-stage change journey (getting clear, launch, adoption, optimization, habits), and the real lever is moving the middle of your team toward the performance of your models.

### Chapter 7: Installing Discipline 1: Focus on the Wildly Important

This chapter is a four-step process for defining a team WIG. The authors argue that selecting the right WIG is crucial: leaders hesitate because they fear choosing wrongly or failing, but a WIG starts a game that matters. A client put it well: "When you work on that many goals, you actually work on none of them."

**Step 1: Consider the possibilities.** Brainstorm candidate WIGs. What you ask depends on your situation: if your team belongs to an organization with many goals, find which are most crucial; if the organization already has a top-level WIG, find how to contribute; if you are the organization, find what most advances the mission. You can brainstorm with peer leaders, with your team (which increases ownership) or alone. WIGs should be both **top-down** and **bottom-up**: only the leader can give clarity about what matters most, but "no involvement, no commitment." Three discovery questions help: which one area, if everything else holds, would we most want to improve; what are our greatest strengths that can be leveraged; and where does poor performance most threaten the overall WIG? A longer list produces a better final choice. Think "what," not "how." At a five-star hotel with a $54 million to $62 million profit WIG, departments brainstormed ideas, and the event management team listed revenue and expense options such as increasing corporate events, premium bar packages and weddings, or reducing overtime and food costs.

**Step 2: Rank by impact.** Rank ideas by their impact on the *overall* WIG, not the team's own performance. Corporate events and weddings rose to the top for the events team because they bring room bookings, meals and spa visits to the whole hotel. A drug company's regulatory affairs team, aiming to cut time to market from seven and a half years to five, debated hiring ex-regulators and lobbying governments, but then noticed that applications kept being sent back for errors, doubling approval time. Eliminating mistakes became the WIG. A Scandinavian shipping company's loading team, facing hazy goals of quality, productivity and cost, chose "double our number of container moves per hour" after learning that a Malaysian port had unloaded a giant ship in half the usual time.

**Step 3: Test top ideas.** Apply four tests. Is the team WIG **aligned** with a seamless line of sight to the overall WIG? Is it **measurable** from day one ("If you're not keeping score, you're just practicing")? Does the team own at least **80 percent of the result**, without heavy dependence on other teams (joint ownership is fine if both win or lose together)? And is it a **team game rather than a leader's game**? Reject flawed ideas, since pressure will expose the flaws.

**Step 4: Define the WIG.** Four rules:
- **Begin with a verb**, and skip long preambles (use "Cut costs" rather than "In order to drive increased value to our shareholders...").
- **Define the lag measure as "from X to Y by when,"** such as decreasing routing error rate from 11 to 4 percent by July 31.
- **Keep it simple**, because 85 percent of working adults cannot name their organization's top goals. "Raise our customer-loyalty score from 40 to 70 by December 31st" beats "enhancing and enriching our relationships."
- **Focus on what, not how.** Leave the how for lead measures.

The WIG should be both worthy and winnable, not padded with sandbagging "stretch" goals the team privately expects to hit 75 percent of. The deliverable is a team WIG and lag measure, such as the event team's "Increase revenue from corporate events from $22 million to $31 million by December 31."

**If your WIG is a project.** A lag measure of "100 percent complete" is imprecise and vulnerable to scope expansion. Link it to the business outcome, echoing Theodore Levitt's remark that "People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole." For a CRM project, add measures such as meeting 100 percent of specified marketing functions or full integration with Outlook. A WIG Builder tool and checklist close the chapter: gather input from above and below, confirm impact on the overall WIG, confirm that the team has the power to achieve it, check the format, and simplify.

> **Key takeaway.** Choose a team WIG by brainstorming widely, ranking by impact on the overall goal, testing for alignment, measurability and ownership, and stating it as a simple "from X to Y by when."

### Chapter 8: Installing Discipline 2: Act on the Lead Measures

The authors call lead measures "the secret of excellence in execution," and also the single hardest part of installing 4DX. Their examples pair lag and lead measures that are both predictive and influenceable: a hospital quality team cutting mortality from 4 to 2 percent by evaluating susceptible patients twice daily against pneumonia protocols, a shipping dispatch team reducing trucking costs 12 percent by ensuring 90 percent of trips are fully loaded, and a restaurant raising average check 10 percent by suggesting the cocktail of the day to 90 percent of tables. Three things make leads difficult: they are **counterintuitive** (leaders fixate on lag), **hard to track** (behaviors need a tracking system, which you may have to invent), and they often **look too simple**. A retailer's lead measure of twenty or fewer out-of-stocks a week seems like something they should already be doing, but inconsistently applied, it sends customers away. Leads often simply close the gap between knowing and doing.

**Two types of lead measures.** *Small outcomes* focus the team on a weekly result and leave methods open ("Limit out of stocks to twenty or fewer per week"), and the team is accountable for the result. *Leveraged behaviors* track specific behaviors everyone performs consistently ("Complete 2 additional shelf reviews each day"), and the team is accountable for the behavior. Younger Brothers chose the small outcome of compliance with safety standards but could have started with the single behavior of wearing safety boots. Both are valid; the question is which suits your team.

**Step 1: Consider the possibilities.** Brainstorm widely. Useful questions ask what you could do that you have never done before, what strengths and pockets of excellence you can leverage (what do your best performers do differently?), and what inconsistencies hold you back. A grocery store aiming for 5 percent sales growth might greet shoppers during rush hour, take orders by text, copy the bakery's service checklist, review shelves every two hours or keep queues to two customers. Stay on ideas that drive the WIG. The model example is 3M's 15 percent rule: researchers spend 15 percent of their time on projects of their choice, which Jim Collins credits with a stream of innovations such as Post-it Notes.

**Step 2: Rank by impact.** Susan's hotel events team narrowed a list to three: more site visits (nearly every visit led to a proposal and a contract), upselling the premium bar package (the highest margins), and more high-quality proposals. A warning: "We need to do all of these things" dissipates pressure, since "a lever must move a lot to move the rock a little."

**Step 3: Test top ideas.** Six questions:
1. **Is it predictive?** The first and most important test.
2. **Is it influenceable?** The team has at least 80 percent control, so "win back former clients" is a lag but "contact former clients lost to other hotels and generate persuasive proposals" is a lead.
3. **Is it an ongoing process or "once and done"?** Maintaining 100 percent compliance with a banquet checklist beats a one-off training session or joining the Chamber of Commerce.
4. **Is it a leader's game or a team game?** More frequent audits fails if only the leader audits, whereas responding promptly to all audit findings engages the team.
5. **Can it be measured?** If the WIG is truly wildly important, find a way.
6. **Is it worth measuring?** A fast-food chain's inspectors who were seen as "spies" cost more in morale than they were worth.

**Step 4: Define the lead measures.** Decide whether to track team or individual performance (individual is the most accountable and demanding) and whether to track daily or weekly (at least weekly). Choose a quantitative standard based on the WIG's urgency: Younger Brothers set 97 percent compliance with six standards, and a building-materials client found that three promotional e-mails worked where two didn't. If you measure an existing activity, performance must rise significantly, since doing the same things expecting different results is "insanity." Set a qualitative standard (how well) where relevant, start each lead with a verb, and keep it simple. For process-based WIGs, find the **leverage points** in the process (for example needs analysis and business cases in an eleven-step sales process) rather than trying to improve everything. Project milestones can serve as leads if they are predictive and substantial, since a milestone under about six weeks is too granular for weekly commitments.

The deliverable is a small set of lead measures. Susan's team chose two quality site visits per associate per week and upselling the premium bar package to 90 percent of events. The authors warn that teams often think "from here it's all easy," but the plan will vanish into the whirlwind within days without Discipline 3. A Lead Measure Builder and a checklist close the chapter.

> **Key takeaway.** Build lead measures by brainstorming widely, ranking by leverage, and testing for prediction, influence, ongoing behavior, team ownership and measurability, then set clear quantitative and quality standards.

### Chapter 9: Installing Discipline 3: Keep a Compelling Scoreboard

Discipline 3 is the discipline of engagement. The authors cite a FranklinCovey retail survey in which 73 percent of top-performing stores agreed that "Our success measures are visible, accessible, and continually updated," against only 33 percent of the bottom performers. Three principles anchor the chapter: people play differently when *they* keep score, a coach's scoreboard is not a players' scoreboard (a leader can guide but cannot build it without the players), and the purpose of the board is to motivate players to win. Team ownership grows with involvement in designing it.

**Step 1: Choose a theme.** Pick a format that shows the measures instantly.
- **Trend lines** are best for lag measures and "from X to Y by when." A goat marks where you should be now.
- **Speedometer** gauges suit time measures such as cycle time or retrieval time.
- **Bar charts** compare teams or groups.
- **Andon** charts use green, yellow and red signals, which suit lead measures.
- **Personalized** touches (team names, photos, cartoons) build pride. The authors have seen cardiac nurses put surgical instruments on their board, engineers add flashing lights and motorcycle-riding chefs add leather chaps.

**Step 2: Design the scoreboard.** Ask four questions. Is it **simple**? Don't add history, year-over-year comparisons, projections or general announcements. Can the team **see it easily**? Post it where people (and other teams) will see it often, or electronically if dispersed. Does it show **lead and lag measures**, both actual and target, answering "Where are we now?" and "Where should we be?" A bottling team charted weekly output against a goal and maintenance compliance, noticed that a drop in maintenance matched a drop in production, and became more consistent. Include legends, since 85 percent of team members can't name the goals. Can you **tell at a glance if you're winning** in five seconds? This is the true test.

**Step 3: Build the scoreboard.** Let the team build it, because their ownership grows with involvement. If they have little discretionary time, the leader does more, but most teams volunteer their own time. The medium (electronic sign, poster, whiteboard, chalkboard) doesn't matter if it meets the standards.

**Step 4: Keep it updated.** It should be easy to update at least weekly, or the whirlwind will crowd it out. Make clear who is responsible, when it is posted and how often it is updated.

**An example.** Susan's events team put the WIG ($22 million to $31 million by December 31) at the top as a trend line with a darker target line, a table recording each associate's weekly site visits (individual tracking, and the team wins only when every member shows green), and a bar graph for the 90 percent upsell standard. Target lines are drawn subjectively if no budget exists; lead targets are usually a single standard, perhaps preceded by a ramp-up diagonal. The leader audits periodically: "trust, but verify." Jim Stuart notes that "Without clear, visible measures, the same goal will mean a hundred different things to a hundred different people."

The deliverable is a scoreboard that keeps the team engaged. Without one, the WIG disappears into the whirlwind: "people disengage when they don't know the score." The three disciplines so far have designed a game that is clear and winnable but is still on the drawing board. A Scoreboard Builder and a checklist (team involvement, WIG and both measures, explanations, actual versus target, glanceable, visible, easy to update, personalized) end the chapter.

> **Key takeaway.** Build the scoreboard with your team, keep it simple, visible and updated, and make sure it shows lead and lag measures against targets so everyone can tell in five seconds whether they are winning.

### Chapter 10: Installing Discipline 4: Create a Cadence of Accountability

Discipline 4 is the discipline of accountability. A team can build a clear, winnable game and still lose it, because without regular accountability the whirlwind pulls everyone back into reacting to the urgent. The chapter opens with an *Inc.* magazine description of a familiar pattern: managers put up scoreboards, the numbers improve for a while, then somebody stops updating the board, improvement stalls, and the boards are quietly taken down. Discipline 4 breaks that cycle by reconnecting team members to the game frequently and personally, so that they are accountable to each other rather than to a distant boss.

**The WIG session.** The mechanism is a short, intense team meeting held at least weekly, with a fixed three-part agenda and no other business.
1. **Account.** Each person reports on the commitments they made the previous week to move the lead measures.
2. **Review the scoreboard.** The team learns from successes and failures, asking whether commitments are moving the lead measure and whether the lead measure is moving the lag measure.
3. **Plan.** Each person clears the path for others and makes fresh commitments for the coming week.

Because people write their own commitments and state them publicly, they leave determined to follow through. Teams with almost no discretionary time, such as an inner-city emergency room, can hold a five-to-seven-minute weekly *WIG huddle* around the scoreboard, with one team commitment instead of many. The authors credit the format to Stephen Cooper's turnaround of ETEC, where weekly reviews with four-minute status reports kept problems from becoming crises. Sessions keep focus on the WIG, spread learning, surface barriers early, let the team adapt as conditions change, and give leaders a moment to celebrate progress.

**A session in action.** Susan's event team, three months in, is above target on its lag measure ($14 million against $10.4 million). Susan opens with the scoreboard and her own commitments, modelling the standard before anyone else speaks. Kim, Bob and Karen then report what they promised, what happened and what they will do next. Karen's "memories packets" of photos and handwritten notes, for example, produced four thank-you calls and two site visits. The team accounts to each other as well as to Susan.

**High-impact commitments.** The guiding question is, "What are the one or two most important things I can do this week to impact the team's performance on the scoreboard?" Each phrase matters:
- **One or two:** a few commitments kept well beat many kept badly.
- **Most important:** skip peripheral work.
- **I:** commit only to what you personally will do.
- **This week:** anything longer dilutes accountability.
- **Performance on the scoreboard:** every commitment must move a lead or lag measure.

Leaders should check that commitments are specific, aligned to the scoreboard and timely. "Do some interviews" becomes "Interview three candidates and make an offer to the best one."

**Watch out.** Four pitfalls: whirlwind responsibilities masquerading as WIG commitments (test with "How will this move the scoreboard?"), sessions with no specific outcomes, repeating the same commitment for more than two weeks, and accepting unfulfilled commitments. The last is "the moment that matters most." When Jeff misses a commitment because a big event consumed his week, Susan responds in three steps: demonstrate respect, reinforce accountability, and encourage performance by asking him to catch up on both weeks. Commitments must be *unconditional*, since most are secretly conditional on "unless something urgent comes up," and something always does. Hyrum Smith's test: if your paycheck depended on this one commitment, you would choose it carefully and keep it.

**Keys to good sessions.** Same time and place each week; brief (twenty to thirty minutes); leader reports first; scoreboard updated and present; celebrate wins; share learning; keep the whirlwind out; clear the path for each other; hold everyone unconditionally accountable.

**The payoff.** Back at Store 334, the worst of 250 stores, Jim Dixon had a WIG, lead measures and a scoreboard, but nothing changed until he asked each department head for the one thing they could do that week to move the score. Yolanda chose to clear the back room; Ted, who planned to focus on a lobster promotion, realised it would not move store conditions and chose to train a new hire. Within ten weeks the store-conditions score rose from 13 to 38 out of 50, and the store ended up outselling the rest of its zone. Jim told his division president he could be left there as long as they wanted. Chapter-ending tools include a WIG Session Agenda and a "Did You Get It Right?" checklist.

> **Key takeaway.** A weekly, unconditional cadence of short WIG sessions, where each person commits to one or two specific actions that move the scoreboard, is what keeps a team winning in spite of the whirlwind.

### Chapter 11: Automating 4DX

This short chapter argues that a team's chances of installing 4DX successfully rise when software supports it. The authors illustrate with their own web tool, my4dx.com, but stress that the capabilities matter more than the product: whatever system a team uses, it must capture the whole game in one place. Most organizations are rich in data yet rarely track these things, and when they do, the data lives in several systems and has to be consolidated by hand.

**The five components of the game.** Any automated system should hold:
1. The team's structure and members, with their roles (and personal touches such as photos).
2. The WIG, the "from X to Y by when" lag measure, and week-by-week targets.
3. The lead measures and their daily or weekly performance standard.
4. Commitments from last week, whether each was kept, and commitments for next week.
5. At-a-glance summary tracking of WIGs, lead measures, WIG sessions and commitments across teams.

**An execution dashboard for one team.** Using Susan's event team, the chapter walks through a dashboard that answers "Where are we now? Where should we be now?" for each lag and lead measure, with green, yellow or red status. Susan's team is green on its revenue WIG and on site visits, but below target on upsells, which tells them where to aim their next commitments. A physical scoreboard builds public accountability and engagement; the software adds something the board does not, namely a record of every individual commitment and whether it was fulfilled.

**Supporting the WIG session.** Before each weekly thirty-minute session, every team member has three jobs: enter personal results on the lead measures, check off last week's fulfilled commitments, and enter next week's commitments. That keeps the meeting brisk, because as each person reports aloud, the leader can bring up that person's numbers on screen. At the end the leader shows the team's overall results and gives final recognition. For dispersed teams, or when someone is away, everyone can see the same display online, so the software can stand in for a physical scoreboard.

**Across the organization.** The need grows when many teams launch 4DX. A graphics-based Team Status Report shows one line per team: sessions held (how many people are attending), commitments made, commitments kept, lead-measure status and WIG status. The leader's job is to "get the red out," reading from the right (WIG sessions) to the left (the WIGs themselves). High participation with low follow-through means people are identifying good activities but not executing; leaders can zero in on such teams and help. The authors name simplicity and transparency as two of the greatest drivers of 4DX success and say technology can supply a clear line of sight from top-level WIGs to frontline teams.

> **Key takeaway.** Automation should capture all five components of the 4DX game, make each team's score and commitments visible at a glance, and let leaders see adoption and results across the organization so they can get the red out.

## Section 3: Installing 4DX in Your Organization

Section 3 widens the lens from one team to an organization made of many teams, whether a small business, a multinational or a government agency. It is written as a field guide for leaders and offers four things: first-person accounts from executives who ran 4DX at scale, the steps for translating organizational strategy into WIGs, a rollout process with a coaching model, and answers to frequently asked questions.

### Chapter 12: Best Practices from the Best

The authors chose four leaders who used 4DX to reach extraordinary results at scale and let them tell their stories in their own words. The lessons overlap, which is part of the point.

**Alec Covington, Nash Finch (about $5 billion in wholesale food distribution).** After six months Covington calls the results "almost unbelievable." His premise is that transformative change is almost impossible without a crisis, because once the crisis passes the whirlwind outweighs the strategic plan. 4DX replaced crisis urgency with an organized way to focus on the most important things. People now ask "What's the X to Y by when?" even in unrelated meetings. He watches two indicators only: whether WIG sessions are attended and held consistently, and how long they run, because a long session signals poor preparation. He values peer pressure over top-down reporting, since leaders and teams choose their own goals and so care about meeting them. He recommends celebration and fun (banners, vivid scoreboards), looking for future leaders in WIG sessions, and using the process to see who is not keeping commitments. His advice for celebrations is to thank the front line rather than the senior leaders, who are "like golf caddies": they supply the tools the front line asks for.

**Dave Grissen, Marriott International.** Grissen started in eight pilot hotels, then rolled 4DX out to more than seven hundred hotels in two years, certifying nearly four thousand leaders and logging about a million commitments. The process was one people ran toward: no hotel had to be asked to take part. The Marriott Marquis in New York achieved its best guest-satisfaction scores in thirty years. His five lessons:
1. Design the implementation to fit your culture. A mandate would have failed; pilot hotel managers telling their peers they would never go back worked far better.
2. It is harder to install 4DX in an already-successful organization, because there is no burning platform.
3. The senior leader must hold every leader accountable, with weekly reporting that "rolls up."
4. Build the infrastructure (internal coaches, training, software) before scaling.
5. 4DX raises engagement even in a strong culture, and gives employees skills they use in their personal lives.

**LeAnn Talbot, Comcast Greater Chicago Region.** Talbot inherited a region that had ranked last for nine years. She changed 70 percent of the leadership roles, celebrated every small win so the team would believe it could win, then piloted 4DX quietly in the city of Chicago. Repeat visits fell by almost half, saved customers doubled, and costs dropped by more than $2 million in five months. Teams loved the cadence (supervisors wore pink wigs to sessions). She was honest that the whirlwind never goes away, used HR partners as 4DX coaches, and had experienced teams "pay it forward" to new ones. In two years the region moved from last to second in the company. She likens 4DX to the frame of a house, which still needs talented people to build on it.

**B. J. Walker, Georgia Department of Human Resources.** Running a $3.2 billion agency with almost twenty thousand staff, Walker credits 4DX with three shifts: results come from team play rather than individual stars; waiting for lag reports is like posting the score only after each inning; and leaders must learn to lead from the field as well as from thirty thousand feet. Her WIG came from the heart of the agency's fear: cut incidents that can lead to the death or serious injury of vulnerable clients. Weekly WIG sessions, she says, closed the gap between leaders and the front line; Discipline 2 connected frontline effort to the WIG. Leaders must stay the course, give the front line what it needs and deliver their own messages. Her closing advice: embed the language of 4DX, check that leaders are clearing the path, communicate directly and often, stress that the front line produces results, lift B-level leaders, and hold the high ground when critics say it is "all about the numbers." The agency exceeded its WIG, reducing repeat child maltreatment by 60 percent.

> **Key takeaway.** Across a distribution firm, a hotel chain, a cable region and a state agency, the same pattern appears: fit the rollout to the culture, keep WIG sessions consistent, give the front line ownership of its goals, and have senior leaders hold everyone accountable and clear obstacles.

### Chapter 13: Focusing the Organization on the Wildly Important

Written with contributing author Scott Thele, this chapter expands the four rules of Discipline 1 (one to three WIGs per person, battles must win the war, veto but don't dictate, and every WIG needs a finish line) into a step-by-step method for turning a complex strategic agenda into WIGs that reach the front line. Focus is never simple to create; it only looks simple once it exists.

**The Opryland case.** When the authors met the leaders of the Opryland Hotel in Nashville, a 2,000-room property planning a 400,000-square-foot expansion, they were juggling dozens of urgent priorities, from marketing and occupancy to expense control and guest wayfinding. The first step was to ask, "If every other area of our operation remained at its current level of performance, which one area would we want to improve the most?" This works better than "What's our most important priority?", which invites endless debate. Guest satisfaction won, since it touched every other part of the business and every employee could contribute. General manager Arthur Keith advanced it as the top-level WIG, showing the leader's dual role of listening and then stepping in to help the team decide. The authors compare choosing a top-level WIG to buying shoes: walk around in it a while, and change it if it does not fit.

**Three sources of organizational WIGs.** Almost every leadership team chooses its top WIG from financial (dollars, revenue, profit), operational (production, quality, efficiency, market share) or customer-satisfaction (closing the gap to excellence, based on customers' perception) goals. Fewer than a third pick a financial WIG first; most start operationally.

**From mission to WIG.** A top-level WIG is not a mission statement or a vision, and rarely the whole strategy. A strategy has three parts: *stroke-of-the-pen* initiatives (things you can simply decide and fund), the *whirlwind* (the core operations, which tools such as a balanced scorecard can monitor), and initiatives that require people to change their behavior. Only the third belongs to 4DX. Leaders will be tempted to pull more initiatives inside the boundary as results come in, but doing so destroys the narrow focus that makes 4DX work.

**Finish lines, wars and battles.** Opryland measured only "top box" scores (a perfect 5 out of 5). Last year's was 42 percent and the best ever was 45 percent; after debate, they set a lag measure of 55 percent. This top-level WIG is the *war*. The leaders then asked, "What is the fewest number of battles necessary to win the war?" The metaphor helps because you should fight one war at a time, every battle must serve the war, and the fewest battles keep attention concentrated. They settled on three:
- **Arrival experience**, because impressions formed in the first fifteen to twenty minutes are nearly impossible to change.
- **Problem resolution**, because the question is not whether things will go wrong but how the hotel responds.
- **Food and beverage quality**, because guests rarely leave the huge property and expect fine-dining standards.

Each battle needed its own X-to-Y-by-when finish line, and together the battles had to add up to the war; if they would not win it, the strategy is not winnable. The work took one day. The general manager called it the most valuable day the team had spent, and Danny Jones said it looked "like something we could have written on the back of a napkin."

**Translating to the teams.** Seventy-five diverse Opryland teams chose their own team WIGs in support of one of the battles. The front desk targeted check-in speed; housekeeping targeted early-check-in room availability; and the bell-stand team cut luggage delivery from 106 minutes to a goal of 20, then reached 12. After nine months the hotel hit 61 percent top-box satisfaction, far above its 55 percent goal and its previous best of 45, and led every other property in its parent company.

**Multiunit organizations.** Where many units do the same thing, such as a retail chain, all units adopt the same WIG and battles but set their own finish lines. One retailer used "Increase Likelihood to Recommend" as its war, with the battles of customer engagement, fewer out-of-stocks and faster checkout. Regions and districts set their own targets rather than having them dictated, and store managers, with district oversight, chose which battles to emphasize, which raised their commitment. The authors warn that the closer you are to an operation, the harder it is to narrow its focus.

The result is a clear and executable strategy that lives in 4DX territory only where behavior must change. The authors suggest that the ability to focus an entire organization may become its greatest competitive advantage.

> **Key takeaway.** Choose one organizational war, define the fewest battles that would win it with X-to-Y-by-when finish lines, and let teams pick their own supporting WIGs, so that a single day of leadership work gives every level a clear, winnable game.

### Chapter 14: Rolling Out 4DX Across the Organization

The authors call this the hardest chapter to write. In their first three years they refined the disciplines until they were right in principle and in practice, but they could not make them spread. Clients embraced the ideas and almost every engagement produced "campfires," isolated pockets of excellence led by a leader who caught the vision. Turning campfires into a wildfire across a whole organization required a new installation method.

**What doesn't work.** The authors' first instinct was to teach 4DX in classrooms, since FranklinCovey is a training company ("if all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail"). Leaders loved the training and left passionate, but embracing a concept is not the same as applying it. The whirlwind is waiting when training ends, colleagues back at work don't share the new understanding, and the disciplines are still *disciplines*, which Atul Gawande notes we are not built for. Only a few trained leaders ever produced results.

**What does work.** Three principles shape the revised approach.
- **Implement as a process, not an event.** The rollout has six steps.
- **Implement with intact teams.** Work with all the leaders needed to achieve the overall WIG, but launch ten or at most twenty teams at a time rather than the whole organization at once.
- **Implement through the leader.** The breakthrough was *leader certification*: equip front-line-adjacent leaders to launch 4DX with their own teams instead of using consultants. Teaching forces real learning, makes the leader an advocate, creates accountability to live the disciplines, and carries more credibility, since teams watch their own leader to see if this is real.

**The six-step installation process.**
1. **Clarify the overall WIG** (the method is in Chapter 13).
2. **Design team WIGs and lead measures.** Over about two days, leaders learn 4DX in depth and each chooses the team WIG that best contributes to the overall one. Senior leaders may advise or veto but may not dictate. Lead measures are the hardest part and often take several tries. The result gives every leader a line of sight from the overall WIG to their own team, but nothing is final until the team has validated it: "No involvement, no commitment."
3. **Leader certification.** In a day, leaders practice scoreboard design, WIG-session skills (including holding peers accountable, using mock sessions) and preparing the team launch meeting, which they rehearse with teaching videos and slides.
4. **Team launch.** A roughly two-hour meeting: about forty-five minutes of 4DX overview, review of the draft WIG and lead measures, team feedback and finalization, scoreboard design, and a practice WIG session. Reluctant teams benefit from observing the sessions of seasoned teams.
5. **Execution with coaching.** This is where the game is actually played. Leaders typically need about three months of experienced coaching as they build new behaviors.
6. **Quarterly summits.** Leaders report progress to senior leaders in front of their peers. The more senior the audience, the higher the stakes and the sense of urgency. At the first summit for five Georgia state agencies, Governor Sonny Perdue said he wanted his legacy to be the state's employees, and told his staff he wanted every leader in the state to go through the process. A summit is always only weeks away.

**The internal coach.** If achieving a WIG is like driving a Formula 1 car, the 4DX coach is the head mechanic: fixing operational breakdowns (resisters, weak lead measures, a faltering cadence) and doing preventive maintenance by spotting teams that are falling to the whirlwind. Two people should share the part-time role. Coaches give the organization responsiveness, independence from outside consultants and continuity when leaders change. The best have business knowledge, communication skills and influence without formal authority, and both interest and capacity: "If they're too available, they're probably not too valuable."

**Watch out.** Postpone the rollout if any of three failure points exists: no goal that really matters, less than full commitment from the senior leader responsible, or certifying leaders at the wrong level. The guideline is to certify the lowest level of full-time leadership above the front line (the store manager rather than the bakery manager; the shift supervisor rather than the plant manager), and to check that leaders and team members have discretionary time.

> **Key takeaway.** Spread 4DX as a process, not a training event: certify the leaders closest to the front line to launch their own intact teams, support them with internal coaches for about three months, and hold quarterly summits so accountability and recognition keep the wildfire going.

### Chapter 15: 4DX Frequently Asked Questions

This chapter collects the questions leaders most often ask, grouped under buy-in, sustainability, process tips and traps, and four kinds of teams (manufacturing, high-tech and scientific, sales, and government and military). It ends with a note on employee engagement.

**Generating buy-in.**
- **Common mistakes.** The two big ones are a lack of participation and a lack of patience. Leaders assume success rests only on the certified managers, when the leaders above them must also hold WIG sessions, recognize progress and remove barriers. And they want results before lead-measure performance has had time to move the lag measure.
- **Resisters.** First understand why. Some simply need to be heard. Others are skeptics or cynics; if they continue to resist, require their support as members of a larger team, and they usually fall in line as they see the others' results.
- **Weekly challenges.** Three recur: keeping up consistent lead-measure performance (like going to the gym, results lag effort), updating the scoreboard (without it nobody knows the score) and holding sessions (they must be "sacred").
- **Program-of-the-day skepticism.** Quoting Stephen Covey ("You can't talk your way out of a situation you have behaved yourself into"), the authors advise starting with one critical goal and being extra consistent to deliver a quick win.
- **Does it start at the top?** Usually not; it often starts in the middle, though the sponsoring leader needs lag measures that matter to upper management.
- **A boss who keeps adding goals.** You can't control the goals thrown at you, but you can choose which few to drive with 4DX.
- **Matrix organizations.** No reorganization is needed; the right cross-functional team forms around the WIG, matching cooperation with accountability.
- **Support functions** (HR, IT, finance) should pick WIGs after line functions have chosen theirs, so that they enable them.
- **Shift workers and dispersed teams.** Hold several sessions or a weekly phone follow-up, since the key is that every member accounts for commitments every week. Repeating "What's our WIG?" to associates spreads awareness.
- **Resisting managers.** Require them to report lag and lead results, session attendance, commitments kept and personal commitments; public reporting and peer success usually win them over.

**Sustainability.**
- **Recognition** works best when public (individual and team awards, trophies for fastest launch or best scoreboard) and when it includes real celebration.
- **Fresh commitments.** Leaders do not get paid for what they do but for what they get others to do, so their commitments should improve the team's capability: training, engaging the team in two-way dialogue and recognizing and modelling top performance.
- **Senior leaders** sustain 4DX by staying on the WIG, modelling the process and recognizing performance.
- **No results after a year.** A WIG is a strategic bet, and sometimes the bet is wrong (an insurer executed perfectly and the lag still did not move). Set WIGs with humility, "one eye on your scoreboards, and the other looking over your shoulder."
- **Exceeding the WIG.** Don't just raise the goal. If it was set far too low, congratulate and take responsibility; if the team simply beat expectations, declare victory and set a new WIG; if a windfall helped, declare it achieved and move on.

**Process tips and traps.**
- **Changing a lead measure** too early loses momentum. Ask whether it is moving the lag, whether the lag is moving enough (maybe raise the standard), whether scorekeeping is accurate, whether the team has hit it for twelve consecutive weeks (the minimum to form a habit) and whether performance would hold without it.
- **Lead moves, lag doesn't.** It may just take time, the team may be gaming the numbers (one reason the authors distrust tying pay to lead measures), or the measure may not be predictive, which should be considered last.
- **A good lead measure** is predictive (causal and sufficient, not just correlated), has the right frequency (drug-company reps who visited doctors constantly were banned from offices) and motivates quality (pharmaceutical sales shifted to educating doctors about the science).
- **Compensation.** There is no one answer; pay for WIG performance works if you have the right people. 4DX can replace or supplement performance reviews.
- **Good commitments** are specific, aligned and timely.
- **Raising performance on a lead measure:** raise the bar, raise the quality, or link a related behaviour.
- **When the leader is on vacation,** don't cancel the session. Choose and prepare someone to lead and debrief afterwards. More than one coach is a good idea.

**Different kinds of teams.**
- **Manufacturing.** 4DX supports lean and Six Sigma; a carpet maker nearly halved project completion times with visible scoreboards and weekly WIG sessions.
- **Technical and scientific teams** struggle longer with Discipline 1 because they see what could go wrong, but excel at Disciplines 2 and 3, which feel like puzzles. Creative teams should be asked for their own lead measures, which often come from hand-offs and touch points.
- **Sales teams** need structure to embed a new process one piece at a time. Their existing pipeline measures are predictive but not influenceable, and sales managers, focused on lag numbers, most need real lead measures.
- **Government and military.** Garrison military is swamped by bureaucracy; the WIG energizes mission-oriented people. Public servants must spend more time promoting the WIG to employees and stakeholders.

**Engagement.** A closing table maps standard engagement-survey items (knowing what is expected, getting feedback, being heard, trust, fairness, recognition, resources) to features of 4DX: measurable WIGs, weekly commitments, WIG-session feedback, clear-the-path requests and shared accountability.

> **Key takeaway.** Most 4DX problems come from impatience, inconsistency or poorly chosen lead measures; stay with the weekly cadence, test and refine lead measures before abandoning them, and treat each WIG as a strategic bet to be checked against the scoreboard.

### Chapter 16: Bringing It Home

Change is hard, whether the goal is losing weight, repairing a marriage, quitting smoking or finishing a long-delayed degree. People often approach the authors after a work session and whisper whether 4DX would work in their personal lives. The answer is yes: the principles beneath the disciplines (focus, leverage, engagement and accountability) work at the organizational, team and personal level alike.

**Jami Downs's pregnancy.** When Jami, the wife of FranklinCovey colleague Jeffrey Downs, learned she was pregnant with her seventh child, she wanted to keep pace with a busy household and stay in shape. Her WIG was "Don't gain more than thirty-six pounds by October 9th." She knew that eating and exercise were the two levers; her family already ate well, so she chose a single lead measure, walking ten thousand steps a day, which was challenging but doable amid a husband who travelled three days a week and six children at home. She stopped fixating on her weight and simply walked at every opportunity: parking far away, walking the children to school and rising early to walk with friends.

Finding a scoreboard was harder than expected. Graphs and spreadsheets failed. What worked was a four-column sheet on her bathroom mirror: the day, the steps needed that day, the steps actually taken, and the running total. The key was the "where I *should* be" column, which turned tracking into a game. Her family joined in. Her children asked about her steps, her oldest daughter walked with her when she was tired, and her husband acted as her accountability partner as she made weekly commitments. An unexpected benefit followed: she had feared she would have less time for her other children, but walking and talking strengthened her relationships with them. She finished with 1,751,250 steps, stayed under her weight goal and delivered a healthy son on October 4.

**What to notice.** The authors draw out three lessons: narrow to the one new behavior that makes the difference (she did not track her already-healthy diet); design a scoreboard that is simple, motivating and shows where you should be, which is where most people get stuck; and use public accountability, because involving others raises your odds of success.

**Other personal examples.**
- **A five-year-old, Clay,** overcame bedwetting once his father set up a calendar on the refrigerator; each morning Clay drew a green smiley face or a red frowny face and reported to the family, and a week of green earned ice cream. Within thirty days he was sleeping through the night.
- **A man who wanted to lose eighty pounds** before his son's graduation used three lead measures (walk five miles a day, no eating after eight, 2,500 calories maximum), hit his goal and set another.
- **A colleague who sets three WIGs each year** (professional, family and personal) spends thirty minutes a week reviewing commitments.
- **A grandfather's WIG** that his grandchildren know they are loved had the lead measure of spending time with each every week; his lag measure was whether they run to hug him.
- **A father who tried to improve his family's culture** for a year found the one lead measure that worked: visibly loving their mother.

**Why it matters.** The most important things in life (health, children, education, marriage) are rarely urgent and so lose out to the whirlwind. Citing Dr. Ray Levey, the authors note that 80 percent of the health-care budget goes to five behaviors: smoking, drinking, overeating, stress and too little exercise. Even after heart attacks, many people do not change. Perhaps the missing link is an operating system for changing human behavior, which 4DX aspires to be.

> **Key takeaway.** The same four disciplines work for personal and family goals: pick one wildly important goal, choose the one behavior that will drive it, keep a simple scoreboard that shows where you should be, and report weekly to someone who holds you accountable.

### Chapter 17: So, Now What?

The final chapter is a brief send-off. The authors acknowledge that 4DX is not in most people's DNA: it is counterintuitive to manage this way, and to some it seems too simple and to others far too complex. They believe, though, that once leaders understand it they will never lead in the same way again, and that the art of execution comes down to the handful of principles and practices in the book. They hope readers' minds are spinning with possibilities, and offer a few quick thought experiments, one per discipline.

- **Discipline 1: Focus on the Wildly Important.** Draft a WIG and lag measure for your work team and ask what achieving it would mean to the team, the organization and yourself.
- **Discipline 2: Act on the Lead Measures.** Draft lead measures that would drive the WIG and ask how a new understanding of lead measures would change the way you operate.
- **Discipline 3: Keep a Compelling Scoreboard.** Sketch a scoreboard with the WIG, lag measure and lead measures, and ask what difference it would make to focus your best efforts on moving those numbers.
- **Discipline 4: Create a Cadence of Accountability.** Visualize your team holding a WIG session around that scoreboard and ask how regular sessions would change focus and engagement.

**Finally.** The authors ask readers to picture the day they report to their own leaders that the WIG has been achieved, and then to imagine that day never comes: the book is forgotten and life continues as a relentless whirlwind where everything is urgent and real priorities are postponed forever. They quote Peter Drucker on people who are "magnificent at getting the unimportant things done." The goal is to make a high-value, high-impact contribution rather than to be "magnificently trivial." The authors offer to help if they can.

They close with the larger purpose. Achieving the WIG is not the only objective; 4DX also reignites the passion of the team, brings focus and discipline to its efforts and helps people see that they are winners. Instilling that sense of winning is, they say, the greatest legacy a leader can leave, because it equips people to win as workers, parents and community leaders in ways that cannot be measured.

> **Key takeaway.** Try the four disciplines on one real goal now, because the lasting payoff of 4DX is not just a hit goal but a team that has learned to win.

## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The book's core claim is that execution is a behavior problem, not a planning problem. Strategies that need people to do something new fail quietly because the whirlwind of daily operations always wins on urgency. The disciplines do not try to manage the whirlwind; they carve out a narrow, protected zone for the one or two goals that must change behavior, and the authors repeatedly warn against widening that zone.

A second theme is leverage through the right measures. Lag measures tell you the score too late, while lead measures, which are both predictive and influenceable, let a team act now. The authors back this with case after case (the Opryland bell stand, the Store 334 turnaround, Marriott, Comcast, the Georgia agency) in which a clear finish line and a few lead measures made a bigger difference than additional effort.

A third theme is engagement through ownership. People commit to goals they help shape: teams choose their own team WIGs and lead measures, build their own scoreboards and make their own weekly commitments. "No involvement, no commitment" explains why 4DX is installed through intact teams and certified leaders rather than handed down. The last chapter extends the same logic to family and personal goals.

### Strengths and limits

The strengths are clarity and practicality. The disciplines are few, the rules for each are specific (one to three WIGs, predictive and influenceable lead measures, a scoreboard that shows winning at a glance, unconditional weekly commitments) and the installation chapters give meeting scripts, checklists and common pitfalls. The case studies show the method working in very different settings, and the book is candid that rollout, not understanding, is the hard part.

The limits are about evidence and fit. The results are reported by the authors and by clients who chose to share success stories, so there is little about failures beyond a few illustrative cases. The method assumes a goal that can be measured with a numeric finish line and behaviors a team can count, which sits awkwardly with creative, relational or highly uncertain work (the FAQ makes a case that it does apply, but mostly through anecdotes). It is also a product of a consulting firm, and the later chapters on certification, coaching and software read partly as a description of that firm's offering.

*Where I'd push back:* the insistence that commitments are "unconditional" regardless of the whirlwind can reward visible busyness over good judgment, and the discouragement of tying pay to lead measures sits uneasily beside the advice that compensation can be aligned to WIGs. Teams will still need to decide when a missed commitment reflects a real emergency.

### Applying it

1. **Pick one WIG** (Chapters 2 and 7). Ask what single area you would most want to improve if everything else stayed level, and write it as "from X to Y by when."
2. **Choose one or two lead measures** (Chapters 3 and 8). Test each for being predictive and influenceable, and involve the team in choosing them.
3. **Build a players' scoreboard** (Chapters 4 and 9). Make it simple, visible and updated weekly, showing where you are and where you should be.
4. **Hold a weekly WIG session** (Chapters 5 and 10). Keep it to twenty or thirty minutes with the three-part agenda, and make specific, unconditional commitments.
5. **Scale carefully** (Chapters 13 and 14). Define the fewest battles that win the war, certify the lowest level of full-time leaders, and add coaches and quarterly summits.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 1, 2, 3 and 10. Chapter 1 sets out the problem, Chapters 2 and 3 cover focus and lead measures, and Chapter 10 describes the weekly cadence that makes everything else stick.

A lag measure tracks the result you want but arrives too late to influence. A lead measure tracks the new behaviors that drive that result and is both predictive and influenceable.

It is specific, aligned to moving the scoreboard and timely, meaning it can be completed in the coming week.

Because the whirlwind always produces something urgent; if urgency excuses a missed commitment, the cadence collapses and the WIG is swallowed.
