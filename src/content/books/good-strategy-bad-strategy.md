## Overview

*Good Strategy/Bad Strategy* is Richard Rumelt's argument that most of what organizations call strategy is not. Rumelt, a professor at UCLA's Anderson School who has advised companies and governments for decades, says a real strategy is a coherent response to an important challenge. It starts from an honest diagnosis of what is going on, sets a guiding policy for dealing with the critical obstacle and backs it with coordinated action. What passes for strategy in most plans is ambition, a list of goals, a statement of vision and values, or an exhortation to try harder. He calls that bad strategy, and he says it has a logic of its own that can be recognized and avoided.

The book has three parts. Part I (Chapters 1 to 5) shows how rare and surprising good strategy is, how power is discovered, what bad strategy looks like and why it spreads, and what the kernel of good strategy contains. Part II (Chapters 6 to 15) explains the sources of power: leverage, proximate objectives, chain-link systems, design, focus, growth, advantage, dynamics, inertia and entropy, with a long case, Nvidia, that combines them. Part III (Chapters 16 to 18) turns to the strategist's mind, treating strategy as a scientific hypothesis, showing how to improve and test your own judgment and warning about being swept along by the crowd. The numbering follows the book. The Introduction is summarized first, unnumbered.

Three ideas hold the book together.

1. **Good strategy has a kernel.** A diagnosis that names the critical challenge, a guiding policy that tackles it and a set of coherent, coordinated actions. If any of the three is missing, you have goals, slogans or a budget, not a strategy.
2. **Power comes from focus and fit.** Concentrating effort on a pivot point, setting a feasible next objective, designing tightly fitted policies and riding waves of change multiply the effect of resources, while chasing growth, vision or "doing everything" dilutes them.
3. **Strategy is judgment that must be guarded.** A strategy is an educated hypothesis, and the main enemies are your own quick closure on the first idea, social herding and the belief that "this time is different." Test it, attack it and learn from outside evidence.

## Introduction: Overwhelming Obstacles

Rumelt opens with Lord Nelson at Trafalgar in 1805. Outnumbered by a combined French and Spanish fleet, Nelson broke his line into two columns and drove them at the enemy's line from the side, betting that the less-trained gunners could not cope with the heavy swell. The French and Spanish lost twenty-two ships and the British lost none. Rumelt's point is that good strategy usually looks this simple and obvious. It does not come from a matrix or a thick deck of slides. A talented leader finds the one or two critical issues, the pivot points that multiply the effect of effort, and concentrates action and resources on them. The core of strategy work is always the same: discover the critical factors in a situation and design a way of coordinating and focusing action to deal with them.

He then gives four examples of leaders who offer exhortation and call it strategy. At a "strategy retreat" about two hundred managers watched a film, heard a vision of being the most respected company in the field and released balloons, but nobody offered a strategy. Lehman Brothers answered the first signs of a housing downturn in 2006 by growing faster and raising its "risk appetite" while holding only 3 percent equity, which is ambition, not a way of overcoming a challenge, and the firm collapsed in 2008. In Iraq the US had many high-sounding goals but no strategy for the insurgency until General Petraeus arrived in 2007 with a real one: protect the population so that it would supply the information needed to isolate the insurgents. Finally, a Web-services CEO told Rumelt at lunch that "strategy is never quitting until you win," which Rumelt took as the very confusion of strategy with determination that the book is meant to clear up.

His central claim is that a good strategy honestly acknowledges the challenge it faces and provides an approach to overcoming it, and the greater the challenge, the more it must focus and coordinate effort. Bad strategy skips the problems, ignores the power of choice and focus, and tries to accommodate every conflicting interest. It covers its failure to guide with broad goals, vision and values. Rumelt argues that the word "strategy" has been stretched until it means almost anything, so he draws lines. Ambition, determination, innovation and inspirational leadership are different virtues from strategy, which selects the path along which they are applied. A strategy is not a decision made by someone with a senior title; it is a cohesive response to an important challenge, a coherent set of analyses, concepts, policies, arguments and actions.

Two further points set up the rest of the book. First, executives who complain of "execution problems" have usually confused strategy with goal setting; strategy is about how, and the coherent actions are the punch of the strategy, not "implementation details." Second, a good strategy has an essential logical structure he calls the kernel, made of a diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent action. Once you know the kernel you can spot bad strategy without special expertise, as in the 2008 financial crisis response, which lacked any official diagnosis and so could not focus resources on a cure. Rumelt closes by saying that bad strategy has a life and logic of its own, that it is spreading in governments, boards and schools, and that the remedy is to demand good strategy from leaders rather than charisma and vision.

> **Key takeaway.** Strategy is a coherent response to a specific challenge, built from a diagnosis, a guiding policy and coordinated action, and it is not the same thing as ambition, vision or goal setting.

## Part I: Good and Bad Strategy

Part I defines the difference. It first shows how rare and surprising focused strategy is, then how power arises, then what bad strategy looks like, why it spreads, and finally the kernel that good strategy contains.


### Chapter 1: Good Strategy Is Unexpected

Rumelt argues that good strategy has a natural advantage because most organizations do not have one, and rivals do not expect you to. A strategy is a coordination of actions, policies and resources to accomplish an important end, whereas most organizations pursue many goals and initiatives that signal progress but offer no approach beyond "spend more and try harder." He illustrates with two cases.

The first is Apple in 1996 to 1998. After Windows 95 Apple was in a death spiral and its CEO reorganized product groups while magazines offered "101 ways to save Apple." By late 1997 the company was two months from bankruptcy. Steve Jobs returned and did something both obvious and unexpected. He shrank Apple to a niche producer: he secured $150 million from Microsoft, cut fifteen desktop models to one, cut portables to one laptop, dropped printers and peripherals, cut development, software and distributors, dropped five of six national retailers, moved manufacturing to Taiwan, cut inventory by more than 80 percent and opened a web store selling direct. Jobs did not announce revenue goals or messianic visions. He redesigned the business logic around a simplified line sold through a few outlets, and he explained it plainly: the lineup was confusing, the company was bleeding cash, and a friend of the family could not tell which computer to buy. Asked in 1998 what the long-term plan was, since network effects seemed to lock Apple into a niche, he only smiled and said he would wait for the next big thing, which was a realistic reading of how windows of opportunity open in that industry. Rumelt contrasts this with twenty-six electronics executives he interviewed, who could explain how their sector's leader had leapt through a window of opportunity but described their own strategy as a pile of alliances, feedback programs and stretch goals, "look-busy doorknob polishing."

The second case is Desert Storm in 1991. Press and politicians feared a bloody, World War I style assault on dug-in Iraqi positions, with predictions of heavy casualties. General Schwarzkopf instead rejected his staff's direct attack, which was estimated to cost 2,000 dead and 8,000 wounded. He used air strikes to cut Iraqi capability by about half, then secretly shifted 250,000 troops far to the west for a massive "left hook" into the flank of the Republican Guard, while marines moved slowly up the middle and the sea-based marines served as a diversion. The ground war lasted one hundred hours. Yet this was the Army's own published Plan A, envelopment, described in field manual FM 100-5, available to anyone for twenty-five dollars. Deception explains part of the surprise, but the deeper answer is that people did not expect so pure and focused a strategy to be implemented. Schwarzkopf had to suppress the ambitions of the air force, the marines, coalition partners and Washington, for example forcing air force commanders who believed bombing Baghdad could win the war to divert resources to support the land offensive.

![Envelopment: a feint against the front combined with a main force sweeping around the flank](/figures/good-strategy-bad-strategy/envelopment.svg "Redrawn: envelopment pairs an apparent frontal attack with a much larger end run onto the enemy's flank and rear.")

Rumelt concludes that conflicting goals, resources spread over unconnected targets and accommodation of incompatible interests are the luxuries of the rich and powerful, and they make bad strategy. Good strategy needs leaders willing to say no to many actions and interests, and it is at least as much about what an organization does not do as about what it does.

> **Key takeaway.** Good strategy is surprising mainly because it requires focus: concentrating resources on one coherent approach and saying no to everything else.


### Chapter 2: Discovering Power

The second natural advantage of good strategy is insight into new sources of strength and weakness. Looking at a situation from a fresh angle can reveal advantages and threats that others miss. Rumelt illustrates this with three cases.

The first is David and Goliath. A conventional list of strengths and weaknesses favours the nine-foot veteran warrior over the shepherd boy, which is why King Saul tried to hold David back. Only after the stone is slung do we see that David's sling skill and quickness were strengths, that armor would only have slowed him, and that Goliath's helmet left his forehead exposed. David's weapon delivered force with precision at a distance and cancelled the giant's size. The story gives us the discovery of power in a situation, the creation or revelation of a decisive asymmetry, and not merely its deft use. Not every good strategy draws on such insight, but those that do separate ordinary excellence from the extraordinary.

The second case is Wal-Mart, which Rumelt uses in teaching. A case about its rise to 1986 states the conventional wisdom that a full-line discount store needs a population base of at least 100,000. Students list the standard explanations, namely big stores in small towns, everyday low prices, computerized logistics, non-union staff and low administrative costs, and sketch a small town with a 45,000-square-foot store and a hub-and-spoke distribution system serving about 150 stores. Rumelt then asks why Kmart, with the case published in 1986, did not copy the formula, since success implies either blocked or failed competition. He points out that bar-code scanners helped Wal-Mart far more than Kmart because Wal-Mart fed the data into its satellite systems, its inbound logistics and its supplier negotiations. The policies were complements forming an integrated, coherent design, and copying one piece brings little benefit. The final insight is that Walton did not break the conventional wisdom so much as redefine the store: the network of 150 stores, with its data flows, distribution hub and management circuit, became the basic unit, and a regional network can serve millions of people. That also broke the era's doctrine of decentralization, which Kmart followed, giving each manager authority over products, vendors and prices and thereby losing coordination. Breaking with basic doctrine is rare without a near-death experience, so Kmart, which filed for bankruptcy in 2002, could not respond.

The third case is Andy Marshall, director of the Pentagon's Office of Net Assessment. He told Rumelt that Cold War planning had been driven by the annual budget cycle: the Joint Chiefs estimated Soviet inventory as the threat, the Pentagon produced a shopping list in response, and Congress funded part of it. Planning reacted to Soviet strengths and had a war plan, a "catastrophic spasm," but no plan to compete over the long term. A 1976 paper by Marshall and James Roche argued instead for using distinctive US competences, above all technology, to gain competitive advantage by imposing exorbitant costs on the other side, for example through more accurate missiles and quieter submarines that forced the Soviets to spend on counters without adding to any threat against the US. Rumelt believes this logic, with other forces such as oil prices, limited access to Western technology and the war in Afghanistan, helped overextend the Soviet Union. The strategy rested on a shift from comparing military capability to imposing asymmetric costs on an opponent, and it needed no complex charts, only "the terrible simplicity" of discovering hidden power.

> **Key takeaway.** Strategic power is often discovered, not assembled: a fresh view of the situation, such as redefining the store as a network or competing through cost imposition, reveals an asymmetry that rivals cannot easily copy.


### Chapter 3: Bad Strategy

Rumelt says bad strategy is not just the absence of good strategy. It grows from specific misconceptions and leadership failures, and it has four hallmarks: fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives. He coined the term in 2007 at a Washington seminar on national security strategy, where insiders such as former defense secretary James Schlesinger agreed that strategy work had declined. The 2002 and 2006 national security strategies, they felt, were lists of goals and values, such as democracy and economic well-being, with little guidance on how to achieve them. The preventive-war doctrine lacked analysis of its own conditions and side effects, such as the need for much better intelligence, and slogans such as "work with others to defuse regional conflicts" gave nobody useful direction. Rumelt told the group that business had the same problem: bad strategy is long on goals and short on policy or action, assumes goals are enough, sets incoherent or impracticable objectives and hides all this behind high-sounding words.

**Fluff.** Fluff is superficial restatement of the obvious combined with buzzwords. A bank's memo declared its strategy to be "customer-centric intermediation," which means the bank takes deposits and lends them, so the strategy was "being a bank." Rumelt's longer example is a summer 2000 Arthur Andersen presentation praising Enron's plan to trade bandwidth. A diagram showed commodity markets "evolving" toward "knowledge space" and "exotics," but there was no spot price, no quality standard, no way to ship bandwidth, near-zero marginal cost and a glut of fibre, so the gas-and-electricity playbook could not carry over. The suggested "strategies" such as being an information provider were just names, like butcher or baker. Enron failed fourteen months later. True expertise makes the complex understandable, while bad strategy hides emptiness behind complexity.

**Failure to face the challenge.** A strategy is a way through a difficulty, so if the difficulty is not defined you cannot judge or improve the strategy. In 1979 International Harvester's "Corporate Strategic Plan" combined five divisional plans promising more market share and lower costs, with a perfect hockey-stick profit forecast. It ignored the elephant in the elevator: grossly inefficient work rules and the worst labor relations in American industry. A six-month strike under CEO Archie McCardell achieved nothing, and over 1979 to 1985 the firm lost more than $3 billion, closed thirty-five of forty-two plants and cut its workforce from about 100,000 to 15,000. Today's fill-in-the-blanks templates of vision, mission, values, goals, strategies and initiatives repeat the flaw. As a counterpoint, DARPA defines its challenge precisely, as the "DARPA-hard" niche of risky problems the services avoid, and backs it with policies such as four-to-six-year terms for program managers and minimal overhead and facilities.

**Mistaking goals for strategy.** Chad Logan, CEO of a graphic-arts firm, asked Rumelt to coach his managers in "strategic thinking." His "20/20 plan" targeted 20 percent annual growth and a 20 percent margin, against a history of flat share and about 12 percent margins, and his "key strategies" were goals and values. When asked what lever would produce the jump, he quoted Jack Welch about reaching for the impossible. Rumelt compared that faith in willpower to the "one last push" at Passchendaele, where Haig's shelling destroyed the dikes and turned the ground to a quagmire and five miles were gained for over 70,000 dead. The troops lacked not motivation but competent strategic leadership. Rumelt advised a month-long review of buyers, competitors and bottlenecks to find one or two promising opportunities. Logan hired another consultant, who ran a "visioning" session asking how big the company could be. Rolling three- or five-year budgets with market-share projections are not strategy either; planning is useful, but strategy is episodic and aims at overcoming obstacles.

**Bad strategic objectives.** Senior leaders do not just take goals, they design the objectives that turn broad goals (values and desires) into specific operational targets, and that strategic bridge is the leader's main job. Chen Brothers, a specialty food distributor, did it well when Whole Foods threatened its retailers: it formed a team with the single objective of landing its most distinctive product as a national Whole Foods account. One bad form is the "dog's dinner," a long list from many stakeholders; a Pacific Northwest city had 47 strategies and 178 action items (number 122 was "create a strategic plan"), and the Los Angeles school district's plan had 7 strategies, 26 tactics and 234 action steps. The other is the blue-sky objective, which merely restates the desired state. Superintendent David Brewer's plan for the 34 weakest LA schools was well focused, but it defined the challenge as underperformance (a result, not a cause), used a test index that dropouts could improve, and offered objectives such as "transformational leadership" and shared beliefs without diagnosing why leadership was weak, without a way to create such leaders inside a union-bound bureaucracy and without confronting the poverty and instability of the communities served.

> **Key takeaway.** Bad strategy shows up as fluff, an unfaced challenge, goals dressed as strategy or objectives that are either a laundry list or wishful thinking; if no obstacle is identified, you do not have a strategy.


### Chapter 4: Why So Much Bad Strategy?

Rumelt says bad strategy is not miscalculation. More training in analysing competitors or resources barely reduces it, because bad strategy floats above analysis, logic and choice and is the active avoidance of the hard work of crafting a good strategy. He describes three common pathways: unwillingness or inability to choose, template-style strategy, and New Thought.

**The unwillingness or inability to choose.** Strategy means focus, and focus means setting some goals aside. In 1992 Rumelt sat in on a Digital Equipment Corporation meeting where three executives backed different futures: Alec wanted to stay a computer company selling integrated "Boxes," Beverly wanted "Solutions" built on customer relationships, and Craig wanted to concentrate on semiconductor "Chips." Doing all three was not viable, for two reasons. If every option is adopted, nobody has a reason to sharpen arguments, since only the prospect of choice produces the best reasoning, and Chips and Solutions were each deep transformations that cannot be run at once. Their rankings produce a Condorcet cycle: Boxes beats Chips, Solutions beats Boxes, yet Chips beats Solutions, so no stable majority coalition exists, and Kenneth Arrow proved that cleverer voting schemes cannot cure it. CEO Ken Olsen asked for consensus, and the group settled on a statement of commitment to "high-quality products and services" and leadership in data processing, a political outcome that hurt no one and crippled the company. Robert Palmer replaced Olsen in June 1992 and chose Chips, but it was five years too late, and Compaq acquired DEC in 1998.

Rumelt's wider point is that the real difficulty in strategy is choice itself: strategy is scarcity's child and means saying no to whole worlds of hopes. When strategies work, we remember what was achieved and forget what was set aside. Eisenhower, after the Project Solarium study, put aside his campaign promise to roll back Soviet control of Eastern Europe and chose deterrence. Andy Grove describes the long, painful move of Intel out of memory chips: after Gordon Moore said a new CEO would get out of memories, it still took over a year to refocus on microprocessors, and the 386 made Intel the largest semiconductor firm by 1992. Because any change in strategy makes some people worse off, there are strong forces against it, and the longer a pattern persists, the more its resource allocations are treated as entitlements. Mom-and-apple-pie goals that everybody accepts show a lack of will to choose; universal buy-in usually signals the absence of choice.

**Template-style strategy.** Another route runs through the study of charisma. Max Weber distinguished charismatic from formal leaders, and in 1985 Bennis and Nanus and Bernard Bass argued that charisma, now "transformational leadership," could be taught: develop a vision, inspire sacrifice, empower people. Peter Drucker replied that effective leaders such as Eisenhower, Marshall and Truman had no more charisma than "a dead mackerel." Rumelt argues that leadership motivates and strategy works out which purposes are worth pursuing and achievable, and that confusing them leaves charisma without craft, as in the 1212 Children's Crusade, where thousands followed visionary boys into slavery and death. Gandhi succeeded because vision was joined to careful strategy. The result of the confusion is a fill-in-the-blanks template of vision, mission, values and "strategies," which Dow, Enron, the Department of Defense, Cornell, Cal State Sacramento, the CIA and NEC all illustrate with statements of the obvious. For example, Cornell's mission amounts to "Cornell is a university," and NEC, earning under 2 percent on equity, cannot afford its stated ambitions. He calls this "charisma-in-a-can," and notes it also gives consultants a way to avoid analysing the client's real challenges while never hurting anyone's feelings.

**New Thought.** The third path is the belief that positive thinking produces success. Rumelt traces it from Emerson's transcendentalism and Mary Baker Eddy's Christian Science to the New Thought movement of the 1880s and 1890s (Prentice Mulford, Wallace Wattles, Ernest Holmes) and on to Napoleon Hill, Norman Vincent Peale, Anthony Robbins and Rhonda Byrne's *The Secret*, which repeats Mulford's idea that you get what you think about. He argues that it has seeped into management through "shared vision," as in Peter Senge's claim that vision explains the rise of AT&T, Ford and Apple, which he calls a distortion of history: Apple prospered from Wozniak's clever engineering and VisiCalc, and Ford from materials, industrial engineering and promotion. Writers such as Mark Lipton even call for suspending disbelief. Rumelt says that believing you can change the world by thinking only of success is not a basis for strategy: all analysis starts with what might go wrong, and he would not fly in a plane designed by people who never considered failure modes.

> **Key takeaway.** Bad strategy persists because choosing is painful; consensus, template planning and positive-thinking rituals let leaders avoid the choices and the diagnosis that real strategy requires.


### Chapter 5: The Kernel of Good Strategy

Rumelt defines good strategy as coherent action backed by an argument, with a basic structure he calls the kernel. A strategy may contain more than the kernel, but if the kernel is missing or misshapen there is a serious problem. The kernel has three elements: a diagnosis that defines or explains the nature of the challenge, a guiding policy that sets the overall approach to the obstacles identified, and a set of coherent actions designed to carry out that policy. A doctor's version is a diagnosis, a therapeutic approach and specific prescriptions. In foreign policy a challenge is diagnosed by analogy ("another Hitler" versus "another Gadhafi"), and in a business the first step is to diagnose the specific structure of the challenge instead of naming performance goals. Visions, goal hierarchies, time spans and talk of adaptation are supporting players.

![The kernel of strategy: diagnosis, then guiding policy, then coherent action](/figures/good-strategy-bad-strategy/kernel.svg "Redrawn: the three elements of the kernel and the question each answers.")

**The diagnosis.** Colleague John Mamer once remarked that Rumelt asks one question in every case: "What's going on here?" A diagnosis names or classifies the situation, links facts into patterns and tells you which issues deserve attention, and an explicit diagnosis lets you evaluate and later revise the rest of the strategy. At Starbucks in 2008, flat traffic and return on assets falling from 14 to about 5.5 percent could be read as a problem of managing expectations, a search for new growth platforms or an eroding advantage, and none of these can be proved; diagnosis is a judgment about the meaning of facts in an ill-structured situation. A good diagnosis replaces overwhelming complexity with a simpler story and also defines a domain of action. Social class explains school results better than organization does, yet Bill Ouchi's diagnosis that schools suffer from the way they are organized is more useful because organization can be changed by policy. Diagnoses are often conveyed by metaphor, as in George Kennan's 1946 long telegram, which saw the USSR as a non-ordinary state, a kind of virus to be contained. Business turnarounds usually begin with a changed diagnosis: when Lou Gerstner reached IBM in 1993, analysts thought it was too integrated for a fragmenting industry, but he judged that IBM was the only firm with expertise across all areas and had failed to use it, so it should integrate around customer solutions.

**The guiding policy.** It is "guiding" because, like highway guardrails, it channels action without prescribing it. Containment and Gerstner's solutions focus are examples. Good guiding policies are not goals or visions: Wells Fargo's stated vision of satisfying all customer needs says nothing about how, whereas Richard Kovacevich's policy of network effects from cross-selling does. Defining strategy as only the policy is a mistake, because without a diagnosis you cannot compare policies, and without actions you cannot know it is implementable. The heart of a policy is advantage, anything that multiplies the effect of resources, and the policy itself can create advantage by anticipating others' actions, reducing complexity, concentrating effort on a pivotal point and making actions coherent. Stephanie, who owns a corner grocery, faced hundreds of choices from prices to parking. An economist's advice to "maximize profit" is useless. She diagnosed the problem as competition with a 24/7 supermarket, chose to serve the busy professional with little time to cook, and that policy immediately organized the rest: a second checkout for the 5 p.m. rush, alley parking, prepared take-home foods in place of student snacks, and no need for late-night hours.

**Coherent action.** Many people stop at the guiding policy, but the kernel must contain action, enough to bring concepts down to earth. An executive of a consumer goods company in London had a "Pan-European" initiative that produced committees and rotations but little else. Asked what he would do if it were truly critical, he said he would close the rival development groups, start afresh in the Netherlands, create a single group with full profit responsibility and override country budgets and promotions. He then admitted that would be painful. It took him nine months to decide it was important, and Rumelt's lesson is that the necessity to act forces the choice that concepts let you postpone. Coherence means resources, policies and manoeuvres that are consistent and coordinated. Ford under Jacques Nasser bought Volvo, Jaguar, Land Rover and Aston Martin on the belief that brand drives profit, while a one-million-units-per-platform scale policy pushed Jaguar and Volvo onto common platforms, diluting both brands. A list of non-conflicting but unrelated actions, such as closing an Akron plant, spending more on advertising and starting 360-degree feedback, is just operational management, not strategy. Coherence is imposed by policy and design, not by ad hoc mutual adjustment, and a proximate objective can also coordinate effort.

Rumelt acknowledges the objection to centralized direction, since central planning starved millions under Stalin and Mao while decentralized markets allocate many resources well. But decentralized choice fails when costs and benefits are not borne by the deciding unit, across time, or when benefits depend on coordination, as with rush orders versus long production runs, or Roosevelt's decision to put Europe first and support the Soviet Union. Coordination is costly because it cuts into the gains from specialization, so a good strategy specializes on the right activities and imposes only the essential amount of coordination.

> **Key takeaway.** Good strategy has a three-part kernel: a diagnosis of what is going on, a guiding policy that tackles the critical obstacle, and coordinated actions that carry it out.


## Part II: Sources of Power

Part II explains where the force in a good strategy comes from: leverage, a feasible next step, an interdependent chain, design, focus, growth, advantage and dynamics, and how they combine.


### Chapter 6: Using Leverage

Rumelt says a good strategy draws power from focusing minds, energy and action on a pivotal objective at the right moment, and he calls that power leverage. Archimedes' lever would have to be billions of miles long to move the earth, so he would be wise to apply it where a tiny movement makes a large difference. Finding such pivot points and concentrating force on them is the secret of strategic leverage. Madison in 1787 and Bill Gates in 1980, who insisted on keeping the right to sell the operating system to third parties after IBM paid for it, both seized such moments. Leverage comes from a mix of anticipation, insight into what is pivotal and concentrated effort.

**Anticipation.** The strategist may foresee predictable aspects of others' behaviour. While SUVs boomed, Toyota invested more than $1 billion in hybrid technology, anticipating that fuel economy pressure would make hybrids a major category and that other carmakers would license its system instead of developing their own. By contrast, US planners for the 2003 invasion of Iraq failed to anticipate an insurgency, and even failed to anticipate the Iraqis' own anticipation that media coverage of casualties would erode US resolve. Much anticipation draws on the predictable downstream results of events already under way. Shell planners Pierre Wack and Ted Newland predicted in 1970 the rise of OPEC from the incomes, populations and ambitions of Iran, Iraq and Venezuela, like predicting a flood on the Ganges from a storm in the Himalayas. Wack also warned that "high, medium, low" scenario graphs mislead, because the real risk is that prices go high, tempt you into a big investment, then collapse; oil peaked near $36 a barrel and fell to $20 as North Sea and Alaskan supply arrived. Anticipation requires no psychic power, just attention to others' habits, policies and inertia.

**Pivot points.** A pivot point is a natural or created imbalance where a small adjustment releases large pent-up forces. Noritoshi Murata of Seven & i explained that Japanese consumers get bored quickly, with over two hundred soft-drink brands and 70 percent turnover in a store's fifty varieties. 7-Eleven Japan therefore collects local taste information from staff, forms quick-response merchandising teams and works with second- and third-tier manufacturers who have spare capacity to make private-label products. In China its pivot was cleanliness and service, delivering twice the sales per square foot of competitors. In rivalry the pivot can be an imbalance between a rival's pretension and reality. Reagan's 1987 "tear down this wall" challenge worked in part because Gorbachev's claim of liberalization clashed with the facts on the ground; the same words to Andropov in 1983 would have had little effect.

**Concentration.** Returns to concentration arise from constraints and threshold effects. If resources were unlimited or rivals could not respond, there would be no need to select. A threshold effect means effort below a critical level has little payoff, as with advertising, which may be better pulsed, or launched region by region. Strategists often prefer dominating a small segment to owning a sliver of a big one, and politicians prefer a clear benefit to a recognizable group. People also ignore sub-threshold signals and believe in momentum, so it may do more for opinion to turn around two schools completely than to improve two hundred by 2 percent. Rumelt's example is Harold Williams at the Getty Trust, which had to spend about $65 million a year. Buying art would only raise prices and move works from New York and Paris to Los Angeles, so Williams aimed to transform the study of art through a digital catalog, teacher education, research and conservation science, an objective scaled to the resources and visible enough to attract support.

> **Key takeaway.** Leverage comes from anticipating others' moves, finding the pivot point where a small push has outsized effect, and concentrating enough force there to pass the threshold.


### Chapter 7: Proximate Objectives

Rumelt calls the creation of a good proximate objective, one close enough to be feasible, one of a leader's most powerful tools. Kennedy's moon goal looks like a bold vision, but it was a carefully chosen proximate objective. Kennedy diagnosed the problem as world opinion and argued the US had far more resources to coordinate, and he laid out steps: unmanned exploration, bigger boosters, parallel rocket development and a landing vehicle. Engineers knew how to build rockets, and Werner von Braun's memo to Lyndon Johnson argued that the Soviets' lead in heavy-lift rockets would help them win near-term spectaculars, but a moon landing needed rockets ten times more powerful than either side had, which favoured the larger US resource base. Kennedy spoke a month later. Rumelt contrasts this with objectives nobody knows how to reach, such as the War on Drugs or energy independence, which are not proximate within current law, politics and enforcement.

**Resolving ambiguity.** Rumelt worked as an engineer at JPL on Surveyor, which was to soft-land on the moon. Nobody knew whether the lunar surface was powder, needle-sharp crystals or boulders, and designers could not defend a design against someone else's horror story. Phyllis Buwalda, who led Future Mission Studies, wrote a specification of a hard, grainy surface with slopes under about fifteen degrees, scattered small stones and boulders no bigger than two feet, much like the Southwestern desert. She admitted it was a guess, but engineers cannot work without a specification, and if the moon were much harder, the whole manned program would be in trouble anyway. The spec absorbed ambiguity and handed designers a solvable problem; five Surveyors landed in 1966 and 1967. The lesson is that a leader must absorb much of a situation's complexity and pass on a simpler problem, and taking responsibility means setting proximate objectives, not just accepting blame.

**Taking a strong position and creating options.** The more dynamic a situation, the poorer foresight is, so the more proximate objectives should be. Herbert Goldhamer's description of chess masters shows the logic: most moves aim to improve position by increasing mobility and imposing stable patterns, and accumulated positional advantage is later cashed in tactically. Rumelt helped a small business school whose draft plan listed initiatives to "try harder." He asked its leaders for one feasible objective that would make the most difference. They proposed getting students into better jobs, and another group proposed raising the school's media profile. The groups merged them: pick ten target firms that should hire graduates but do not, create faculty committees to study their recruiting, and launch a media-management program so that two targets were media companies. Both ideas were strong positions pregnant with options.

**Hierarchies of objectives.** High-level proximate objectives create goals for lower units in a cascade of problem solving, and they also cascade in time, as when Nestlé bought Rowntree and moved its British brands abroad. What counts as proximate depends on accumulated skills. A helicopter pilot named PJ told Rumelt that flying must become automatic before you can concentrate on a crisis, night flying, formation or landing on a pitching ship. Skills are rungs on a ladder, and a start-up that has not mastered coordinating engineering, marketing and distribution cannot sensibly concentrate on opening European offices, while a new international firm cannot yet move knowledge around the world like Procter & Gamble.

> **Key takeaway.** A good objective is feasible and absorbs ambiguity for those who must act on it, and in uncertain conditions leaders should take strong, nearby positions that create options.


### Chapter 8: Chain-Link Systems

A system has chain-link logic when its performance is limited by its weakest link, so strengthening the other links does not help. Rumelt's emblem is the *Challenger* shuttle, destroyed in 1986 by the failure of a rubber O-ring: improving guidance, communications or crew training would have meant nothing while that link was weak. Quality matters where quantity is a poor substitute. Two one-ton trucks can replace a two-ton truck, but no number of short-order cooks replaces an ill three-star chef, and a hundred mediocre singers do not equal one great one. Keeping children longer in broken schools does not help. In home remodelling the limiting factors, such as highway noise or a poor paint job, cap a property's value, and a smart investor looks for limiting factors that can be fixed.

![A chain breaks at its weakest link, so strengthening other links changes nothing](/figures/good-strategy-bad-strategy/chain-link.svg "Redrawn: in a chain-link system the weak link sets overall performance, so upgrading any other link adds cost but no strength.")

**Getting stuck.** When each link is managed separately, a chain-linked system can sink into a low-effectiveness state because of quality matching: nobody has an incentive to improve their link if the other managers will not improve theirs. Improving one unit can even lower overall profit, since the extra cost buys no system gain. Rumelt sees this in General Motors from 1980 to 2008, where better transmissions meant little if knobs fell off the dashboard, door panels rattled and designs were pedestrian, and in economic development, where advanced machinery is useless to unskilled workers, educated workers are useless without jobs, better roads strain poor ports and better ports and roads attract corrupt demands. Homeland security faces the same logic: tighter passport checks do little with thousands of miles of border uncontrolled, and stopping two of three radiological attacks is not enough.

**Getting unstuck.** Marco Tinelli, who took over his family's machine company near Milan after his uncle died, faced falling machine quality, high costs and a sales force too unsophisticated to sell microprocessor-controlled machines. Rumelt recognised a stuck chain: better machines would be wasted by weak sales, better sales would add little without better machines, and neither would save the firm without lower costs. Marco ran three campaigns in order. First, twelve months solely on quality. Then a sales campaign that built skills, selling tools and links back to the factory. Last, nine months on cost, which he left to the end so cost cutting would work with, but not define, the machines. The big savings came from dropping two products and making dies in-house, which made the machines faster and thus cheaper for customers to run, a message that needed a sophisticated sales-engineering team. Rumelt notes what Marco did not say: he did not raise profit pressure, set quality metrics or bring in new managers. He gave the overall definition, absorbed the cost of change, congratulated managers on each proximate goal, and suspended normal local measurement and reward so that change itself became the objective, tilting the organization toward central direction for a time.

**Excellence.** The same logic explains durable excellence. IKEA designs ready-to-assemble furniture, sells through its own big suburban stores with catalogs standing in for salespeople, uses flat-pack designs to cut shipping and storage costs, lets customers collect their own stock and manages its own logistics while contracting out manufacturing. The strategy is hardly secret, but it is hard to copy because its policies are chain-linked and unusual for the industry. Adopting only one policy adds cost without competing with IKEA, and a rival would have to start afresh and compete with its own business. Three conditions sustain the advantage: each core activity is performed superbly, the activities are linked so that copying one is useless, and the grouping is unusual enough that expertise in one does not carry to the others. Rumelt concludes that excellence and being stuck are two faces of chain-link logic.

> **Key takeaway.** In chain-link systems the weakest link caps performance, so progress needs leaders who find the bottlenecks and fund fixes to every link, and a well-run chain of this kind is also very hard to imitate.


### Chapter 9: Using Design

Rumelt cautions that "strategy" comes from military affairs, yet business is less like a battle than a dance contest in which firms present offers to buyers, employees can quit, and size is more often a result than a cause of success. Still, careful military examples can teach fundamentals.

**The father of strategy.** At Cannae in 216 B.C., Hannibal faced eighty-five thousand Romans with about fifty-five thousand men. He drew his line in an arc bulging toward the Romans, with Spanish and Gallic troops in the centre and heavy Carthaginian infantry on the flanks. The centre fell back as ordered, drawing the Romans forward and compressing their ranks, while his cavalry rode around and defeated the Roman cavalry. The bulge reversed into a bow, the flanking infantry then turned in on both sides and the cavalry closed the rear. The Romans were so tightly massed they could not raise weapons, and numerical superiority was nullified. At least fifty thousand Romans died, a quarter of the Republic's elected leadership, and southern Italian cities went over to Hannibal. Historian Theodore Dodge called him "father of strategy," since Rome learned strategy through his teaching. Cannae shows three things in pure form: premeditation (winging it is not a strategy), anticipation of others' behaviour (the Romans were enticed into a trap built in part from their own vigour, Hannibal having studied their doctrine and baited the proud, impetuous consul Varro) and design of coordinated action in time and space, with mock retreat and recombined cavalry that no army had choreographed before. Rumelt adds that strategy is often not choice among clear alternatives but a designed, novel response, more like designing an aircraft than choosing a forklift.

**The parts of a whole.** Business strategy involves large design problems. Making a BMW 3 Series feel like a "driving machine" means tuning chassis, steering, suspension, engine and controls together; adding cost, brand, buying experience, other drivers and finally competitors makes the search space larger still. A competitive design finds a sweet spot of "smile per dollar" in a target market where you have a differential advantage, which usually means focusing on a narrower set of models, plants and customers. Design stresses mutual adjustment, since gains for good combinations and costs for bad ones are sharply peaked. Rumelt learned this as a systems engineer at JPL sketching a Jupiter probe (later Voyager). With 3,000 pounds the subsystems could be bolted together, but with 1,200 every choice rippled: less power for the radio meant a sharper antenna, better pointing, more sensors and fuel, and a longer power boom swayed. From this came his trade-off principle: a design-type strategy is an adroit configuration of resources and actions that yields advantage in a challenging situation. The greater the challenge, the greater the need for tight integration, and better resources lessen that need. Tight integration has costs, being harder to create, narrower and more fragile, which is why a Formula 1 car is faster than a Subaru Forester but far less versatile.

![Resources and integration as partial substitutes: more challenge calls for more integration or more resources](/figures/good-strategy-bad-strategy/integration-tradeoff.svg "Redrawn: Rumelt's trade-off between strategic resources and tight integration of actions, which are partial substitutes for meeting a given challenge.")

**The arc of enterprise.** Ordinary inputs such as trucks, graduates and lawyers are not strategic resources, which are durable assets built or discovered over time that rivals cannot duplicate without net loss. A strong resource makes strategy simple. Xerox's plain-paper copier patents let it sell for about $3,000 a machine that cost $700 to make, so its "strategic plans" were financial projections. But easy profits breed laxity, and leaders attribute results to recent habits, so books recommend successful firms' dress codes. Existing resources can also block new ones. Xerox's service system for failure-prone leased machines made it blind to paper handling, and Canon, Kodak and IBM took the lead. Success leads to laxity and bloat, which open the door to upstarts with tightly crafted designs, such as Canon, young Microsoft, Wal-Mart, Dell, FedEx, Enterprise, Nvidia and Google, which in turn loosen as they age. Rumelt advises studying design-type strategy in the upstart's early conquests, not in the mature company's posturing.

**Order out of chaos.** His extended example is Paccar, maker of Kenworth and Peterbilt, which earned 16 percent return on equity versus 12 percent for rivals and has not lost money since 1939 in a cyclical US heavy-truck market. Paccar sells on quality at premium prices, but fleet operators buy on cost per mile, quality takes years to prove, and buyers resist paying up front for future savings. Its design answers these obstacles by viewing quality through the owner-driver, for whom the cab is a home and the brand carries a classic American aura. Trucks are built to order through experienced dealers using 3-D displays, with common parts and supplier networks, and fleets are drawn along because drivers prefer them. It accepts higher variable costs for stable engineering talent and loyal, profitable dealers, makes only large premium trucks and builds slow-to-copy resources in image, dealers and know-how. A "Frankenstein" truck firm assembled from generic bits would not last. The threats to Paccar are changes that undermine its design logic, such as more Mexican trucking under NAFTA or software that reduces the dealer's role.

> **Key takeaway.** Strategy is often designed, not chosen: tightly fit policies, resources and actions into a coherent whole, with more integration when the challenge is high and resources are scarce.


### Chapter 10: Focus

Rumelt teaches executive MBAs the case of Crown Cork & Seal, a metal-container maker, to show how to identify a company's strategy, analyse unstructured information, and understand a policy-and-positioning mix called focus. John Connelly crafted Crown's strategy in the early 1960s, and over thirty-five years it delivered an average 19 percent annual return to shareholders in a brutal industry. The conventional explanation is that Crown specialised in hard-to-hold products such as aerosols and soft drinks, with great customer service. But Rumelt argues that most analysts stop there because analysing unstructured information is hard work, and that a strategy is not necessarily what the CEO says it is.

His method is to compare policies with industry norms and ask what distinctive policies are coordinated to accomplish. The big three can makers (Continental, National, American) accept being captive producers, with dedicated plants for one customer such as Miller Brewing and long runs to avoid costly line changeovers, and they earn only 4 to 5 percent return on assets. Crown beats them by fifty to sixty percent. A student argues that Crown is a low-cost producer, which Rumelt knows is wrong because its unit costs are almost certainly higher. A second student cites technical assistance and responsiveness, but another asks why putting soda in a can would be a high-profit business. Rumelt's trick is to replace general nouns with concrete examples: does Coors need technical help? Not really, since Coors invented the two-piece aluminum can, so the target of technical assistance is smaller businesses without canning expertise. The target of rapid response turns out to be rush orders from seasonal or new-product demand. Manufacturing policy adds smaller plants, none captive, each serving at least two customers, with excess lines waiting for orders. That means less production per customer and prices perhaps 40 to 50 percent higher than the majors'.

Putting it together, a student sees that Crown does short runs. Everything the majors avoided, small customers, rush orders and many changeovers, defines Crown's focus: shorter runs, which bring higher cost per can but also bargaining power. The majors with one customer and several rival suppliers gave up bargaining power by chasing long runs, whereas Crown is a supplier with many customers per plant. Crown and the majors played by different rules, and Crown captured a larger fraction of the value it created. Rumelt therefore defines focus as attacking a segment with a business system that delivers more value to that segment than others can, with two meanings: the coordination of policies producing extra power through their overlapping effects, and the application of that power to the right target. The logic was not secret, just unremarked. He adds that many large organizations have no real strategy at all because they pursue multiple goals and never concentrate resources enough to break through on any.

> **Key takeaway.** Focus means coordinating policies so they reinforce each other and aiming that combined power at the right target; to find a company's real strategy, look at where its policies differ from the industry norm and what they jointly aim at.


### Chapter 11: Growth

The chapter continues the Crown story. John Connelly retired in 1989 and died a year later; his protégé William Avery began growth by acquisition, saying the company's growth had slowed in the 1980s. With a team of deal specialists he doubled Crown by buying Continental Can's businesses in 1990 and 1991, then Constar, Van Dorn and Tri-Valley Growers, and in 1995 began an eighteen-month effort to buy CarnaudMetalBox, the biggest European container maker. By 1997 Crown had made twenty acquisitions and become the world's largest container maker. Avery's stated aim was to grow bigger and have a foundation for further growth, which Rumelt says is a way of wanting growth in order to grow. The plan assumed Crown's cost-control skill would trim CarnaudMetalBox, though its true competence had been flexibility and short runs.

Trouble came in 1998. Plastic PET containers were replacing glass and metal, but growth by substitution has a ceiling and then stops; PET unit sales even shrank when large two-quart bottles replaced several small ones. Prices in metal cans fell instead of rising, because no competitor would close European plants and PET competition spilled over, all of which Michael Porter's Five Forces framework could have predicted. Crown's share price fell from $55 to $5 between 1998 and 2001. Avery retired and John Conway shifted emphasis to cost, quality and technology; sales were flat from 2001 to 2006 and about $1 billion of debt was repaid. In the 1980s Crown had grown only 3.1 percent a year but returned 18.5 percent to shareholders against 8.6 percent for the S&P 500, whereas from 1990 to 2006 it grew rapidly yet returned only 2.4 percent against 9 percent, and its return on capital fell from 15.3 percent to below 10 and then below 5 percent. The root error was that Avery abandoned the traditional advantage, based on short runs and customer bargaining power, without replacing it. His CFO treated "focus" as merely a restricted product line.

Rumelt explains why growth is a poor target. In a commodity industry, growth is an industry phenomenon driven by overall demand, and firms without a competitive advantage plough profits back into new capacity, so the growing commodity industry absorbs more cash than it ever returns. The belief that growth itself creates value is almost unquestioned, and the word becomes a magical invocation. Acquisitions normally cost a premium of about 25 percent plus fees, so unless you can buy for less than value or add more value than anyone else, no value is created. Leaders still pursue them because they believe overhead falls with size, because they want to sideline executives, because bigger firms pay more, because deals are more fun than reading divisional reports, and because bankers, consultants and law firms earn large fees.

His illustration is a 1998 episode in which he advised Telecom Italia on a proposed merger with Cable & Wireless. Morgan Stanley's managing director cited "economies of scale", then said Telecom Italia needed to carry traffic on Cable & Wireless's cables, which a contract could do. He finally offered "economies of mass", meaning a bigger combined cash flow, which is arithmetic. When pressed, he said that with more cash flow you can do a bigger deal. The board rejected the merger two days later. Rumelt closes by saying healthy growth is not engineered. It is the outcome of growing demand for special capabilities, of superior products and skills, and of innovation, and it shows up as market share gains together with a superior profit rate.

> **Key takeaway.** Growth is a result of advantage, not a strategy: expanding for its own sake, especially by acquisition in commodity markets, destroys the focus and value that earned the growth in the first place.


### Chapter 12: Using Advantage

Rumelt begins from the point that advantage is rooted in asymmetry: two equal chess players or two identical armies on a plain have no advantage over each other. Among the countless asymmetries in real rivalry, the leader's job is to identify which are critical and can be turned into advantage.

**Wrestling the gorilla.** In 2000 a start-up developed a microporous material whose pores adjusted with temperature, for clothing that would be waterproof like Gore-Tex, warmer in cold and cooler in heat. The team wanted to build a textile and outdoor-wear company. Susan, the venture capitalist who knew them best, advised licensing the technology or selling to a major textile manufacturer, because the team had won an Olympic gold in the 1,500 metres and might win the 10,000 but wanted to switch to wrestling gorillas. No one has an advantage at everything: you must press where you have advantages, avoid situations where you lack them and exploit rivals' weaknesses. Rumelt applies this to Afghanistan. The US used its strength in high-intensity force to drive the Taliban from power but let bin Laden escape from Tora Bora, and later it shifted to winning the populace for a corrupt central government in a society with local, warlord-based loyalties, while the Taliban's terror tactics were effective and everyone knew America would leave, at a cost of about $1 million per soldier per year. The US was wrestling the gorilla, in a conflict where advantage lies with patience and indifference to casualties.

**Competitive advantage in business.** Michael Porter's 1984 book made the term standard and Warren Buffett looks for "sustainable competitive advantage." You have it if you produce at lower cost, deliver more perceived value, or both. Because costs vary with product and application and buyers differ, advantages extend only so far, for example Whole Foods over Albertsons only for some products and well-off organic buyers. An advantage is sustained only if rivals cannot duplicate the resources behind it, which requires an isolating mechanism such as a patent, reputation, relationships, network effects, large scale economies or tacit know-how. Apple's iPhone is protected by its brands, reputation, iTunes and app network effects. Because an "advantage" on sale to all comers is a contradiction in terms, claims that an IT system or training program will give competitive advantage misuse the term.

**"Interesting" advantages.** Rumelt visits Stewart Resnick, chairman of Roll International, a serial entrepreneur with success in alarms, Teleflora, the Franklin Mint, citrus, nuts, POM Wonderful, Fiji Water and Suterra. At Teleflora they changed from competing on price to a service model, and it grew from a tenth of FTD's size to twice it. Resnick says a business is "interesting" when he sees ways to increase its value. A small nut farmer takes yields and prices as given, but Roll's large holding allowed it to earn back research on yield and quality and to stimulate demand through branding. The seven-to-ten-year lag before competitors' trees mature gave time to plant, brand and build processing, whose economies of scale keep smaller farmers out. Private ownership helps because it frees the firm from quarterly pressure.

Rumelt links this to a thought experiment he and Steven Lippman made in 2002, a "silver machine" left by a UFO that produces $10 million of silver a year at zero cost. If it is sold for $100 million with a 10 percent interest rate, the buyer earns only an ordinary return, which puzzles people who equate advantage with wealth. The answer is that competitive advantage and financial gain differ, and the machine's advantage, though real, is not interesting because nobody can engineer an increase in its value. eBay is a more realistic case. Its advantage in person-to-person auctions is considerable, with $2.9 billion operating cash flow and a 26 percent after-tax margin in 2009, but its market value was stagnant for seven years. It would become interesting when someone finds unexploited ways to expand its value.

**Value-creating changes.** Wealth rises when competitive advantage rises or when demand for the underlying resources rises, through four routes: deepening advantages, broadening their extent, creating higher demand, and strengthening isolating mechanisms.

- **Deepening** means widening the surplus between buyer value and cost. It stalls when management thinks improvement is natural or achievable by pressure alone, as Frank Gilbreth showed by more than doubling bricklayer productivity just by raising pallets to chest height, using movable scaffolds and getting mortar consistency right, or when weak isolating mechanisms tempt firms to wait for others' improvements.
- **Broadening** extends an advantage into new fields, "build on your strengths" (eBay might use PayPal's payment skills for cell-phone payments). The danger is vague generalities such as "management" or "branded consumer products." DuPont moved from explosives to polymers, Lucite, Teflon and nylon, and knowledge is not used up when applied, whereas brand extensions can dilute the core. Disney's Mark Zoradi explained guidelines of no bad language, no uncomfortable sexual situations and no gratuitous violence, which let the brand cover *Pirates of the Caribbean* and *National Treasure*.
- **Creating higher demand** raises the value of scarce resources. Embraer gains when more buyers want small aircraft. The Resnicks kept 120 acres of pomegranate bushes because they earned more per acre than nuts, funded over $30 million of research on the fruit's health effects, expanded to six thousand acres, and created a new category, 100 percent pure refrigerated POM juice, with a heart-shaped "O." The huge plantings risked a "red tide" of unsold juice, but demand surged and POM led the category by 2004.
- **Strengthening isolating mechanisms** includes patents and brand protections, reducing turnover where know-how is collective, collective action to change rules (the rule of capture drove overpumping in oil fields, with forty-four wells on one block in Kilgore, until martial law and later regulation fixed it) and offering a moving target, as Windows does by constantly changing.

> **Key takeaway.** Advantage comes from asymmetry, so press where you are strong and avoid fights you cannot win; wealth grows only when you deepen, broaden, or demand-boost your advantage or protect it from imitation.


### Chapter 13: Using Dynamics

Rumelt starts from the classical idea that the defender prefers the high ground, a natural asymmetry. Academic strategy explains why such positions are valuable but skips how to get there, since easily captured positions fall as easily to the next attacker. Two routes exist: creating new high ground through innovation (Gore-Tex, FedEx's overnight system) or exploiting a wave of change. Waves of change are largely exogenous, the net result of shifts in technology, cost, competition, politics and buyer perception, and are like an earthquake that creates new high ground and levels old. Like wind in a racing boat's sails, they supply raw power that a leader must harness with insight and skill, channelling resources toward positions that will become valuable and defensible as the dynamics play out.

Rumelt warns against the claim that change is accelerating. Most industries are stable most of the time, and the fifty years from 1875 to 1925 brought electric light, streetcars, electric motors in factories, the telephone, radio, railroads, the car, the airplane, paved highways, tabulating machines and modern branding, a larger change in daily life and business than the last fifty years of television, jets, big-box stores, computers and the Internet. A wave is easy to measure after it has passed but too late to use, so the task is to perceive it early. The challenge is not forecasting but understanding past and present, as evidence lies in plain sight. Most people see only the main effects; the strategist digs for second-order consequences. Television hurt cinemas, but its subtler effect was ending the studios' B-movie routine and leading to independent production, with studios as financiers and distributors.

**Sensing the wave's swell.** In 1996 Rumelt met Jean-Bernard Lévy, CEO of Matra Communications, who said scale had been decisive in telecommunications equipment and that a firm without presence in two of Japan, Europe and North America would be a niche player. Rumelt asked how Cisco, started by two university staff members, had taken the internetworking market from giants including IBM, AT&T, Alcatel, NEC, Siemens and Matra. The CFO cited stock options; Lévy said the heart of Cisco's router was about 100,000 lines of firmware written by a team of two to five people, and Matra could not replicate it. That evening Rumelt saw the larger point: a router is a small computer built from chips everyone can buy, so the hard-to-copy element is skill embodied in software. Success was shifting from economies of size, the coordination of hordes of engineers in billion-dollar projects that underlay IBM, AT&T and Japan's engineering strength, to the cleverness of small teams, as if warfare had shifted from large armies to single combat. He sensed this reaching machine tools, bread makers and even cars, and shifting the wealth of nations. Riding such a wave requires getting into the gritty details and knowing enough to question experts.

**Software's advantage.** The common cause behind the rise of software and the deconstruction of the computer industry was the microprocessor. Rumelt, an undergraduate at Berkeley in 1963, remembers that putting more transistors on a chip made a one-chip processor obvious. Intel's 4004 arrived in 1971, but the chip business had two segments, commodity logic and memory chips, and proprietary custom chips whose rights belonged to the customer. Intel treated the 4004 as a custom design for Busicom, and when Busicom wanted a lower price, Intel got the right to sell the chip to others; the 8008 for CTC went the same way. It took years to see that a general-purpose chip specialised by software could be mass-produced, and Andy Grove said Intel took the 4004 for a sideshow for ten years. Why is software cheaper than custom hardware? Both kinds of engineers cost a lot, but trial and error is much cheaper in software, where a fix is a few lines of code, a recompile and a retry, even after shipment. The advantage of software comes from the rapidity of its development cycle.

**Why computing deconstructed.** Grove's *Only the Paranoid Survive* described an inflection point that turned the vertical computer industry (each maker built processors, memory, drives, keyboards and software, and parts did not interoperate) into a horizontal one with specialist suppliers. Rumelt asked why. A former IBM systems engineer who had lost his job explained: components are now smart. Keyboards, disk drives, screens and printers each have a microprocessor and speak standard messages, so the CPU asks for "sector 2032" without managing the drive. With smart components and a de facto standard operating system, systems integration became trivial, the glue of proprietary systems engineering lost its value, and the industry fragmented into specialists. The current web of relationships is the ghostly remnant of IBM's nerve, muscle and bone.

![Computing before and after the microprocessor: vertically integrated makers versus layers of specialist firms](/figures/good-strategy-bad-strategy/computing-deconstructed.svg "Redrawn: the vertical computer industry (each maker owns every layer) became horizontal as smart components removed the need for proprietary systems engineering.")

**Cisco rides the wave.** Cisco's story shows how to use waves. In the early 1980s Stanford's Ralph Gorin wanted to link incompatible networks, and staff members Andy Bechtolsheim and William Yeager built the "blue box." Len Bosack and Sandy Lerner refined it and formed Cisco, which got legal rights to the software in 1987 after a split with Stanford, for $167,000 and discounts. Professional managers (John Morgridge, then John Chambers) rode four waves: the microprocessor and the centrality of software (Cisco outsourced hardware manufacture and focused on software, sales and service, in Gorin's words "software that plugged into the wall, had a fan, and got warm"), corporate networking that needed multi-protocol routers, the shift to the free, vendor-neutral Internet Protocol while incumbents such as IBM (SNA), DEC (DECnet), Microsoft (NetBIOS) and Apple (AppleTalk) clung to their own protocols, and from 1993 public Internet use, which gave Cisco two thirds of the corporate networking market. Cisco worked around barriers, adding SNA support with IBM and telecom protocols such as ATM and Frame Relay. Its success was skill and some luck: IBM was pulling punches after thirteen years of antitrust litigation, the Internet arrived at the right time and incumbents were held back by inertia and proprietary strategies.

**Some guideposts.** Strategy skill shows most at moments of transition, as sailing skill needs wind. There is no framework for waves (Luis Alvarez said his course was labelled "advanced" because "we don't understand it very well"), but strategy only has to be more right than rivals', seeing perhaps 10 percent more clearly in the fog. Rumelt uses five guideposts.

- **Rising fixed costs.** When development costs jump, only the largest can cover them and industries consolidate. Colour film R&D left Kodak and Fuji and removed Ilford and Ansco, and rising costs underlay IBM's rise in the 1960s and the narrowing of jet engine makers to GE, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce.
- **Deregulation.** Regulated prices subsidise some buyers at the expense of others (rural air travellers, rural telephone customers, savings and loan depositors), yet newly deregulated firms keep chasing formerly profitable segments because of inertia, mental maps and cost data that hide real costs, closing profitable lines and continuing unprofitable ones.
- **Predictable biases.** Forecasters rarely predict a peak: durable goods such as flat-screen televisions and fax machines see a boom and then a sharp drop, and the faster the uptake, the sooner saturation (a client once said, "if you can't get that bump out of the forecast, I can find a consultant who will"). They also predict a "battle of the titans": the long-forecast convergence of computing and telecommunications, as championed by NEC's Koji Kobayashi, saw IBM and AT&T advance like sumo wrestlers only to have the floor crumble beneath them, and the 1998 forecast of global megacarriers ignored that one firm need not own all the networks, just as UPS need not own the roads. And advice tends to copy current winners: Delta's hub-and-spoke strategy depended on subsidies that deregulation removed, WorldCom's fibre rings were undercut when telephone companies cut prices, and portals such as Yahoo! and AOL were outgrown by the Web.
- **Incumbent response.** Incumbents resist transitions that undermine accumulated skills and positions (the subject of Chapter 14).
- **Attractor states.** An attractor state describes how an industry "should" work given technology and demand, evolving toward efficiency in meeting buyers' needs. Cisco's "IP everywhere" vision was an attractor state because it eliminated the margins of proprietary standards and put intelligence at the network's edges, unlike a self-serving corporate vision. Accelerants include demonstration effects like Napster, which showed that songs are data. Impediments include nuclear licensing in the United States, which takes ten years for a boiler change that France takes five years to do for a whole plant.

Rumelt applies the attractor-state idea to newspapers. The *New York Times* earned about $668 million in subscriptions in 2008, enough for newsroom costs, but printing and distribution cost two or three times subscription revenue and had been covered by advertising, which was now collapsing as readers got free news and advertisers moved to targeted media such as Google. He argues that the attractor state has specialists along territory, frequency and depth rather than generalist bundles. That would mean local news, weather and sports in a leaner daily, in-depth analysis in a weekly magazine, national and world news online and on mobile devices, and aggregation of content from many writers, so the strategic challenge is unbundling, not just "moving online."

> **Key takeaway.** Exogenous waves of change reshuffle advantage; look beneath surface effects for the underlying forces (here, the microprocessor and software), read the guideposts and position for the industry's likely attractor state.


### Chapter 14: Inertia and Entropy

Rumelt defines inertia as an organization's unwillingness or inability to adapt to changing circumstances, like a supertanker that needs a mile to stop. Entropy is a second force: weakly managed organizations drift toward disorder and lose focus, so leaders must keep working at purpose, form and method even when strategy and competition do not change. Two implications follow. Successful strategies often depend on rivals' inertia, as when Netflix passed Blockbuster, which could not abandon stores, or Apple and Google moved through the opening left by Microsoft's slowness in mobile software. And an organization's biggest challenge may be its own entropy and inertia, so renewal becomes a strategic problem needing diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions to change routines, culture and the structure of power.

**The inertia of routine.** Routines encapsulate layers of experience, limit action to the familiar and shape what managers perceive. Outside shocks reveal them. After US airline deregulation in 1978, Rumelt was asked to help Continental decide how to spend about $400 million on new aircraft. Under the Civil Aeronautics Board, short routes had fares below cost and long routes above, and airlines were forced to fly both. Cost per available-seat-mile falls with distance (about $0.22 for Los Angeles to Phoenix versus about $0.09 for a 2,000-mile trip). Rumelt forecast that fares would converge toward costs, with short-haul prices rising and long-haul prices falling, and he guessed that employers paying business travel would not pay a premium for comfort. The industry assumed the opposite: United's CEO planned $3 billion for long-haul planes. Continental's executives rejected the forecast because "we already have a planning model." Rumelt found it was the Boeing Fleet Planner, which computed cost per seat-mile, assumed a 55 percent load factor and added a 12 percent return on capital, a fare predictor only if a regulator guaranteed the markup. The CAB's "fair return" also guaranteed debt payment, giving the rule "reequip when everyone else does." After deregulation, the old rules failed: United, American and Eastern lost $240 million in 1981 while shorter-haul carriers earned profits, and in 1984 and 1985 long-haul fares fell 27 percent while short-haul fares rose 40 percent. Continental's strike, scuttled aircraft purchase, hostile takeover by Frank Lorenzo and the suicide of CEO Al Feldman followed. Inertia from obsolete routines is fixable once senior leaders are convinced, by hiring from better firms, acquiring one, using consultants or redesigning routines, usually replacing people invested in the old methods.

**The inertia of culture.** In 1984 Rumelt worked on strategy at AT&T, which invented Unix and the transistor yet could not make working consumer products: its first cellular market test used Motorola equipment, its videotex trial with Knight Ridder needed software from a small outside firm, and Bell Labs quoted three million dollars and two years for a simple demonstration that Rumelt wrote himself in three weeks, after being told not to interfere with "design prerogatives." The problem was culture, with breakthroughs from a few talented individuals used to justify a contemplative life for thousands. A good product-market strategy is useless if assumed competencies are absent and blocked by long-established culture. After Lucent's spin-off and a stock run from $8 to $80, it collapsed below $1 in 2002. Culture strongly resists change (Pol Pot killed a fifth of Cambodians and left the culture largely intact). Rumelt's recipe for organizations: first simplify, stripping layers and nonessential operations and disbanding coordinating committees; then fragment operating units that are separable, to break coalitions and cross-subsidies; then triage into units to close, repair or use as nuclei, judging culture as well as performance; replace the "alpha" who sets group norms and set a challenging goal to build new habits; and finally reinstall coordination.

**Inertia by proxy.** Sometimes lack of response reflects choice, because responding would undermine profit streams protected by customers' own inertia. When interest rates hit 20 percent in 1980, older banks did not push money-market accounts to retired depositors, one Philadelphia Savings Fund executive calling them "the last giant pool of 5 percent money left on the planet." In 1998, DSL ran about a third of T1 speed at a thirtieth of the price, yet telephone companies serving big cities declined to offer it rather than cannibalise $4,000-per-month T1 lines, losing about 10 percent of corporate data business a year to new carriers such as WorldCom and the CLECs. The attackers' growth looked like competitive superiority, until the phone companies responded in 2000 and no CLEC survived. Inertia by proxy ends suddenly when the firm decides adaptation matters more, and attackers' customers, self-selected as price-sensitive, may leave quickly unless bonds of cost and loyalty have been built.

**Entropy.** Rumelt compares an unmanaged firm to an untended house: the product line is less focused, prices are set low to please sales, shipping schedules are long to please the factory, and bonuses go to executives who out-compete each other. Undoing entropy is the bread and butter of consultants. Denton's Inc., a family garden-supply chain with twenty-eight outlets, measured return on capital using old purchase prices (land at $5,000 an acre in 1950 versus $95,000 in 1989), which confounded retail with real estate gains. Rumelt created a measure called gain to operating (GTO) and ranked outlets. The best outlet earned $1.05 million and the worst lost $0.97 million, and the whole chain's GTO was $0.32 million against $8 million of reported net income. His cumulative "hump chart" rises to about $4.68 million and then sags as outlets 15 through 28 lose a combined $4.4 million, so the best outlet earned more than the whole chain. Cross-subsidies persisted because incentive payments were left out of location profit and paid on corporate totals. After two years of closing five weak outlets and a best-practices program (garden-style layouts, richer plant descriptions, collections that drive impulse purchases, separate sales areas), GTO rose from $100,000 to over $5 million and accounting profit doubled, from "just management" undoing years of clutter.

The larger example is General Motors. In 1921 Ford had 62 percent of the US car market with the $495 Model T, while GM's ten lines held about 12 percent, with six divisions offering cars in the $1,800 to $2,200 range and several losing money. Alfred Sloan's product policy gave each brand its own price band, so that "each car in the line should properly be conceived in its relationship to the line as a whole," with quality competition against cheaper cars and price competition against dearer ones. GM became the largest automaker by 1931, and the ladder from Chevrolet to Cadillac became part of American culture. But design decays without active maintenance: decentralised executives push brands into each other's price points, as Chevrolet reaching up and Pontiac reaching down. By the 1980s the design had faded into badge engineering; Oldsmobile closed in 2001, and in 2008 GM offered nine vehicles at about $25,500 against Toyota's two. Internal competition shrinks the corporate pie because investment takes business from sister brands and pushes down both brands' prices. GM went bankrupt in 2009 and dropped Saturn, Pontiac and Hummer. Rumelt contrasts Denton's, where entropy could be fixed because leaders were willing, with GM, where entropy joined inertia of routine, culture and chain-link systems.

> **Key takeaway.** Organizations resist change through routine, culture and customer-protected profit streams, and drift into disorder without constant management; both are strategic problems that require diagnosis, simplification and active maintenance of design.


### Chapter 15: Putting It Together

This chapter shows the building blocks of the book working together in a single story, Nvidia's rise in 3-D graphics chips. Nvidia went from nowhere to dominate the high-performance 3-D graphics chip market, and Forbes named it Company of the Year in 2007 after its shares rose 21-fold since its 1999 listing. Rumelt says the story contains the full kernel of diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action, along with anticipation, a policy that reduced complexity, design, focus, advantage, a wave of change and the inertia and disarray of rivals.

**Background.** 3-D graphics lets a user move an imaginary camera through a scene the computer "knows" in three dimensions. The University of Utah, under Ivan Sutherland and David Evans, produced the field's pioneers, including John Warnock, Nolan Bushnell, Edwin Catmull and Jim Clark. Clark founded Silicon Graphics in 1982 to build the fastest graphics workstations, and its hardware and GL language framed the "graphics pipeline," which breaks scenes into triangles, processes each and reassembles them. SGI's 1992 Reality Engine put the whole pipeline in hardware, stood four feet high and cost over $100,000. By the early 1990s chips would soon be fast enough for PCs, but nobody knew the application. It turned out to be violent action games: id Software's Doom (1993), distributed free online with the registered version sold after players were hooked, and Quake in 1996 with online competitive play. In online play a poor graphics system meant a "lagged," out-of-date view and repeated death, creating a sharp demand for an edge, much as VisiCalc gave professionals an edge in 1979. The first to tap this was 3dfx, founded in 1994 by Silicon Graphics alumni, whose Voodoo board used its own Glide language (a cut-down GL) and a standard-setting plan taken from Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm*.

**Strategy at Nvidia.** Nvidia was formed in 1993 by Jen-Hsun Huang, Curtis Priem and Chris Malachowsky. Its first product, the 1995 NV1, aimed to be "the Sound Blaster of multimedia," but its audio was no better than rivals' and its quirky 3-D approach failed. With 3dfx rising, Huang reformulated the strategy with advice from a temporary technical board: drop multimedia for 3-D graphics on desktop PCs, adopt the SGI triangle method, and stay "fabless," designing chips and outsourcing fabrication. The industry advanced in lockstep on a "road map" of denser chips about every eighteen months (Moore's law). Nvidia's plan was to beat it for graphics, by anticipating large gains from putting more of the pipeline on one chip, and by using extra transistors for parallel processors, not simply for cheaper chips as Intel did. It judged that demand for graphics power was effectively limitless, as chief scientist David Kirk put it, with the graphics processing unit becoming the centre of consumer computing value. It also judged it would be self-defeating to adopt Glide and bet on Microsoft's unproven DirectX. Huang's central insight was that graphics power could advance three times faster than CPU power, so Nvidia could ship a substantial upgrade every six months instead of every eighteen.

A bad strategist would have wrapped this in slogans about speed and growth. Instead, Nvidia designed coherent actions:

- **Three overlapping teams**, each on an eighteen-month cycle, delivering a new product every six months.
- **Less delay and uncertainty**, because a two-month slip hurts a six-month cycle far more. Nvidia invested heavily in simulation and emulation, with Malachowsky pushing formal verification of the chip's logic and electrical simulation of its physical behaviour, to avoid a month-long fabrication round trip for each bug.
- **A unified driver architecture (UDA).** Drivers had been written by board makers only after receiving chips, with mixed incentives to share bug fixes, producing different drivers for identical chips. Nvidia took control, writing one driver that queries each chip for its capabilities and is downloadable online, and used hardware emulators to start driver work four to six months before chips arrived.

The benefit of the faster cycle is that Nvidia's product would be best in class about 83 percent of the time against an eighteen-month rival, plus the buzz of constant launches in place of advertising. The RIVA 128 (August 1997) was a moderate success that kept the company alive. The RIVA TNT (1998), released alongside DirectX 6 and using UDA, began to match or beat rivals, and seven months after the TNT2 came the GeForce 256, with about 23 million transistors, twice a Pentium II, and 50 gigaflops, on one $100 chip running faster than the $100,000 Reality Engine. As Nvidia led in performance, it tackled problems with board makers. Diamond Multimedia refused to cut its margins, so a senior team went to Dell, which agreed to offer boards with Nvidia chips made by Celestica, and thereafter Nvidia relied increasingly on contract manufacturers who mostly kept the Nvidia name. Performance rose 157 percent a year from 1997 to 2001 and about 62 percent a year from 2002 to 2007. Rumelt notes that technology change set off a change in industry structure that few foresaw. Powerful buyers like Dell, normally a negative for a supplier, helped a better product get past an incumbent brand, as they would not for a me-too product.

**The competition.** Great success usually has a complementary story of impeded competitive response. Rival 3dfx, following Wall Street advice and a new CEO's mass-market instincts, moved engineers to lower-tech boards, copied "Intel Inside" advertising and bought board maker STB, then stretched its next chip beyond its development capability and closed in late 2000, selling assets to Nvidia. Rumelt says that, like Hannibal at Cannae, Nvidia's fast cycle enticed its rival into overreaching. Intel's i740 failed because it used a CPU-style eighteen-to-twenty-four-month development process and would not redesign its processes for a sideline. SGI's new CEO told his team to "think out of the box and figure out how to grow 50 percent," then tried to buy growth through acquisitions of workstation firms, while Windows-Intel machines overtook its proprietary systems, and it went bankrupt in 2006 with no PC graphics entry. Rumelt calls "grow by 50 percent" classic bad strategy: a goal, not a response to the challenge, and growth is an outcome, not something you engineer. Only ATI survived as a serious rival, after buying ArtX, a team of ex-SGI engineers, in 2000, which let it match the six-month cycle. Rumelt calls Nvidia's passing on ArtX a blunder, since acquiring it would have denied scarce talent to a competitor.

**What next?** Success in 1998 to 2008 does not guarantee a future, since the waves Nvidia rode were waning: the SGI pipeline was fully realised, gamers waited less eagerly for new chips, and DirectX had become too complex for most game companies to master. Nvidia is running a two-pronged pincer. One is opening its chips' hundreds of floating-point processors to general computing via Tesla, which by November 2010 powered the world's fastest supercomputer in China. The other is Tegra, a complete system on a chip aimed at smartphones, netbooks and consoles, a "disruption from below" of the Intel-AMD-Windows hegemony. Both paths are uncertain.

> **Key takeaway.** Nvidia's rise shows the whole toolkit: a diagnosis of a shifting market, a guiding policy of a six-month cycle, tightly coordinated actions to make it real, and rivals who could not or would not respond.


## Part III: Thinking Like a Strategist

Part III turns from the sources of power to the strategist's own thinking: how to treat strategy as a science of hypotheses, how to use your judgment and avoid its traps, and how to keep your head in the face of bias, groupthink and false certainty.


### Chapter 16: The Science of Strategy

Rumelt argues that good strategy rests on functional knowledge about what works, what does not and why. Generally available knowledge is essential but rarely decisive because everyone has it; the most precious knowledge is proprietary, created by actively exploring the organization's chosen arena through what he calls scientific empiricism. A new strategy is, in the language of science, a hypothesis, and its implementation is an experiment from which leaders learn and adjust.

**Strategy is a hypothesis.** Rumelt describes working with Hughes Electronics engineers, standing before an eighteen-thousand-pound communications satellite. An experienced engineering manager, Barry, complained that strategy was nonsense because it lacked a theory showing what happens if you do A versus B, and that Hughes was good at meticulous planning. Rumelt answered with the analogy to science. A good scientist pushes to the edge of knowledge and forms a conjecture, tests it first against known laws and experience, then designs an experiment. A good business strategy likewise deals with the edge between known and unknown, where competition pushes us, and asking for a strategy guaranteed to work is as unreasonable as asking a scientist for a guaranteed-true hypothesis. The uneasy sense of ambiguity is "the scent of opportunity." A good strategy is an educated judgment, and the engineers know their business better than anyone. This broke the impasse. Rumelt realised the group had expected "crank winding", a deductive machine that outputs plans.

**Enlightenment and science.** If no new ideas are needed, deduction suffices, and "more of the same" may be right. But in a changing world a strategy needs an entrepreneurial component, new combinations of resources, and deduction does not produce interesting new ideas; even in mathematics stating a new theorem is a creative act. Deduction applies fixed rules to fixed facts (Newton's law gives Mars's orbit; tanker and refinery costs give the best oil flow), and the presumption that everything important is already known deadens innovation. Rumelt recounts how reason slumbered after Rome, assuming all important knowledge was revealed, until the Enlightenment (1630 to 1789). Its trigger was Galileo, who built a better spyglass in 1609 and quickly saw lunar mountains, the Milky Way's stars, the phases of Venus and Jupiter's moons, supporting the Copernican view. The Inquisition forbade his position in 1616 and sentenced him in 1630 to arrest for life, and Newton was born the year after Galileo died. The Enlightenment's answer to what to believe was scientific empiricism: trust the senses and the data from instruments, and test beliefs by experiment, so ideas must be refutable. A hypothesis cannot be deduced from existing knowledge or it would not be new; its worth is determined by empirical data, not by its author's popularity or wealth. A strategy is likewise an educated prediction judged by success, so good strategy work is empirical and pragmatic. Business seeks to understand particular situations rather than universal laws, but science is a method, and the basic method of good businesspeople is intense attention to data and to what works.

**Anomalies.** An anomaly is a fact that does not fit received wisdom, a blemish to some and an opportunity to others. Rumelt's example is galaxies. Gravity predicts that stars farther from the core should orbit more slowly, yet measurements since the early 1980s show nearly constant orbital speeds, "flat rotation curves," which implies either that most of the universe is invisible dark matter or that the theory of gravity is wrong. Anomalies appear by comparing facts with refined expectations; as Holmes told Watson, "you see, but you do not observe."

**The espresso anomaly.** In 1983 Howard Schultz, marketing manager for a small Seattle chain selling dark-roasted beans, visited Milan and saw espresso bars as theatre: baristas performing, customers in camaraderie, rapid turnover and relatively high prices, with 1,500 bars in Milan alone. For Schultz it was an anomaly. In Seattle quality coffee was a niche, and most Americans drank cheap, bland coffee. In Milan expensive quality coffee was the mass-market product, and "fast coffee" was served in a lively social setting rather than a plastic diner. His strategic hypothesis was that the Italian espresso experience could be re-created in America. Starbucks' owners gave him a small space but doubted it, believing their strength was buying and roasting beans.

**Coffee divergence.** Coffee shops were not new: the first English coffeehouse opened in Oxford in 1652, and English coffeehouses fuelled the Enlightenment, hosting Newton, Dryden, Adam Smith, Addison, Pope and Swift, and spawning Lloyd's of London and the Stock Exchange. In England tea replaced coffee, but in the United States the Boston Tea Party and later conflicts revived coffee as a cheap tea substitute, and the cheap robusta bean and instant coffee carried American habits far from Europe's. Meanwhile Luigi Bezzera invented the espresso machine in 1901, making a rich drink with a short-lived crema that needs expensive equipment and cannot easily be made at home. So what Schultz saw in Milan was the result of hundreds of years of divergent social history, and he wanted to change American tastes.

**Testing the hypothesis.** Schultz faced two problems. The change in consumer tastes was radical, and nothing about espresso bars was new or privileged, since millions had been to Italy. His insight was only a mood in his mind, a blessing and curse because it could not easily be shared to win backers. Luckily the hypothesis could be tested cheaply: one bar cost several hundred thousand dollars, not hundreds of millions. After leaving Starbucks he opened Il Giornale, a copy of a Milan bar, 700 square feet with no chairs, opera music, bow ties and an Italian menu, and treated it as a living experiment. Operating a business creates privileged information, one of the most important resources, because alert businesspeople can know more about their customers, products and production than anyone. Schultz dropped Italian from the menu, then the opera and the vests, added chairs, paper cups for takeout and nonfat milk. In 1987 his company bought Starbucks' retail operations and took its name, was profitable by 1990, went public in 1992 with 125 stores and by 2001 had 4,700 outlets and $2.6 billion in revenue. After hundreds of iterations, the original hypothesis was replaced by many new ones. This cycle of hypothesis, data, anomaly and new hypothesis is scientific induction.

**Capturing proprietary information.** Why did incumbents not respond? Joe Santos, a former CEO of Italian coffee company Segafredo Zanetti, told Rumelt that Starbucks was small beside Segafredo's fifty thousand weekly customers, that the big American roasters focused on the mass market, and that Europeans could not tell what Starbucks was. Roasters and restaurants are separate in Europe, a Starbucks is a retailer like McDonald's rather than a "coffee company," and its sit-down, takeout, milk-based drinks and company-owned chain model looked nothing like a family-run espresso bar. Viewed from Europe, it was "more of a milk company." Starbucks did not integrate vertically to confuse competitors but to adjust many elements of its business together and capture information from each. Integration is wasteful when good products can be bought outside, but when a strategy needs mutual adjustment of several elements and there is valuable learning in their interaction, owning and controlling them can be vital.

> **Key takeaway.** Treat strategy as a testable hypothesis: spot anomalies, form an educated guess, run it as a cheap experiment and let the proprietary information it generates guide your next version.


### Chapter 17: Using Your Head

Rumelt opens in 1967, as a nervous twenty-five-year-old Harvard doctoral student interviewing Fred Fletcher, general manager of Fansteel's Advanced Structures division. He simply asked obvious questions about the division's purpose, competition, strengths and weaknesses and management problems, and Fletcher said it was the most useful conversation he'd had all year. Rumelt did not understand why for fifteen years.

**Make a list.** At a lunch in Pittsburgh he heard the story of Andrew Carnegie offering Frederick Taylor ten thousand dollars for something worth hearing about management. Taylor said to list the ten most important things you can do and start on number one, and Carnegie paid. Rumelt saw that the value lay in constructing the list. The human mind is finite and attention is like a flashlight that lights one subject and darkens another, so people forget larger purposes, like a professional absorbed in career building who loses sight of family, or a CEO locked in a bidding contest who forgets why he wanted the company. Taylor's task was to think through the intersection of what was important and what was actionable, and Fletcher's interview had likewise reminded him of his priorities. A list counters forgetfulness, forces judgments about urgency and importance, and turns "things to worry about" into "things to do now." The common challenge across all strategy tools is working around one's own cognitive limitations, one's myopia, and being strategic means being less myopic than others, working with facts on the ground and not pretending to see the future.

**TiVo.** In 2005 Rumelt taught seventeen managers a case on TiVo, a thorny mix of technology, competition, intellectual property, manufacturing, standards, bargaining with cable and satellite providers and privacy. They had each written a one-paragraph recommendation. He asked how they arrived at it. The senior executive thought of unbundling disk capacity, so only heavy users pay for bigger disks; another saw that rivals would dominate cable while TiVo held satellite; another saw TiVo as an outsider loved by consumers and hated by the industry, like Napster. None could say where the idea came from, and almost everyone had picked a problem area and then grabbed the first solution that popped into mind, without reconsidering the problem area or looking at more than one response. This is predictable and usually efficient. A participant cited Malcolm Gladwell's *Blink*, which is right that instincts can make quick judgments about people and patterns, but research shows people are poor at judging likelihoods, their own competence relative to others (all of Rumelt's MBA students expect to be in the top half) and cause and effect; they favour vivid examples, see patterns in randomness and ignore contrary information. Nobody would want a president to go to war in the blink of an eye. Why do experienced executives close quickly? Strategy situations are ill-structured, with too many variables, no clear list of actions and no clear links to outcomes, and that is uncomfortable. The first idea is a relief, like a swimmer in choppy water finding something to hold. We then spend our effort justifying the idea instead of questioning it, a dodge of which we are not conscious. The most important personal skill, more than any framework, is thinking about your own thinking.

**Some techniques.** Strategy needs deep specifics and experience, but knowledge is not enough. Three habits matter: tools for fighting your own myopia, the ability to question your judgment (a strategy that cannot withstand a vigorous attack will not survive real competition) and recording judgments so you can improve. Rumelt offers three techniques.

- **The kernel.** Insight rarely arrives as a full strategy. It strikes one element: an action (smaller disks), a guiding policy (focus on cable) or a diagnosis (TiVo as consumer friend and industry thorn). The kernel reminds you to extend the insight into an internally consistent argument from facts to diagnosis to policy to action. Rumelt himself usually starts with a diagnosis and, when a client asks for an appraisal of an action, backs up to a better diagnosis.
- **Problem-solution.** Work backwards from what is being done to why, from the directions chosen to the problems they address. TiVo's engineering problem is time-shifting and skipping ads, where rivals include VCRs, video on demand and DVDs. A different view sees the obstacle as content aggregators (Comcast, Time Warner, DirecTV, EchoStar) bundling their set-top boxes with service, extracting most of TiVo's profit; as in antitrust cases against IBM, the regional phone companies and Microsoft, this suggests spending less on marketing and more on attacking the industry structure with larger allies.
- **Create-destroy.** People asked for alternatives add a shallow one or two, a straw man or a generic "walk away" or "more study." New alternatives should flow from the facts and address weaknesses in existing ones, which means trying to destroy your own ideas. Rumelt does this with a virtual panel of experts in his head: Bruce Scott asking why anyone should listen and what the action is, Alfred Chandler favouring scale, scope and enduring enterprises, David Teece noting that TiVo lacks key complementary assets and that cable and satellite firms would take any advertising profit, and Steve Jobs, whose bluntness ("Then you've failed," after learning UCLA had produced no known Silicon Valley entrepreneurs) joined the panel. Jobs would dislike TiVo, which controls too few variables, and want to integrate the machine, experience and delivery. Both suggest "corner solutions" that favour focus over compromise. A virtual personality works because people have built-in software for recognising personalities.

**Practicing judgment.** Judgments in business and politics are mostly about people, from oneself to individuals, small groups and large groups. Good judgment is partly innate but can be improved with practice, provided you commit your judgments to writing in advance. The MBA student who reads a case and then thinks "I thought of that" for every point in discussion has missed the chance to pre-commit and evaluate her own judgment. Committing to a judgment, especially a diagnosis, raises the chance of disagreeing with others and learning, so before any meeting write down what issues you expect and who will take which position.

> **Key takeaway.** The core strategic skill is managing your own thinking: resist quick closure on the first idea, generate and attack real alternatives, and commit judgments in writing so that you can learn from the results.


### Chapter 18: Keeping Your Head

Rumelt says good strategy comes from an independent, careful assessment of the situation, while bad strategy follows the crowd and substitutes popular slogans for insight. Being independent without being eccentric and doubting without being a curmudgeon is difficult. He tells two stories: Global Crossing, which is about assessing fundamentals when the stock market disagrees, and the 2008 financial crisis, which is about social herding and the "inside view."

**Global Crossing.** The first Atlantic telegraph cable (1866) cut a two-to-three-week sea passage to a click. The first transatlantic telephone cable followed in 1956, with thirty voice circuits at $250 million, and over the next forty years consortia of national telephone companies laid more cables whose capacity was split among members, with prices fixed by regulators and no competition. In 1997 two former AT&T managers, William Carter and Wallace Dawson, sought funding for a private cable, Atlantic Crossing. Gary Winnick's Pacific Capital Group put together $75 million of equity and $660 million of debt, and Global Crossing was formed. AC-1 was an 8,886-mile loop linking the US to the UK and Germany that more than doubled transatlantic capacity: 256 STM-1 units (each about 2,016 voice circuits), expected to double, for $750 million, or about $1.5 million per STM-1. Global Crossing sold units at $8 million each, below the $18 to $20 million consortium price. By the end of 1998, 35 percent was sold for $950 million, more than paying for construction. Six months after operations began the stock offering valued the firm at $19 billion, and six months later at $38 billion, more than Ford. The excitement rested on Internet traffic doubling every year, and George Gilder wrote in the annual report that the undersea portions would be an "agonizing choke point."

Yet the numbers argued otherwise. AC-2 would cost the same $750 million but carry 2,048 STM-1, a quarter of the unit cost, using non-proprietary optical technology, while a consortium planned 4,096 STM-1, another private project planned 4,096 STM-1 more, and engineers expected to nearly double AC-1 just by changing end electronics and to reach 20,480 STM-1 per new cable by 2001. David Cleevely told Rumelt that the "fat pipe" wins by scale economies. Rumelt realised that "cost" belongs to choices, not products: marginal cost, average cost and long-run average cost differ, and the cost of carrying one more call, or thousands of calls a day, was essentially zero whether the pipe was fat or narrow. With competition, nothing would keep prices from falling to cost. His UCLA MBA students did a Five Forces analysis of the industry: a near-perfect commodity, indistinguishable suppliers, new competitors, non-proprietary technology, cheap capacity and sunk costs that keep old cables running, "one struggles to imagine a worse industry structure." A student answered that the stock market said otherwise. Rumelt shows how the doctrine that stock prices are accurate forecasts shaped analysis: a 1998 Lehman report found terrestrial fibre capacity seventy times working capacity, then concluded that electronic agents and appliances would fill it, and consultants compared stock performance across strategies rather than evaluating their logic.

When he wrote the case in 2001, Winnick's final interview was cancelled because the company filed for bankruptcy in December 2001. The firm had built the "building" for $750 million and sold "condos" for over $2 billion. It was not just rogue CEOs: trillions of expected revenue never appeared. By 2001 Atlantic plans totalled 16,384 STM-1, enough for thirty-five million people to broadcast continuous video of their lives to each other. Resellers flooded the market, and the STM-1 price fell from $6.5 million to $2 million in late 1999 and to $325,000 by early 2002, 4 percent of Global Crossing's original price. Two things went wrong with demand. Undersea traffic grew more slowly than terrestrial traffic, because most traffic was local and busy sites copied servers city by city. And rapid growth came from near-zero prices, with browsing and piracy, not high-paying corporations. The collapse in prices could have been foreseen by a simple Five Forces analysis, but it was ignored because the market promised something better, and "judgment fled." Rumelt compares this to Gödel: in a closed circle in which investment, traders, prices and corporate decisions feed one another with the axiom of market accuracy at the centre, the question "are transatlantic cables an overcapacity commodity business?" becomes undecidable within the system, and you must look outside for an independent estimate. He sees similar circles in policy driven by opinion polls and in schools designing curricula from student ratings.

**Social herding and the inside view.** The 2008 crisis was the bursting of the largest credit bubble in history, in which easy credit pushes asset prices up and the assets become collateral for more borrowing. Bubbles without leverage, like the dot-com crash, hurt less, but overleveraged borrowers transmit shocks across firms and nations. Bear Stearns reported leverage of 32 to 1 (more like 50 to 1 in practice, and 85 to 1 in some hedge funds), and the first homeowners to default in 2006 had 90 percent mortgages plus piggyback loans and home-equity loans for furniture. Like Wile E. Coyote hanging in the air until he looks down, a bubble collapses when prices dip and leveraged owners rush to sell, which triggers "debt deflation" (Irving Fisher). Household debt rose from 60 percent of after-tax income in 1984 to 130 percent in 2007, while talk focused on government debt. Rumelt blames five intertwined errors of judgment:

1. **Engineering overreach**, building systems whose failure modes exceed the designers' ability to comprehend.
2. **The smooth-sailing fallacy**, assuming no recent tremors means no risk. Risk models using past price vibrations missed a design flaw (the premise that home prices never fall) that gave no signal until it collapsed, as with the *Hindenburg* or New Orleans levees; these are not "black swans."
3. **Risk-seeking incentives.** When you profit if things go well and others bear losses, you seek risk, and bailouts of New York City (1975), Continental Illinois (1984) and Long-Term Capital Management (1998), and executive pay with upside only, rewarded it.
4. **Social herding**, treating others' behaviour as information until everyone is calibrating off everyone else.
5. **The inside view**, Kahneman and Lovallo's tendency to ignore pertinent outside data and believe "this case is different," as with a driver who knows phone use multiplies accident risk five times but thinks the statistic does not apply to her, or the restaurateur who thinks his new restaurant will not fail.

In 2008 inside-view doctrines held that other nations' and eras' histories did not apply, that the Federal Reserve had abolished big swings, that "deep and liquid" markets could absorb shocks, and that new tools for pricing and parcelling risk worked. The outside view was plain: twenty-eight severe house-price boom-bust cycles and twenty-eight credit crunches in twenty-one OECD economies in fifty years, and American history repeating the pattern, from the 1819 depression (land sold on easy credit, cotton falling from 31 cents to 8 cents) through the Panic of 1837, the railroad-bond bubble and 1873 crash (about eighteen thousand business failures, a quarter of railroads, 14 percent unemployment), the Panic of 1893, and Melbourne's 1880s land boom and 1891 bust. The twentieth century shifted to housing: Hoover's "Own Your Own Home," Roosevelt's FHA and Fannie Mae, the GI Bill, Clinton's 1995 home-ownership strategy (looser mortgage standards: three years of stable income, paperwork in place of interviews, telephone-only lenders) and George W. Bush's plan for 5.5 million more minority homeowners. Treasury Secretary Paulson praised "deep and liquid" markets in late 2007, Bernanke spoke in 2004 of the "Great Moderation," Greenspan in 2005 and Geithner in 2006 praised derivatives and risk transfer, while home prices rose unmeasured in an inflation index that counted only rents, cheap imports held down consumer prices and low rates were "free." Rumelt hears hubris in these statements, as claims of benefit without the long real-world stress testing across cycles that would warrant them. The remedy for herding and the inside view is to attend to real-world data that refute the crowd's echo chamber and learn from history and from other places.

> **Key takeaway.** Keep your head by testing fundamentals against the crowd: when prices or popular opinion say one thing and the basic structure says another, look to the outside view and history before you believe that this time is different.


## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The first theme is that strategy is problem solving, not goal setting. From the Introduction onward, Rumelt insists that good strategy honestly acknowledges the challenge and offers a way to overcome it (Chapters 3 and 5). Goals, visions and values only say where you want to be, and bad strategy is what you get when leaders skip the diagnosis. The hallmarks in Chapter 3 (fluff, failure to face the challenge, goals mistaken for strategy and bad objectives) and the sources of bad strategy in Chapter 4 (avoiding choice, template planning and New Thought) are the negative image of the kernel.

The second theme is concentration and coherence as sources of power. Leverage and pivot points (Chapter 6), proximate objectives (Chapter 7), chain-link logic (Chapter 8), design (Chapter 9) and focus (Chapter 10) all say the same thing from different angles: a strategy gets its punch from coordinating actions around a few things that matter, and from saying no to the rest. The third theme is that advantage is dynamic. Chapters 11 to 14 show that growth, advantage, waves of change, inertia and entropy shape which positions are worth taking and whether an organization can hold them. Growth is a result of advantage, wealth comes from engineering increases in advantage and demand (Chapter 12), and organizations decay without active management (Chapter 14).

The fourth theme is the discipline of the strategist's own mind. Chapters 16 to 18 treat strategy as a hypothesis tested by evidence, ask leaders to notice their own myopia and quick closure, and warn against herding and the inside view. The cases are the book's real teaching tool: Apple, Desert Storm, Wal-Mart, Crown Cork & Seal, Paccar, Cisco, Nvidia, Starbucks, Global Crossing and the 2008 crisis.

### Strengths and limits

The strengths are clarity and usefulness. The kernel is a short, memorable test for any strategy, and the book's vocabulary (fluff, dog's dinner objectives, blue-sky objectives, proximate objective, chain-link system, isolating mechanism, attractor state) gives readers language for criticizing plans in their own organizations. The cases are rich and the author's personal involvement, from JPL to Telecom Italia, makes them credible and often funny. The book also treats strategy for non-profit and government settings as seriously as for business.

The limits are method and balance. Most of the evidence is the author's recollection of cases and conversations, selected to fit his thesis, and some of the later causal stories (the 2008 crisis, Afghanistan) are argued with strong opinions that the book does not fully support with evidence. The kernel can be hard to apply in complex organizations with competing stakeholders, because the book says more about what to avoid than about how to build agreement around a hard choice. The sources-of-power chapters are a catalogue of ideas more than a method for choosing among them.

*Where I'd push back:* Rumelt's insistence that growth cannot be a strategic goal and that the market's view is not evidence is persuasive in the Crown and Global Crossing cases, but it slights cases where scale, learning curves or network effects make growth itself a source of advantage, and hindsight does much of the work in showing that the dot-com and credit booms were "obviously" wrong.

### Applying it

1. **Write your kernel** (Chapter 5). In three sentences, state the diagnosis of the biggest challenge, the guiding policy and the first coordinated actions. If you cannot name the obstacle, you do not yet have a strategy.
2. **Cut the dog's dinner** (Chapter 3). Reduce the plan to one or two objectives that are feasible with current resources, make them proximate, and drop the rest.
3. **Find the pivot and the weak link** (Chapters 6 and 8). Ask where a small push has a large effect, and which link in the chain is limiting performance. Fix that before investing elsewhere.
4. **Check the fit** (Chapters 9 and 10). List your distinctive policies, and ask what they jointly aim at. Remove any policy that pulls away from that target.
5. **Treat the plan as a hypothesis** (Chapters 16 and 17). Write down your judgment before the meeting, attack your first idea with an alternative, and run cheap experiments to collect proprietary information.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 3, 5, 10 and 17. Chapter 3 teaches you to spot bad strategy, Chapter 5 gives the kernel, Chapter 10 shows focus working in a real case and Chapter 17 gives techniques for guarding your own judgment.

## Test Yourself

Answer these from memory, then open the ones you missed.

<details>
<summary>1. Why did Nelson's tactic at Trafalgar illustrate good strategy (Introduction)?</summary>

He found the pivotal factor, the Franco-Spanish fleet's coherence and weaker gunnery, and concentrated his columns there. Good strategy looks simple and focuses effort on one or two critical issues.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is good strategy often "unexpected" (Chapter 1)?</summary>

Most organizations spread resources and placate interests, so they do not expect rivals to focus. Jobs's cuts at Apple and Schwarzkopf's left hook at Desert Storm were surprising mainly because they were focused.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What did Sam Walton redefine, and why could Kmart not respond (Chapter 2)?</summary>

He redefined the store as a network of about 150 stores with shared logistics and data. Kmart's doctrine of decentralization, and the fit of Wal-Mart's complementary policies, made it hard to copy.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Name the four hallmarks of bad strategy (Chapter 3).</summary>

Fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy and bad strategic objectives (a dog's dinner of items or blue-sky wishes).

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What lesson does Digital Equipment's 1992 meeting teach (Chapter 4)?</summary>

Strategy requires choice. Seeking consensus among three equally powerful positions produced a fluffy compromise that avoided the hard work of choosing, and the company was acquired.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. What are the three elements of the kernel (Chapter 5)?</summary>

A diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy for dealing with it and a set of coherent actions that carry out the policy.

</details>

<details>
<summary>7. What are the three ingredients of leverage (Chapter 6)?</summary>

Anticipation of others' behaviour, insight into a pivot point and concentration of effort on it past the threshold needed to matter.

</details>

<details>
<summary>8. Why was the moon landing a proximate objective (Chapter 7)?</summary>

It was feasible with known technology and a larger US resource base, and Kennedy laid out the steps. A good objective absorbs ambiguity and gives people a problem they can solve.

</details>

<details>
<summary>9. What is a chain-link system, and why do such systems get stuck (Chapter 8)?</summary>

Performance is limited by the weakest link. Each link's manager has little reason to improve alone, so the system stays at low quality until a leader funds improvements across the whole chain.

</details>

<details>
<summary>10. What did Hannibal's Cannae show about strategy as design (Chapter 9)?</summary>

Strategy involves premeditation, anticipating others' behaviour and designing coordinated action in time and space. It is often constructed, not chosen from a list.

</details>

<details>
<summary>11. What is the trade-off between resources and integration (Chapter 9)?</summary>

They are partial substitutes: the greater the challenge, the more tight integration of actions is needed, and better resources reduce that need. Integration has costs in flexibility.

</details>

<details>
<summary>12. How did Crown Cork & Seal earn above-average profits (Chapter 10)?</summary>

By focusing on shorter runs for smaller customers and rush orders, with policies that raised cost per can but kept bargaining power and higher prices instead of becoming captive like the majors.

</details>

<details>
<summary>13. Why did growth by acquisition hurt Crown (Chapter 11)?</summary>

It abandoned the old focus, bought into commodity plastic containers where growth came from substitution and drove returns down. Growth is an outcome of advantage, not a strategy.

</details>

<details>
<summary>14. What are the four ways to increase the value of an advantage (Chapter 12)?</summary>

Deepen it, broaden it, create higher demand for the advantaged product and strengthen the isolating mechanisms that block imitation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>15. What caused the deconstruction of the computer industry (Chapter 13)?</summary>

The microprocessor made each component smart, which made systems integration trivial and removed IBM's and DEC's proprietary glue, so specialists in each layer could flourish.

</details>

<details>
<summary>16. Name the three kinds of organizational inertia (Chapter 14).</summary>

Inertia of routine, inertia of culture and inertia by proxy, in which an incumbent protects profits that depend on its customers' own inertia.

</details>

<details>
<summary>17. What does the Nvidia story demonstrate (Chapter 15)?</summary>

A diagnosis of a shifting market, a guiding policy of a six-month product cycle, coordinated actions (three teams, emulation, unified drivers) and rivals that could not respond.

</details>

<details>
<summary>18. In what sense is a strategy a hypothesis (Chapter 16)?</summary>

It is an educated judgment about what will work, tested against known principles and then run as an experiment. Results produce proprietary information that refines the next version, as Schultz did with the espresso bar.

</details>

<details>
<summary>19. What does Rumelt recommend to avoid quick closure on the first idea (Chapter 17)?</summary>

Use the kernel to extend an insight into a full argument, work backward from solutions to problems, create and then destroy alternatives (including with a virtual panel of experts) and commit judgments in writing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>20. What are social herding and the inside view (Chapter 18)?</summary>

Herding is taking others' behaviour as evidence until everyone is calibrating off each other. The inside view is believing that your case is different and ignoring relevant outside data and history.

</details>

### Teach it back

Try to explain the book in five sentences, from memory:

1. Good strategy is a coherent response to a specific challenge, not a goal, a vision or a plan to spend more and try harder.
2. Its kernel is a diagnosis of what is going on, a guiding policy and coordinated action.
3. Its power comes from leverage, feasible objectives, tight design and focus, and from riding waves of change while rivals are held back by inertia.
4. Bad strategy is fluff, avoids the challenge, mistakes goals for strategy and avoids hard choices.
5. Leaders must treat strategy as a testable hypothesis and guard their own judgment against quick closure, herding and the inside view.
