## Overview

*Psycho-Cybernetics* is a self-help classic first published in 1960 by Maxwell Maltz, a plastic surgeon who noticed that fixing a patient's face did not always fix the patient. Some people stayed unhappy after flawless surgery and kept acting as though they were still disfigured, while others were transformed by small changes. Maltz concluded that what mattered was the inner picture a person holds of themselves, the self-image, and he borrowed the language of cybernetics, the science of self-steering machines, to explain how that picture directs behavior. This summary follows the Updated and Expanded edition (2015), which keeps Maltz's text with light revision and adds a foreword and afterword by Matt Furey.

The book's own chapters set the numbering used here. The foreword and preface are summarized first without a number. Chapters 1 to 15 then follow in book order and are grouped into four parts that organize the argument: understanding the Success Mechanism (Chapters 1 to 5), building the habits of success (Chapters 6 to 9), releasing your real self (Chapters 10 to 13) and living the method (Chapters 14 and 15). The afterword closes the summary.

Maltz's method is a theory plus a set of daily exercises. The theory says that your brain and nervous system work like a servo-mechanism, a goal-seeking guidance system that steers by feedback and cannot tell a vividly imagined experience from a real one. The exercises, from mental rehearsal to relaxation to forgiveness, are meant to be practiced rather than merely read. He insists that new habits and images need at least 21 days to take hold.

Three ideas hold it together.

1. **The self-image governs behavior.** Your actions, feelings and even abilities stay consistent with your picture of yourself, so lasting change comes from changing that picture, not from willpower aimed at isolated habits.
2. **Imagination programs the Success Mechanism.** Because the nervous system responds to vivid imagery as if it were real, regular mental rehearsal of success gives the automatic guidance system a target and builds new patterns and feelings.
3. **Relaxation, forgiveness and gradualness let it work.** Strain, fear, resentment and perfectionism jam the mechanism, so the method asks you to relax, let go of the past, build confidence in small steps and trust the process.

## Foreword and Preface

The updated edition opens with a foreword by Matt Furey, president of the Psycho-Cybernetics Foundation, and Maxwell Maltz's original preface. Furey, a college wrestling champion who felt like a failure because he never won everything, describes starting a fitness business in 1987 with no clients and no confidence. A customer asked whether he had read *Psycho-Cybernetics* and described Maltz's key idea: you cannot rise above how you see yourself, because a mental blueprint controls where you think you belong. Furey followed the book's exercises, including replaying his best memories in what Maltz calls the Theatre of the Mind and then imagining future goals with the same feeling, and says he felt like a winner at once. He builds a career on the method and notes that the edition preserves Maltz's words with light updating plus his own commentary. His main lesson is that you can be happy now, not only after reaching your goals.

In the preface, Maltz, a plastic surgeon, explains why a surgeon is writing about psychology. Changing a face often changes a person's future, but some patients stayed unhappy after successful surgery and kept acting as if they were still ugly. Something other than the physical image had to be reconstructed: what he calls the "face of personality," the self-image. He found the mechanism in cybernetics, the science of goal-striving systems, which offered a concrete explanation for how a person pursues goals. He argues that the self-image sets the boundaries of what a person can accomplish, so expanding it expands "the area of the possible." Positive thinking works only when it is consistent with the self-image. Because the nervous system cannot tell the difference between an actual experience and one imagined vividly and in detail, a person can create "synthetic" experience in the mind to build a better self-image.

The preface also sets the terms for using the book. It is meant to be experienced, not simply read: Maltz leaves out chapter summaries and asks readers to write their own key points, to complete the exercises and to reserve judgment for 21 days, the minimum time he says an old mental image takes to dissolve and a new one to form. Finally he defines success as the satisfactory accomplishment of a goal that matters to you, not the pursuit of prestige symbols, and says striving for it also produces happiness, because people are built to be goal strivers.

> **Key takeaway.** Treat the book as a set of experiences to practice for at least 21 days, because the self-image changes through vivid experiencing, not through intellectual argument.

## Part One: Understanding Your Success Mechanism

### Chapter 1: The Self-Image: Your Key to a Better Life

Maltz opens with what he calls the most important psychological discovery of the century: each person carries a mental blueprint of "the sort of person I am," built from beliefs formed through past experience, successes and failures, humiliations and the reactions of others, especially in childhood. Once a belief enters this picture it becomes "true" and the person acts as if it were. Two discoveries make the self-image a key to a better life.

First, all your actions, feelings, behavior and even abilities stay consistent with your self-image, and you cannot act otherwise by willpower alone. A man who sees himself as a failure finds a way to fail even when opportunity is handed to him. A schoolboy who thinks he is dumb in math gets report cards that prove it, and a girl who feels unliked behaves in ways that invite rejection. Because experience seems to confirm the self-image, it rarely occurs to anyone that the problem is the self-image itself. The second discovery is that the self-image can be changed, at any age.

**Why positive thinking often fails.** Most people direct their efforts at the circumference of the self instead of the center, applying positive thoughts to a particular situation or habit while keeping the same self-image. Maltz says it is "literally impossible" to think positively about a situation while holding a negative concept of yourself, and compares it to putting new wine in old bottles. Once the concept of self changes, behavior consistent with it follows easily.

**Lecky's experiment.** Psychologist Prescott Lecky, a schoolteacher, saw personality as a system of ideas that must seem consistent, with the self-conception as its keystone. Students who "couldn't" learn a subject usually held a self-definition that made learning it inconsistent. When the self-definition changed, performance did: a student who misspelled 55 of 100 words became one of the school's best spellers, a girl who failed Latin four times passed, and a boy rejected by one college became a straight-A student at Columbia. The students identified with their mistakes, saying "I am a failure" rather than "I failed that test."

**What surgery taught Maltz.** As a plastic surgeon he saw correcting a facial defect transform some patients, such as a boy mocked for his large ears and a salesman scarred in a car accident. But a duchess with a corrected nose continued to act like an ugly duckling, and others insisted they looked unchanged. Meanwhile some scars bring pride, like the saber scars German duelists wore, and many people seek surgery for imaginary ugliness (a survey found 90 percent of college women unhappy with some part of their looks). The common factor was the self-image. Surgery changes personality only when it changes the picture a person holds of themselves, much as phantom-limb pain continues after amputation.

**What the self-image requires.** To live satisfyingly you need an adequate and realistic self-image you can accept and be proud of, one that matches reality and shows both strengths and weaknesses honestly, "neither more than you are nor less than you are." When it is secure you feel confident and express yourself freely, and when it is threatened you feel anxious and hostile.

**A new career and a new model of the mind.** Maltz concluded that people need a "spiritual face-lift" as well as surgery. He argues psychology had become too pessimistic and focused on abnormality, and he proposes an inborn "life instinct" that works through a Creative Mechanism, which becomes a Success Mechanism when used well. Cybernetics shows the "subconscious mind" is not a second mind but a goal-striving servo-mechanism of the brain and nervous system that the conscious mind operates, much like a guidance system. It works impersonally on the goals you give it, so negative goals and an unworthy self-image produce failure. The goals it pursues are mental images, the key one being the self-image, which sets "the area of the possible." The program consists of learning, practicing and experiencing new habits of thinking, imagining, remembering and acting, using creative mental picturing and "acting as if." In his words, "If you can remember, worry, or tie your shoe, you can succeed," and Furey's commentary adds that even one small memory of success is enough to begin.

> **Key takeaway.** Your behavior and abilities stay consistent with the picture you hold of yourself, so lasting change comes from changing that self-image, not from trying harder on isolated habits.

### Chapter 2: Discovering the Success Mechanism Within You

Maltz begins with the claim that, until recently, science had no unifying theory of how the brain works toward goals. Once engineers built goal-seeking machines they had to discover the principles involved, and cyberneticists such as Norbert Wiener and John von Neumann suspected the human brain works the same way. The word *cybernetics* comes from the Greek for "the steersman." Maltz coins *Psycho-Cybernetics* for these principles applied to the brain and insists that man is not a machine but *has* a machine that he uses.

**A built-in guidance system.** Every living thing has a goal-striving mechanism aimed at "living." Animals carry preset goals, which we call instincts: a squirrel stores nuts for a winter it has never seen. Humans have a Success Instinct that is far broader because they also have Creative Imagination and so can choose their own goals. Imagination is therefore creative in everything we do, as Napoleon, Dugald Stewart and Henry Kaiser noted.

**Two kinds of servo-mechanism.** In the first, the target is known and the system reaches it. A self-guided torpedo moves forward, senses when it is off course (negative feedback), corrects, and zigzags to its target; it ignores positive feedback and simply continues. Wiener held that picking a pen off a desk works the same way. The forebrain only selects the goal and triggers it, and an automatic mechanism handles muscle control through visual feedback. A baby's reach shows the correcting zigzag, and a new driver oversteers. Once a response succeeds it is stored and repeated as habit, while failures are forgotten. In the second kind, the target is unknown and the system must find it: groping in the dark for a pen, or scanning memory for a forgotten name. The brain acts like a computer with stored data, and Maltz cites Wilder Penfield's finding that stimulating parts of the cortex makes patients relive old memories. A center fielder who runs to a fly ball is performing computations faster than any conscious thought.

**The operator.** No computer, Maltz argues, has an "I," imagination, emotions or the ability to set and judge goals; humans are the operators of their machine. He also entertains the idea of a universal mind or storehouse of ideas, citing Emerson, Edison, J. B. Rhine's parapsychology experiments (presented as established) and the story of Louis Agassiz, who dreamed the missing features of a fossil fish. The practical lesson is to assume that an answer already exists somewhere and to search for it, since a scientist who knows a problem has a solution is already about 50 percent of the way there.

**Practice exercise and principles.** A new self-image cannot be imposed by willpower; you must see the old picture as an error. Because you were "engineered for success," the picture of yourself as meant to fail must be wrong. Read the chapter three times a week for 21 days and memorize five principles:
1. The Success Mechanism needs a goal, conceived as already existing in actual or potential form now.
2. It is oriented to end results, so think of the result and let the means take care of themselves. Furey adds that demanding the "how" before the goal is clear interferes.
3. Do not fear mistakes, since servo-mechanisms steer by errors and corrections.
4. Skill is learned by trial and error; then forget the failures and remember the successful response.
5. Trust the mechanism and let it work instead of forcing it. Act as if the capacity is there and it will come through.

> **Key takeaway.** Your brain works like a goal-seeking guidance system that steers by feedback, so give it a clear target, expect errors along the way and trust it to supply the means.

### Chapter 3: Imagination: The First Key to Your Success Mechanism

Maltz opens with a man of about forty who rarely left his room because he believed his nose and ears made him ugly and that everyone laughed at him. His features were ordinary. His imagination had set the failure mechanism running, and once he saw that his own imagination was responsible, he built a truer self-image using constructive imagination. Maltz's point, expanded in Furey's commentary, is that everyone uses imagination, either constructively or destructively, whether they realize it or not. We act, or fail to act, because of imagination more than will.

**The law of mind.** A person always feels and acts according to what he imagines to be true about himself and his environment. Hypnosis shows this dramatically: a subject told he is at the North Pole shivers and gets goose pimples, and wide-awake students asked to imagine one hand in ice water show a measurable temperature drop. Citing Theodore Barber, Maltz says hypnotic subjects behave differently because they believe differently, not because of some occult force. The nervous system reacts to what we think or imagine to be true and cannot distinguish an imagined experience from a real one. That is useful, since fear of a grizzly bear sets off adrenaline and the flight response automatically, but the fear is triggered by an *idea*, so a man in a bear costume or a shaggy dog would produce the same reaction. If your images of yourself are distorted, your responses to the world will be inappropriate.

**Mental practice works.** Since the nervous system cannot tell the difference, you can rehearse success. Maltz lists evidence:
- In a dart-throwing experiment, mental practice improved aim as much as physical throwing.
- In a basketball free-throw study, students who practiced daily improved 24 percent, those who did nothing did not improve, and those who imagined shooting (and correcting misses) for 20 minutes a day improved 23 percent.
- Furey describes baseball players at a pitching academy who learned imagery and relaxation; the number reaching 90 mph rose from 18 to 98 in eighteen months.
- Chess champion Alekhine trained for his title match by playing chess in his head for three months.
- Salesmen who role-played situations in their minds raised sales by 100 to 400 percent, and William Marston advised "rehearsal practice" for job interviews.
- Pianist Artur Schnabel practiced "in his head," a violinist with an injured wrist gave his best concert after playing mentally, Ben Hogan rehearsed each shot in imagination, and Alex Morrison taught golfers to improve while sitting in an armchair.

**The real secret.** Napoleon, Conrad Hilton and Henry Kaiser all pictured their achievements first. Cybernetics explains why: the Success Mechanism needs a target to shoot at. Instead of straining with willpower while picturing what could go wrong, you relax, picture the target and let the mechanism take over. Picturing the desired result also forces you to think positively and ends the conflict between what you want and what you picture.

**Finding your best self.** The same method shapes the self. Edward McGoldrick had alcoholics daily visualize a "mental motion picture" of themselves sober and enjoying life, and psychologists found that three-quarters of 45 hospitalized men improved their personality test scores simply by answering "as a typical, well-adjusted person" would. Maltz is careful to say the aim is not an inflated, arrogant self-image (a so-called superiority complex is really a cover for inferiority) but the real self, and he argues most people underrate themselves. He supports this with a religious argument that a loving Creator would not engineer people to fail and quotes Leslie Weatherhead on discarding a false picture of oneself as defeated.

**Practice exercise.** Set aside 30 minutes a day alone, relax, close your eyes and imagine watching a movie of yourself acting, feeling and reacting ideally. Make it vivid and detailed with sights, sounds, smells and textures; one patient overcame her fear of the dentist only after adding details like the smell of antiseptic. Do not promise yourself how you will act tomorrow; just imagine it now. Furey adds that results can start with five or ten minutes a day, that a wandering mind should simply be brought back without self-criticism, that you should recall a small past success and carry its feeling into the future picture, and that consistency matters most.

> **Key takeaway.** The nervous system responds to vividly imagined experience as if it were real, so daily detailed mental rehearsal of yourself acting successfully builds a new self-image and new habits.

### Chapter 4: Dehypnotize Yourself from False Beliefs

Maltz opens with Alfred Adler, who as a boy was labelled "dumb in mathematics" by his teacher and parents and accepted it until a flash of insight led him to solve a problem at the board that no one else could. A businessman patient believed that, because he did not "look like a successful executive," he could not give a good talk; once he replaced that belief with the conviction that he alone had an important message, he became a sought-after speaker. Maltz says Adler was literally hypnotized by a false belief. Hypnosis is the power of belief, so any idea you accept as true, from parents, teachers, advertisements or yourself, has the same power over you as a hypnotist's words.

**Examples of the power of belief.** Prescott Lecky concluded that poor grades are almost always tied to the student's self-definition ("I am dumb," "I am a poor speller"), so that doing well would be inconsistent. A consultant, Elmer Wheeler, found a salesman who earned about $5,000 a year in any territory because he saw himself as a $5,000-a-year man; he coasted or got sick once the figure was reached. Mr. Russell, a vigorous, magnetic man, aged twenty years in a few weeks after his angry girlfriend "cursed" him and a quack told him a small lump in his mouth was a fatal "African Bug." When Maltz removed the lump (merely scar tissue from the operation) and showed it to him, he recovered, regained his youth and married his childhood sweetheart.

**Everyone is hypnotized to some extent.** Under hypnosis a football player "cannot" lift his hand and a weightlifter "cannot" lift a pencil, because the negative idea makes opposing muscles contract. Another athlete's grip rose from 100 to 125 pounds when told he was stronger. The hypnotist adds no strength; he removes a mental block. It is truer to say the hypnotist "dehypnotizes" people from self-limiting beliefs like "I can't," "I'm not worthy" and "I don't deserve it."

**Curing an inferiority complex.** At least 95 percent of people suffer feelings of inferiority to some extent. Maltz argues these come from our *conclusions* about facts, not the facts themselves, and arise because we measure ourselves against another person's norm instead of our own. Being a worse dancer or weightlifter does not make anyone an "inferior person." Striving for superiority only compounds the error, since inferiority and superiority are two sides of a spurious coin. His summary is: "You are not 'inferior.' You are not 'superior.' You are simply 'You.'" Each person is unique, like every snowflake. A psychologist showed how an inferiority complex can be manufactured: he told students the "average person" finished a test in one-fifth the true time, and bright subjects fell apart when a bell signalled the average time was up.

**Relaxation as a tool.** Beliefs and habits were formed without effort, so they are best changed without effort. Émile Coué's Law of Reversed Effort says that when will and imagination conflict, imagination wins. Knight Dunlap found that effort to stop a habit reinforced it, and the best method is to hold a clear image of the desired end result and practice without strain. Dr. Greene's motto was "When they can relax, they can talk."

**Practice exercise (30 minutes daily).** After a few minutes of consciously letting go of tension, let the Creative Mechanism take over using four mental pictures: your limbs as heavy concrete sinking into the bed, your body as a loosely strung marionette, your body as balloons slowly deflating, and a detailed memory of a peaceful scene such as a mountain lake, fireplace or beach. Furey advises sitting or standing if you tend to fall asleep, says benefits can start with ten minutes, suggests practicing on waking and before bed (or whenever you can) and warns against judging yourself.

> **Key takeaway.** Beliefs you accept as true control your behavior as strongly as hypnosis, so identify the self-limiting ones, measure yourself against your own norm and use relaxation to replace them without strain.

### Chapter 5: How to Utilize the Power of Rational Thinking

Maltz expects patients to be disappointed when he prescribes something as simple as reason, but he says it works. The automatic mechanism, which Freudians call the unconscious, is impersonal and has no will; it reacts to the beliefs, interpretations and goals you feed it. Conscious thinking is the control knob. Neither thinking nor behavior can be changed only by digging in the unconscious, he argues, citing Dr. John Schindler's "conscious thought control": the problem is that the patient never learned to control his *present* thinking to produce enjoyment.

**Let sleeping dogs lie.** Like any servo-mechanism, the brain holds memories of errors, and they help when treated as negative feedback, a deviation from the positive goal. After you correct course, the error should be consciously forgotten and the successful attempt remembered. Dwelling on the error or berating yourself turns the failure itself into the goal. A woman with a repaired harelip kept replaying the past and behaving as before, until she stopped condemning her former self. Maltz says we are victims of our conscious conclusions ("I failed yesterday, so I'll fail today"), not of the buried past, and when we change our minds the past loses its power.

**Ignore past failures and act as if.** Under hypnosis shy people speak boldly because they give no thought to old failures. Dorothea Brande, reading psychologist F. M. H. Myers, wondered whether she could do the same awake by acting "as if it were impossible to fail," and within a year her productivity as a writer and lecturer rose sharply. Bertrand Russell described outgrowing his youthful gloom by turning attention from himself to the world, and recommended vividly examining irrational feelings, convincing yourself of their absurdity in detail and refusing to let them dominate.

**Ideas are changed by other ideas.** Lecky's clinical method shows a person that a negative self-concept is inconsistent with a deeper conviction, and an inconsistent idea must be rejected. Maltz asked a salesman terrified of "big shots" whether he would crawl into an office on all fours; he would not, so why mentally cringe? Lecky's two levers are the belief that one can hold up his end and the belief that something inside should not suffer indignity.

**Examine and reevaluate beliefs.** Trace negative behavior to the belief behind it and ask: Is there any rational reason? Could I be mistaken? Would I draw the same conclusion about another person? Why continue to act as if this were true? Get emotional about the answers. Indignation can liberate: Adler "got mad," a farmer quit tobacco when he realized a habit was making him walk two miles for it, and Clarence Darrow and a businessman each turned a doubting remark by a lender's wife into determination.

**Deep desire.** Rational thought works when joined with strong feeling. Worry shows how: you vividly picture an undesirable result, dwell on it, and fear follows. Reverse the goal picture to generate enthusiasm, and, in Knight Dunlap's words, deal with thought habits.

**What reason can and cannot do.** Conscious thought's job is to supply true facts (most people underrate themselves and overrate difficulty), reject untrue messages ("It ain't necessarily so"), conclude logically, decide what you want rather than what you do not want (Eisenhower refused to think about failure), and pay strict attention to the task, keeping your eye on the ball. It is not its job to create the result. Straining to force creative thought causes anxiety. You gather information, act on the best assumptions and trust the mechanism. Furey suggests that public speakers picture speaking to friends and expand the picture to a crowd.

> **Key takeaway.** Use rational thought to examine beliefs, reject untrue ones, forget past errors and set a clear positive goal, then let the automatic mechanism produce results.

## Part Two: Building the Habits of Success

### Chapter 6: Relax and Let Your Success Mechanism Work for You

Maltz argues that stress, worry and insomnia are not inevitable, because people ignore their built-in Creative Mechanism and try to solve everything with the forebrain. The forebrain is the "operator": it sets goals, gathers information and evaluates, but it cannot create or "do" the job, and by nature it was never built to solve problems. He cites William James's essay "The Gospel of Relaxation": confine anxiety and prudence to planning, and once you decide, "dismiss absolutely all responsibility and care about the outcome." James's later work on "victory by surrender" adds that people who struggled for years with anxiety and guilt found relief when they stopped straining and let go.

**Creative thinking and doing.** Writers, inventors and scientists consistently report that ideas arrive "like a bolt out of the blue" when the conscious mind is occupied elsewhere. Examples include Edison's naps, Darwin's carriage-ride insight, Henri Fehr, GE research chief C. G. Suits and Bertrand Russell, who thinks hard about a topic for a few days and then lets it "proceed underground." Preconditions matter, however: intense interest, gathered facts, a clear picture of the desired result and a burning desire. Furey adds that many creators find ideas in water or sleep and suggests asking your mind a question before bed and keeping a notepad nearby. Everyone is a "creative worker." Skill, whether in piano, selling or conversation, consists in relaxing and letting the habit mechanism perform, not in consciously directing each step. Self-conscious, "inhibited" people have really inhibited their own Creative Mechanism. Furey's commentary says to stay in "journey mode" most of the time and tune in to the goal only once or twice a day, since obsessing over where you are in relation to the goal jams the mechanism.

**Five rules for freeing your creative machinery.**
1. **Do your worrying before you place your bet, not after the wheel starts turning.** A businessman's roulette-table insight: weigh risks before deciding, and once a decision is made, stop worrying about the outcome. Much nervousness comes from mentally trying to escape something you've chosen to do.
2. **Respond consciously to the present moment.** The mechanism can only function in the present. Live, as William Osler advised, in "day-tight compartments" (the AA approach of not drinking *today* is the same). Stop, look and listen to your surroundings, and ask what you can respond to here and now. Don't fight straw men from the past: a patient's anxiety in groups came from reacting as if every meeting were the classroom where he'd been humiliated as a child.
3. **Do only one thing at a time.** James Gordon Gilkey's hourglass image shows that duties pass one grain at a time. Maltz tells patients to feed the Success Mechanism's "hopper" one job at a time. Furey says to pick one goal and let that fire spread.
4. **Sleep on it.** The Creative Mechanism works well during sleep, like the elves helping the shoemaker. Edison, Walter Scott, Henry Ward Beecher, Kekulé, Otto Loewi and Robert Louis Stevenson got solutions or ideas this way, and so did businessmen like Vic Pocker.
5. **Relax while you work.** Recall in detail the relaxed feelings from your daily practice several times a day, and tell yourself "I feel more and more relaxed."

> **Key takeaway.** Do your planning and worrying before you commit, then relax and give your attention to the present, one task at a time, so the Creative Mechanism can work without being jammed by strain.

### Chapter 7: You Can Acquire the Habit of Happiness

Maltz treats happiness medically, using John Schindler's definition: "a state of mind in which our thinking is pleasant a good share of the time." He argues that happiness is native to the mind and makes us function better. In experiments people thinking pleasant thoughts saw, tasted, smelled and heard better, eyesight improved with pleasant visualization, memory improved, and psychosomatic medicine links organ function to mood. Harvard and Yale research linked criminality and hostility to unhappy homes and frustration, and surveys found cheerful businessmen more successful. We have the cart before the horse, he says: "be happy and you will be good, more successful and healthier," not the reverse.

**Misconceptions.** Happiness is not earned or deserved and is not a moral issue, any more than blood circulation is (Spinoza called it virtue itself, not the reward of virtue). Seeking it is not selfish, and making it a reward for self-sacrifice leads to guilt about wanting it. William James called an attitude of unhappiness "mean and ugly." Nor does happiness lie in the future. Pascal said we are always hoping to live, and Maltz sees many patients living "on the deferred payment plan," waiting for a marriage, a job or a paid-off house. Happiness is a mental habit that must be practiced now; a caliph who had everything counted only fourteen happy days in fifty years. Lincoln's remark that most people are as happy as they make up their minds to be fits.

**Don't be pushed around.** Much unhappiness is a habitual reaction to events we read as blows to our self-esteem. Maltz compares people to a TV audience obeying the "applause" sign: they let events tell them how to feel. Learning the happiness habit makes you the master. And we often add our *opinion* to the facts, as Epictetus said. Losing $200,000 is a fact, but being "ruined and disgraced" is an opinion. Maltz applies this to himself: being told he could never become a doctor, study in Germany or open a practice in New York were opinions, not facts.

**Goal-striving.** Happiness is a symptom of natural functioning as a goal striver, so it returns when you are working toward a goal. Edison, whose uninsured laboratory burned, said they would start rebuilding the next morning. Maltz's executive was unhappy not because he'd lost the money but because he'd lost his goal; once he faced about and set a new one, he did better than ever within five years. The exercise: form the habit of reacting aggressively and positively to problems, in life and in imagination.

**Practicing healthy-mindedness.** Happiness is something you do, not something that happens to you. Every day contains justification for pessimism or optimism, so choose what to focus on. Carl Erskine recalled a fishing spot to ease tension, and Gene Tunney protected himself by closing his mind to newspaper predictions that Dempsey would win. A salesman worried about his nose cut out negative thoughts for 30 days and saw his sales and relationships improve. A famous scientist, gloomy until fifty, tried a month of dwelling only on pleasant thoughts, and his headaches ended and his work was published. Inventor Elmer Gates recalled pleasant memories daily as exercise, and Furey says remembering your best moments reconnects you to good feeling.

**Learning the habit.** Habits fit the self-image, and changing one changes the other, though habits are not addictions (which Maltz sets aside). About 95 percent of behavior is habitual, but habits can be changed by a conscious decision followed by practice. **Practice exercise:** for 21 days change how you put on and tie your shoes, using it as a cue to resolve to be cheerful, friendly, tolerant, to act as if success is inevitable, not to color facts with negative opinion, to smile at least three times, to react calmly and to ignore negative facts you cannot change.

> **Key takeaway.** Happiness is a habit of pleasant thinking that you can practice now by separating facts from your opinions, keeping a goal and deliberately focusing on what is good.

### Chapter 8: Ingredients of the "Success-Type" Personality and How to Acquire Them

Maltz says failure and success can be diagnosed like disease, because people carry their seeds in their personalities. To help patients, he first gives them a picture of the successful personality, since the Success Mechanism needs a clear target. A good personality, he says, deals effectively with reality and gains satisfaction from important goals. Two patients show why this matters: an advertising executive who felt like a "small potato" right after a promotion and blamed his chin, and a stay-at-home mother who snapped at her family and thought a face-lift would make them appreciate her. Both were in new roles without a matching self-image. Maltz offers the acronym SUCCESS, with a "prescription" for each element.

- **Sense of direction.** The executive had lost his own goals once he reached the top and had begun defending his position, like a climber who looks down. A person is like a bicycle that keeps its balance only by moving toward something. *Prescription:* get a goal or project worth working for, have something to look forward to, develop a "nostalgia for the future," and add at least one cause beyond yourself. (Furey notes that "project" or "what would you like to do?" can work better than "goal" for some people.)
- **Understanding.** Confusion is at the root of much neurosis and most human-relations failures. People react to their mental images, not to things as they are, so ask how a situation appears to the other person. Separate fact from opinion: a husband cracking his knuckles is a fact; "he does it to annoy me" is an opinion. Be willing to see the truth, like Hitler's advisers who dared not tell him. *Prescription:* seek true information, ask what is right and not who is right, and admit mistakes without crying over them.
- **Courage.** Goals and understanding are useless without action. Admiral Halsey said that problems shrink if you confront them. Faith is the courage to act regardless of consequences. Maltz proposes that the gambling instinct is really an urge to bet on yourself. *Prescription:* study options, pick the most promising and go ahead, because you can correct course only when moving, and practice courage on small things.
- **Compassion.** Our feelings about ourselves match our feelings about others, so condemning others perpetuates guilt. *Prescription:* appreciate people as unique creations, stop to consider their feelings, and act as if others are important.
- **Esteem.** Self-disesteem is the deadliest trap ("It's no use. I can't do it"), and it breeds jealousy and irritability. *Prescription:* drop the picture of yourself as defeated, use the exercises, and appreciate others; self-esteem comes from what you *are*, not what you've achieved.
- **Self-confidence.** Confidence comes from experience of success, so start small, as boxing managers match their fighters. Practice improves skill because the brain reinforces hits and forgets misses. *Prescription:* learn from errors and dismiss them, and deliberately recall past successes and brave moments (Furey: reword "success" as "happy moments" for people who draw a blank).
- **Self-acceptance.** A better self-image does not create a new self but releases what is already there. You can change your personality, which is a set of habits, but not your basic self. You are not your mistakes: they belong to you but are not you. The neurotic rejects the imperfect actual self for a fictitious perfect one. *Prescription:* accept yourself as you are, tolerate imperfection and start from there.

> **Key takeaway.** The SUCCESS checklist (sense of direction, understanding, courage, compassion, esteem, self-confidence, self-acceptance) gives you a clear picture to aim at, and each trait can be built with simple practices.

### Chapter 9: The Failure Mechanism: How to Make It Work for You Instead of Against You

Warning lights and pain help the body and the driver, and Maltz says the failure-type personality has similar signals, which work as negative feedback if we recognize them and correct course. Everyone, even the successful, experiences them at times. He gives a mnemonic, FAILURE: **F**rustration, **A**ggressiveness, **I**nsecurity, **L**oneliness, **U**ncertainty, **R**esentment and **E**mptiness. Each began as a mistaken "way" of solving a problem, with some meaning and purpose, so understanding that it does not work cures it better than willpower.

**Frustration.** Some frustration is part of being human. It becomes a failure symptom when it is excessive, which usually means unrealistic goals, an inadequate self-image or both. Jim S., a vice president with a good family and golf game, was chronically frustrated because he "should be" chairman and shoot in the seventies; Maltz advised aiming at an area "the size of a washtub," as a pro does on a long putt. Harry N. had a self-image of an unworthy person and always sabotaged his chances. Frustration is also a childhood strategy (crying brings help) that does not work for adults.

**Aggressiveness.** Aggression is necessary to reach goals, but when blocked, the steam turns on others (the worker who snaps at his family), on oneself (ulcers, overwork) or into trying harder at an impossible task. Citing Konrad Lorenz, Maltz says aggression should be understood and channeled, not eradicated. If you feel like snapping, ask what has frustrated you. Safety valves include exercise, hitting games, writing an angry letter and burning it, and above all working toward a goal. Furey adds internal martial arts that combine breathing and relaxation.

**Insecurity.** It arises from comparing yourself with an imagined absolute self and from thinking you "should" be good, successful and poised. A person is secure only while moving forward. Champions who defend a title do worse than challengers who fight for it, and Maltz tells a boxer to remember he is fighting for the championship, not defending it. Get "off your high horse."

**Loneliness.** Chronic loneliness is alienation from the real self and a self-protective way of avoiding hurt. The lonely wait for others to come to them. *Remedy:* force yourself to mix, develop a social skill, contribute actively, and exposure will reduce fear and bring acceptance.

**Uncertainty.** Indecision avoids being wrong, which the perfectionist cannot bear; the opposite type decides hastily and blames others. Neither works. Nobody is right all the time (Babe Ruth led in strikeouts as well as home runs). Consider the facts, pick a course and correct as you go. "Big men" admit mistakes, and quotations from Gladstone, Davy, Smiles and Edison make the point that errors teach.

**Resentment.** Resentment blames society or life, offers a false feeling of importance and tries to change the past (the word means "to feel again"). It becomes an emotional habit, leads to self-pity and a victim's self-image, and makes you dependent on others. Creative goal-striving, where you are the actor and nobody owes you anything, is incompatible with it.

**Emptiness.** Some people attain the outward symbols of success and lose the capacity to enjoy. Joy accompanies creative goal-striving; emptiness means you have no goal worth striving for or an inadequate self-image that cannot accept success, the "success syndrome" of feeling a phony. Real success, achieved for your own reasons, never hurts anyone.

**Using negatives well.** Like a driver who glances at the dashboard but watches the road, or a golfer who knows where the bunker is but aims at the green, glance at negatives, recognize them as undesirable, take corrective action and replace them with the positive opposite.

> **Key takeaway.** The failure traits are mistaken ways of coping that act as warning signals, so notice them, understand why they do not work, and steer back to a positive goal.

## Part Three: Releasing Your Real Self

### Chapter 10: How to Remove Emotional Scars, or How to Give Yourself an Emotional Face-Lift

Maltz begins with the body's habit of forming scar tissue and calluses as protection, and says we do the same emotionally: when hurt, we grow a protective shell. The example is George T., a promising young lawyer scarred in a car accident. He lost a case, believed his "evil" look repelled others, drank, turned down cases and became a recluse. Surgery restored his face, and his personality returned, but the real cure was removing the emotional scar and restoring his self-image as an acceptable member of society.

**How emotional scars alienate.** People who have never had a physical injury also form emotional scars: a woman burned once never trusts another man; a child with a cruel parent never trusts authority. The wall meant to guard against one person shuts out everyone, including the real self. Psychiatrist Bernard Holland observed that juvenile delinquents, boastful and hostile, are soft inside and attack first to avoid being rejected. Scarred people also hold a damaged self-image and see the world as hostile. In contrast, Arthur Combs's "self-fulfilled person" sees himself as liked, wanted and able, accepts himself, feels at one with others and has a rich store of knowledge.

**Three rules for immunizing yourself.**
1. **Be too big to feel threatened.** The easily offended have the lowest self-esteem, and slights pass unnoticed to someone with a healthy ego. We need an emotional epidermis, not a turtle's shell. The cure for egotism is a stronger ego, not self-abnegation.
2. **Be self-reliant and responsible.** The hard exterior hides a person who wants to be dependent (salesmen say the gruffest prospects are easy once past the defenses). Passive-dependent people feel life owes them love and appreciation and are constantly hurt. Self-reliant people give love and do not demand universal approval.
3. **Relax away emotional hurts.** A surgeon's incision heals without a scar because tension is removed, and the same is true emotionally. We take offense when we are tense and doubtful. Feeling hurt is our own response, and fear, anger and anxiety cannot coexist with complete muscle relaxation. A Massachusetts group-therapy program combining thought control and daily relaxation outperformed classical analysis in the study Maltz cites.

**Removing old scars: forgiveness as surgery.** Old scars must be cut out, not salved. Maltz describes a wife who "forgives" her husband but keeps reminding him that she cannot forget, and says forgiveness that is partial, remembered or used as a weapon does not work. Real forgiveness is complete and forgotten, like a cancelled note torn up and burned. The only difficulty is willingness, because we enjoy condemning and feeling sorry for ourselves. The deepest forgiveness recognizes there was nothing to forgive, because we should not have condemned in the first place. He notes that in the New Testament Jesus never "forgives" the adulterous woman but declines to condemn her.

**Forgive yourself.** Remorse and excessive guilt try to live in the past. Recognize errors so you can correct course, but do not hate yourself. Describe mistakes with verbs, not nouns: "I failed" is an error to learn from, while "I am a failure" fixes it. Wendell Johnson found that parents who label a child "a stutterer" help to create stuttering, and Knight Dunlap found that self-blame defeats the cure of bad habits. "You make mistakes. Mistakes don't make you."

**Don't be an oyster.** Creative living requires a degree of vulnerability: an oyster is safe, but it cannot go after what it wants or know the joys of emotional communication. A spiritual face-lift (relaxation, therapeutic forgiveness, a tough skin but no shell, creative living and "nostalgia for the future") also makes you look and feel younger, since grudges weigh like a heavy load.

> **Key takeaway.** Prevent emotional scars with healthy self-esteem, self-reliance and relaxation, and remove old ones by completely forgiving others and yourself, treating mistakes as things you did, not what you are.

### Chapter 11: How to Unlock Your Real Personality

Personality, says Maltz, is not something acquired from outside but released from within: the outward evidence of your unique creative self. Infants have "personality plus" because they are honest and uninhibited. Everyone has a personality; a "poor personality" is an inhibited one, with the real self locked in an inner prison. Symptoms include shyness, timidity, self-consciousness, hostility, guilt, insomnia, nervousness, irritability and trouble getting along with others, all flowing from the basic frustration of not being able to be oneself.

**Excessive negative feedback equals inhibition.** In cybernetics, negative feedback is criticism: it tells a system it is off course and must correct. Its job is to modify the response, not stop it. A system that is too sensitive overcorrects or halts. A woodsman walking toward a tall tree adjusts when he drifts 15 degrees, but does not conclude that walking is wrong. Many people hear "my way of expressing myself is off" as "self-expression is wrong."

**Stuttering as an example.** We monitor our voices through our ears, and this feedback helps speech (deaf people struggle to speak well). Stuttering arises when monitoring is conscious, anticipatory and oversensitive. E. Colin Cherry had severe stutterers read aloud with a loud tone drowning out their voices and found "remarkable" improvement. Others learned "shadow-talk," speaking along with another voice, and spoke normally. Remove excessive self-criticism and inhibition disappears.

**Excessive carefulness and anxiety.** "Purpose tremor," where your hand shakes when you try to thread a needle or pour into a narrow bottle, shows how being too careful hurts performance. Henry Ward Beecher disliked people who never speak for fear of speaking wrongly, and William James praised a measure of indifference. Prepare thoroughly, then "fling away all further care" and trust spontaneity; the night before an exam, play or sleep.

**Self-consciousness is really "others' consciousness."** Feedback from other people helps us perform socially, but only when it is subconscious and spontaneous. When we constantly monitor what others think, we choke off our creative self and make a poor impression. The way to make a good impression is never to try consciously. Salesman James Mangan cured his hotel-dining-room awkwardness by pretending he was going to eat with Ma and Pa, and Furey adds that any positive memory of poise will work for any situation. Albert Wiggam stopped fighting self-consciousness and acted as he did when alone, ignoring others' opinions, and became a public speaker without fear. This did not make him callous.

**Conscience.** It is a learned negative-feedback mechanism that works well if its data are correct, but it can be wrong and needs recalibrating; it should make you happy and productive, not miserable. Self-expression is not basically a moral matter, but a child who is shamed for speaking up, showing anger or showing love learns that expression is wrong. Stage fright, Maltz says, is excessive negative feedback from a misguided conscience, the fear of being punished for "showing off."

**Disinhibition.** The world needs some inhibition, but the inhibited need a long step in the opposite direction, as a doctor lowers a high fever. Signs of too little inhibition include rushing in, impulsive actions and being unable to admit error; most people show too much (shyness, nervousness, ill ease, taking a back seat). *Practice exercise:* do not rehearse what you will say, improvise; stop moment-by-moment self-criticism; speak louder than usual; and compliment at least three people daily and tell your spouse "I love you" twice a day.

> **Key takeaway.** Inhibition is oversensitive negative feedback, so correct it by acting and speaking spontaneously, dropping constant self-criticism and practicing disinhibition until you strike a balance.

### Chapter 12: Do-It-Yourself Tranquilizers That Bring Peace of Mind

Tranquilizer drugs work by an "umbrella action": they leave the environment unchanged but greatly reduce our *response* to disturbing stimuli. Maltz uses this to argue that peace of mind comes from over-response being toned down, and that you can do this yourself.

**Over-response is a habit.** When the phone rings you jump to answer out of habit, though you need not. Picture yourself sitting calmly, aware of the ring but unmoved, and note that not responding means relaxing from doing, not fighting. Furey says this matters even more now with email and texts. Pavlov's conditioned dog illustrates how we become conditioned to "bells": strangers, crowds, closed spaces or the boss may trigger fear long after the original reason has gone. To extinguish a conditioned response, practice relaxing instead of responding, using the cue: "The telephone is ringing, but I do not have to answer it."

**Delay if you cannot ignore.** When ignoring is impossible, delay. Mary S., fearful in crowds, told herself she could refuse to flee for just two minutes, and delayed responses break the automatic chain, as Scarlett O'Hara's "I'll think about that tomorrow" does. Counting to ten works if you actually delay the tension. Furey adds three deep breaths. Because you cannot feel anger, fear or anxiety while muscles are perfectly relaxed, relaxation is nature's own tranquilizer, and a disinhibitor too.

**A quiet room in your mind.** Citing Marcus Aurelius, Maltz recommends retreating into yourself, as President Truman said he did in a "foxhole in my mind." Build in imagination a simply furnished room in restful blues, greens and golds, with your favorite chair and a window on a silent beach, and visit it for a few minutes whenever tension rises, picturing yourself climbing the stairs and leaving your worries at the bottom. This is useful escapism, comparable to sleep or vacations. A symbolic image helps: remembering Old Faithful, he pictures steam from his head evaporating harmlessly.

**Clear the mechanism.** Like an adding machine, the mind must be cleared between tasks, or moods "carry over": the executive who brings office aggression home, insomnia from unresolved worries, harsh tones answering the phone (one company told executives to pause five seconds and smile), and car accidents that insurers link to emotional carry-over. But calmness carries over too. Maltz uses the quiet room before and after surgery.

**You are an actor, not a reactor.** Humans set their own goals and respond only when it serves them; if a response gets you off course, no response is the appropriate response. Lecky's point is to keep the same stable attitude despite environmental changes. Do not fight straw men, either: worry pictures are imagined, the nervous system reacts to them as real, so ignore them and attend to what is actually present.

**First aid kit and thermostat.** Remember that disturbance usually comes from over-response; delay habitual responses; relax; use the quiet room; and stop frightening yourself with mental pictures. Like the body's thermostat at 98.6 degrees, you have a spiritual thermostat that lets you keep your own emotional climate. *Practice:* picture yourself letting the phone ring in situations that have upset you (a ranting colleague, a pressured day) and say "I am letting the telephone ring."

> **Key takeaway.** Disturbance comes from your own over-response, so ignore or delay it, relax, retreat to a mental quiet room and clear out carry-over emotions between situations.

### Chapter 13: How to Turn a Crisis into a Creative Opportunity

Some golfers, pitchers, students and salespeople perform flawlessly in low-stakes settings and fall apart when pressure rises, while others, such as high jumper John Thomas (who jumped 7 feet 1¼ inches in competition after a best of 6 feet 9¼ in practice) or a clutch pinch hitter, do better. Maltz says the difference is not an inborn quality but how people have learned to react to a crisis, which he defines as a situation that can make or break you. He lists three keys: practice without pressure, adopt an aggressive rather than defensive attitude, and keep the situation in perspective.

**1. Practice without pressure.** We learn fast but badly in a crisis: a man thrown into deep water may swim somehow, but his crude stroke becomes fixed. Edward Tolman's rats that explored a maze while well fed performed better when hungry later than rats trained under hunger, whose "cognitive maps" were narrow, and Jerome Bruner's rats starved 36 hours needed more than 20 trials against six for those starved 12. In fire drills, people practicing a dry run learn flexible escape routes, while those who learn during an actual fire learn narrow, fixated responses. Maltz calls this "shadowboxing":
- Gentleman Jim Corbett threw his left jab at a mirror over 10,000 times, and Gene Tunney fought an imaginary Dempsey a hundred times.
- Harry Lauder practiced a routine 10,000 times, Billy Graham preached to cypress stumps, and Ben Hogan swung a club in his bedroom.
- A dowager rehearsed a "grand entrance" in an empty living room and then enjoyed a ball; Furey says he has taught the technique to many kinds of people.
- Dry-firing teaches marksmen to avoid "purpose tremor," a hunter carried an empty gun and later hit 15 of 17 quail, and a ten-year-old learned to hit by first watching pitches go by with the bat on his shoulder.
- A salesman practiced imaginary interviews and then made "bat-on-the-shoulder" calls with no intent to sell, which he said "worked like a miracle." Maltz also shadowboxed operations on cadavers.

Shadowboxing practices self-expression with no inhibition and builds a mental image of yourself acting correctly.

**2. Crisis brings power.** The word *crisis* comes from the Greek for "decisiveness," a fork in the road. Reliever Hugh Casey thought about what he would do and what he wanted to happen, not what the batter might do. Ordinary people show extraordinary strength in emergencies, such as a frail man who moved a piano out of his burning house. J. A. Hadfield says power comes when we fearlessly accept the challenge and confidently expend our strength. The attitude is "I can handle it," and the key is staying goal-oriented. Lecky argued there is only one basic emotion, excitement, and its expression (fear, anger, courage) depends on the goal you give it. So do not mistake excitement for fear: Jack Dempsey was too jittery to shave before fights but used it to hit harder, actors and horses perform better when "spirited," and an experienced speaker missed the tingle that signalled a good speech.

**3. What is the worst that can happen?** Many people treat ordinary challenges as life-or-death, creating "the jitters" from excessive, unused excitement. Bertrand Russell's method is to look at the worst possible outcome steadily until you can say it would not matter much; Carlyle recovered from despair by asking himself what he was afraid of and defying it. Most "crises" are opportunities in which you will be no worse off if you fail: a salesman either gets the order or stays where he was. One salesman doubled his income by changing his attitude to "everything to gain and nothing to lose," and Walter Pidgeon recovered from a flop by realizing he had nothing more to lose.

> **Key takeaway.** Rehearse without pressure, meet challenges with an aggressive goal-oriented attitude, treat excitement as extra fuel, and ask what is the worst that can really happen.

## Part Four: Living the Method

### Chapter 14: How to Get That Winning Feeling

Maltz begins from the claim that the Creative Mechanism is teleological: it works toward goals and end results. You supply the goal, and the automatic mechanism supplies the means, whether that means guiding muscles with more precision than conscious thought could manage or producing the ideas you need. To engage it, though, the goal must be pictured as a present possibility, so vividly that your brain and nervous system treat it as real and produce the feelings that would accompany success. Worry already works this way: we picture failure in detail, rehearse it, and dredge up old failures until we feel what failure would feel like. Because the nervous system cannot tell a vividly imagined experience from a real one, the same machinery can be pointed at success.

The practical secret is to call up the feeling of success. Maltz treats the winning feeling less as a cause than as a thermometer: it does not create the heat in the room, it tells you the mechanism is set for success. Trying to force spontaneity destroys it, so the method is simply to define the goal, picture it clearly, and capture the feeling you would have if it were already done. He gives athletes as evidence. Golfer Cary Middlecoff said he knew four days before the Masters that he would win and that the feeling gave him a line to the cup. Pitcher Don Larsen had a hunch before his perfect World Series game. J. C. Penney drew on his dying father's confidence in him to rebuild a lost fortune, and Henry Kaiser looked for people who said a hard job could be licked. Les Giblin, a salesman who feared big audiences, recalled the poise he felt with small groups, welded that feeling to a picture of himself addressing a large audience, and within three years was booking his own speaking career in much the way he had imagined it.

Maltz then gives the cybernetic and neurological explanation. A learning machine such as the "electronic mouse" bumps through a maze, remembers the turns that worked, and replays them. People do likewise: a successful action pattern is stored in memory as an engram, an electrical pattern across neurons, which Eccles and Sherrington described. Reviving a past success therefore revives the feeling that went with it, and recapturing the feeling brings back the action. He adds that success habits are built gradually. Harvard's Charles Eliot argued that schoolchildren need early work they can succeed at, and Pavlov's dying advice was "passion and gradualness." Athletes at a plateau drop back and practice easily rather than straining.

The method has concrete steps. First, replay a past success in as much sensory detail as you can, including sounds, setting, and bodily sensations, until the confidence returns. Second, "worry constructively": use the same ladder that makes worry grow, from "suppose" to "it could happen" to detailed pictures, but aim it at the best outcome, and do not force belief. Fear before an event is not an oracle. As General Patton said, never "take counsel of your fears," because they reflect your attitude, not external facts. Treat them as a challenge, as the gifted subjects in J. B. Rhine's card-guessing tests did. Feelings cannot be ordered by willpower, but they can be replaced: fill the mind with positive imagery and the negative feeling fades, and train a "bell" so that each worry becomes the cue for a pleasant image. Maltz used this himself as a medical student, imagining he was looking through a microscope when questioned orally.

The chapter closes with the choice that follows. Engrams change slightly each time they are replayed and strengthen with use, so the past is not fixed and unhappy memories are not destiny. That removes the comfort of blaming parents or circumstances, but it hands you the option of changing the record rather than fighting the music.

> **Key takeaway.** Define the goal, replay past successes in detail until you feel the winning feeling, and meet fear by substituting positive imagery rather than by willpower.

### Chapter 15: More Years of Life and More Life in Your Years

Maltz opens by admitting that medicine has no final answer on whether the Success Mechanism keeps people young or the Failure Mechanism ages them, so this closing chapter shares his "over-beliefs," a term he takes from William James. Such working assumptions go beyond the facts, but they are practical and even necessary, as Columbus needed to assume land lay to the west before he sailed. He offers his as a man, not as a physician. He believes the body is machine-like but that a human being is not a machine: the essence of a person is whatever animates and directs it.

His central claim is that a life force sustains healing and youth. He cites Hans Selye's stress research and the idea of "adaptation energy," a reserve distinct from the calories we eat that we spend adapting to every stressor, including illness and ageing. On this view the body cures itself, and drugs and surgery mostly stimulate or damper its defenses. A speaker who was burned out by travel renewed himself with a new goal, playing a famous golf course in every state, and found energy for his career again. Maltz also updates the 1960 material on the search for a fountain of youth, naming hormone treatments and supplements and quoting Dr. Dave Woynarowski, who links chronic stress to shorter telomeres and faster ageing, and points to education and wealth as correlates of longevity.

Maltz argues that emotional stress and frustration injure the body. Frustrated lab animals healed serious wounds worse, and the fastest-healing surgical patients he observed shared a mindset: they were optimistic, expected recovery, and had a compelling reason to get well. He reads placebo effects the same way. A placebo works because it creates an expectation and a goal-image of health that the Creative Mechanism pursues, so "suggestion" by itself explains nothing. The same logic can run in reverse. People who expect to be old at a certain age may set a negative goal image, and they also taper off physical and mental activity, which accelerates decline. He asserts that you could "create" an old man from a healthy thirty-year-old by convincing him he was finished.

The remedy is to keep needing more life. Beyond Dr. John Schindler's six basic needs (love, security, creative expression, recognition, new experiences, self-esteem), Maltz adds the need for more life, which comes from looking forward with anticipation. Creative people, from Michelangelo to Picasso, stay productive longest, which is why he advises patients to "develop a nostalgia for the future." Retirement kills not because of leaving a job but because of leaving life, and outdated medical beliefs about inevitable decline are disproved by findings on mental ability and exercise. He urges gradual reconditioning, following Dr. T. K. Cureton's program of walking first and building slowly over two years.

The last sections are openly faith-based. Maltz says he believes in "miracles" in the sense of natural healing accelerated by intense faith, and he sees no conflict between medicine and religion. He urges readers not to limit the channels through which life reaches them, whether science, religion, psychology, or other people, and he ends with the most adequate self-image: seeing yourself as made in the image of God.

> **Key takeaway.** Stay goal-oriented, active and forward-looking; creating a need for more life and refusing to expect decline protects both health and the spirit.

## Afterword: What You Can Expect with Psycho-Cybernetics

The updated edition ends with an afterword by the author who revised and expanded the book, Matt Furey, which is outside the fifteen numbered chapters. It speaks to the reader who has finished the book and now has to practice it, and it describes four signs to watch for when you use relaxed mental imagery daily. First, the calm you create before each session deepens and carries into the rest of the day, your ability to picture and feel positive outcomes strengthens, and you drift into a state of flow. The benefit comes from a daily "bath" in the principles, not from reading or occasional practice.

Second, the method does not ask for deadlines. A fixed end date can jam the mechanism if it makes you doubt you can meet it, so the author suggests imagining the goal, feeling good about it, and acting when the steps appear. Third, he recommends starting with short-term, emotionally charged goals, such as something you want within a day or a week, and building confidence before tackling large ones. Fourth, he says that other mental skills, such as intuition, may emerge with consistent practice, citing Maltz's interest in Rhine's parapsychology work and his own experiences. This is the most speculative claim in the book, and it rests on testimony rather than evidence.

The afterword closes with a personal account of forgiveness. In 1982 the author was badly injured by a glass pitcher in a bar fight and later buried the memory. Twenty-five years on, he could not visualize his goals until he re-entered the scene in imagination, felt the sense of helplessness underneath, and followed an inner voice he attributes to Maltz. He forgave himself and the attacker, and he changed the image of the pitcher into a feather that writes his ticket through life. The lesson he draws is that even forgiveness is a mental picture, and that anything you dislike about your life can be changed by changing the picture.

## Themes and Analysis

### Central themes

The first theme is that the self-image is the keystone of personality. Maltz argues in Chapter 1 that the picture you hold of yourself decides what you attempt, what you feel and how you perform, which is why positive thinking applied to a single situation fails if your overall concept of yourself stays negative. Chapters 4, 5, 8 and 11 apply the same idea to false beliefs, rational reevaluation, the traits of a "success-type" personality and the inhibitions that hide your real self.

The second is that the mind works like a goal-seeking machine. The Success Mechanism of Chapter 2 steers by feedback, so errors are information and not verdicts, and it needs a clear goal from you but not your constant supervision. This is the logic behind the advice to relax in Chapter 6, to treat excitement as fuel in Chapter 13, and to set goals as present possibilities in Chapter 14. The Failure Mechanism of Chapter 9 is the same system aimed at the wrong target.

The third is that imagination and feeling do the work that willpower cannot. Chapter 3's mental rehearsal, Chapter 7's habit of happiness, Chapter 12's "quiet room" and Chapter 14's winning feeling all rest on the claim that you change emotions indirectly, by changing the images you dwell on. The fourth theme is release: forgiveness, spontaneity and the refusal to carry old scars (Chapters 10 and 11) free the energy the self-image had been blocking.

### Strengths and limits

The strengths are a memorable central metaphor and practical exercises. The picture of a guidance system that corrects its course by feedback is easy to apply, the daily rehearsal, relaxation and substitution techniques are specific enough to try tonight, and the stories, from golfers to salesmen to patients, keep the principles concrete. The advice also anticipates modern ideas about reframing, visualization and deliberate practice, and its emphasis on gradualness and on small early successes (Chapter 14) is sound.

The limits are about evidence and tone. Much of the support is anecdote, the neurological and cybernetic claims reflect the science of the late 1950s, and the later chapters, especially Chapter 15 and the afterword, move into life-force, faith and extrasensory claims that are testimony and belief rather than demonstrated findings. Some case histories are presented as if the method alone produced the change.

*Where I'd push back:* the claim that positive imagery can replace effort, skill-building or external help risks being read as "just picture it," and the health claims in Chapter 15 should not be taken as medical advice. Maltz himself pairs imagery with action, so the safest reading treats rehearsal as preparation, not as a substitute.

### Applying it

1. **Write down your current self-image** (Chapters 1 and 4). Note the beliefs you accept about what you can and cannot do, and test them against evidence.
2. **Practice mental rehearsal daily** (Chapters 3 and 14). Spend a short, relaxed session picturing yourself acting successfully in detail, and replay a real past success to evoke the feeling.
3. **Relax before you act** (Chapters 6 and 12). Do your planning and worrying first, then give your attention to one task at a time and use a mental quiet room for tension.
4. **Release old hurts** (Chapters 10 and 11). Forgive yourself and others, and practice acting a little more spontaneously.
5. **Start small and be gradual** (Chapters 13 and 14). Build confidence with short-term goals and small wins before tackling large ones.

If you only read parts of it, read Chapters 1, 3, 6 and 14. Chapter 1 sets out the self-image, Chapter 3 shows how imagination reshapes it, Chapter 6 explains relaxation, and Chapter 14 gives the winning-feeling technique.

<summary>1. What is the self-image and why does it matter (Chapter 1)?</summary>

Your self-image is your mental blueprint of the sort of person you are. Your behavior, feelings and abilities stay consistent with it, so changing it changes what you can do.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why does positive thinking often fail (Chapter 1)?</summary>

People apply it to a particular situation while keeping a negative overall concept of themselves, so the old self-image overrides it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What is the Success Mechanism (Chapter 2)?</summary>

A built-in goal-seeking guidance system, comparable to a servo-mechanism, that steers by feedback toward a goal you supply and supplies the means automatically.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does imagination matter so much (Chapter 3)?</summary>

The nervous system cannot tell a vividly imagined experience from a real one, so detailed mental rehearsal builds new patterns, habits and self-confidence.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What does "dehypnotize yourself" mean (Chapter 4)?</summary>

Recognize that beliefs you accept as true control your behavior almost like hypnosis, then identify the limiting ones and replace them, helped by relaxation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. What can rational thinking do and not do (Chapter 5)?</summary>

It can examine and reject untrue beliefs, set goals and choose to forget past errors, but it cannot by itself produce results, which the automatic mechanism supplies.

</details>

<details>
<summary>7. Why should you relax while working toward a goal (Chapter 6)?</summary>

Strain and worry jam the Creative Mechanism, so you do your worrying before committing, then focus on the present, one task at a time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>8. How does Maltz define happiness as a habit (Chapter 7)?</summary>

A habit of pleasant thinking that you can practice now, by separating facts from opinions, keeping a goal and focusing on what is good.

</details>

<details>
<summary>9. What is the SUCCESS checklist (Chapter 8)?</summary>

Sense of direction, understanding, courage, compassion, esteem, self-confidence and self-acceptance, the traits of a success-type personality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>10. What is the Failure Mechanism (Chapter 9)?</summary>

The same goal-seeking system aimed at wrong or negative targets; its traits spell FAILURE and act as warning signals to steer back to a positive goal.

</details>

<details>
<summary>11. How does Maltz propose to remove emotional scars (Chapter 10)?</summary>

Forgive others and yourself completely, build self-esteem and self-reliance, and treat mistakes as things you did, not what you are.

</details>

<details>
<summary>12. How does Maltz explain inhibition (Chapter 11)?</summary>

As oversensitive negative feedback, which you correct by acting and speaking spontaneously and dropping constant self-criticism.

</details>

<details>
<summary>13. What is the "quiet room" (Chapter 12)?</summary>

A mental retreat you visit to relax and clear carry-over emotions, one of the do-it-yourself tranquilizers for over-response.

</details>

<details>
<summary>14. How should you treat excitement before a challenge (Chapter 13)?</summary>

As extra fuel, not fear, and with an aggressive goal-oriented attitude, asking what is the worst that can really happen.

</details>

<details>
<summary>15. What is the "winning feeling" (Chapter 14)?</summary>

The feeling of success and confidence that signals the mechanism is set for success, like a thermometer that measures the heat but does not cause it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>16. How do you build the winning feeling (Chapter 14)?</summary>

Replay a past success in sensory detail, "worry constructively" about the best outcome, and replace negative feelings with positive imagery.

</details>

<details>
<summary>17. What did General Patton say about fear (Chapter 14)?</summary>

That he never takes counsel of his fears: fear before an event does not predict failure and need not be obeyed.

</details>

<details>
<summary>18. What is "adaptation energy" (Chapter 15)?</summary>

Hans Selye's term for the life force the body spends adapting to stress, which Maltz links to healing, youth and resistance to disease.

</details>

<details>
<summary>19. Why does Maltz advise developing "a nostalgia for the future" (Chapter 15)?</summary>

Creating a need for more life by looking forward with anticipation keeps people vital and productive, while living in the past contributes to ageing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>20. What does the afterword say about goals and deadlines?</summary>

Deadlines can jam the mechanism if you doubt you can meet them, so imagine the goal, feel good about it and act when the steps come.

</details>

### Teach it back

Try to explain the book in five sentences, from memory:

1. Your self-image is a mental blueprint that governs your behavior and abilities.
2. Your brain works like a goal-seeking guidance system that steers by feedback and trusts the goal you give it.
3. Because the nervous system cannot tell vivid imagination from experience, daily mental rehearsal rebuilds the self-image.
4. Relaxation, forgiveness and spontaneity remove the strain and scars that jam the mechanism.
5. Replaying past successes and substituting positive imagery for fear give you the winning feeling, and a need for more life keeps you vital.
